A sattvic diet is not a menu. It is a question you ask after eating: do I feel clear, restless or heavy? The tradition sorts food by the effect it has on the mind, and the effect is something only you can observe. Ask the question honestly after every meal for a week and you will understand the idea better than any list could teach you. The lists help. The attention is the real practice.

## What is a sattvic diet?

Sattva is the quality of clarity and balance, one of the three gunas, the qualities that the tradition says run through all of nature. The other two are rajas, activity and restlessness, and tamas, inertia and heaviness. You can watch all three move through an ordinary day, which is where [the three gunas explained](/blog/three-gunas-explained) begins.

Food is read through the same lens. Some foods are said to leave the mind clear and the body light: these are sattvic. Some stimulate, heat up and agitate: rajasic. Some dull and weigh down: tamasic. The same meal can shift categories depending on how it is cooked, how fresh it is and how much of it you eat.

So a sattvic diet, in practice, means food that is fresh, lightly cooked and eaten in moderation. It is not only about what is on the plate. It is also about how much, how late and how you eat it.

## What does the Bhagavad Gita say about food?

The Bhagavad Gita on food is surprisingly specific. In the seventeenth chapter, Krishna describes three kinds of food according to the three gunas.

Foods dear to the sattvic, he says, are those that promote life, strength, health and cheerfulness. They are juicy, wholesome, substantial and pleasing to the heart.

Foods dear to the rajasic are too bitter, too sour, too salty, too hot, too pungent, too dry and burning, and they bring pain, grief and disease.

Foods dear to the tamasic are stale, tasteless, spoiled, left over, and impure.

What stands out is the tone. The Gita describes; it does not command. It says what kind of person is drawn to what kind of food, the way a thoughtful observer would. It leaves you to draw your own conclusions. That makes it a better lens than a rulebook.

It also notices extremes. Rajasic food is not a single ingredient but anything overdone: too spicy, too salty, too hot. Most of us know the feeling of a meal like that.

## A sattvic food list, and its limits

Here is a sattvic food list as many traditions understand it.

- Fresh vegetables and fruit
- Whole grains, such as rice, wheat and millets
- Lentils and dals
- Milk, curd and ghee
- Nuts and seeds
- Mild spices, such as cumin, coriander, ginger and turmeric

And here are the foods commonly left out in temple traditions: onion and garlic, meat, fish and eggs (though, outside the shrine, [not every Hindu is vegetarian](/blog/is-hinduism-vegetarian)), alcohol, and food that has been sitting around for a long time. Onion and garlic get the most questions, and [why onion and garlic are left out](/blog/why-no-onion-garlic) of pooja food covers both the reasoning and the folk tale.

Now the limits. Views differ between communities and families. Some keep strictly to this list; many do not. Sattvic, rajasic and tamasic food are traditional categories, not scientific ones, and this is tradition, not medical advice. If you have health conditions or dietary needs, follow the guidance of your doctor. The tradition itself was never meant to override the body's actual needs.

It also helps to notice what the list is really pointing at. Temple prasadam, like [sundal for Navratri and Fridays](/blog/sundal-for-navratri-and-fridays), is nearly always sattvic in this sense: simple, fresh, lightly spiced, made with attention. [Eating on a banana leaf](/blog/eating-on-a-banana-leaf) adds another layer: the setting of the meal as part of the meal. And [prasad and gratitude](/blog/prasad-and-gratitude) is a reminder that how you receive food matters as much as what it is.

## Earlier, fresher, smaller

For one week, eat dinner a little earlier, a little fresher and a little smaller. Not dramatically. Half an hour earlier, cooked that day, a few spoonfuls less.

Then pay attention to the mornings. Do you wake up clearer, heavier or the same? Keep a note in your head, or on paper if you prefer. At the end of the week, you will have your own evidence for what sattva means, in your own body.
