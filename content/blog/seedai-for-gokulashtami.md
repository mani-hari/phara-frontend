Seedai are tiny crunchy balls that Krishna, the butter thief, is said to love. They also have a reputation for bursting in hot oil. Both facts are why this seedai recipe asks for slow hands. Uppu seedai, the salted kind, and vella seedai, the jaggery kind, are the signature snacks of Gokulashtami in Tamil homes, offered at night with butter and aval. Most of the work is in preparing the flour properly, and once you understand why, the bursting stops being a mystery.

## Seedai recipe, salt and sweet

**For uppu seedai (salt)**

- 150 g fine rice flour (1 cup)
- 1 tablespoon urad dal flour, dry-roasted until fragrant
- 1 tablespoon soft butter
- 1 teaspoon white sesame
- 2 tablespoons finely grated coconut
- ½ teaspoon salt and a pinch of asafoetida
- Warm water, a spoon at a time
- Oil for frying

**For vella seedai (sweet)**

- The same rice and urad flour, butter, sesame and coconut, without salt and asafoetida
- 100 g grated jaggery (½ cup), melted in 60 ml water (¼ cup), strained and cooled slightly, used instead of water

**Method**

1. Dry-roast the rice flour on low heat for a few minutes until it is light and dry. It is ready when you can draw a line through it with a finger and the line holds. Sift it.
2. Mix the rice flour, urad flour, sesame, coconut, salt and asafoetida. Rub in the butter.
3. Add warm water (or the jaggery syrup) a little at a time and bring it together into a soft, smooth dough. It should not crack when pressed.
4. Pinch off marble-sized pieces and roll them very loosely between your palms. Do not press hard.
5. Spread the balls on a cloth and let them rest for fifteen to twenty minutes.
6. Heat the oil to medium-low. Fry a test batch of five or six first. If they hold, fry the rest in small batches, stirring gently, until golden and crisp.

## Why does seedai burst, and how do you stop it?

Seedai burst for two reasons: moisture and trapped air. When a ball holds a pocket of steam or air, the hot oil heats it faster than the dough can let it out, and it splits, sometimes loudly.

So every step above is about removing one or the other.

- **Roast and sift the flour.** Store-bought rice flour holds moisture, especially a bag that has sat through a humid American summer. Roasting dries it; sifting removes lumps that hide air.
- **Grate the coconut fine.** Large bits of coconut carry water.
- **Roll without pressing.** Squeezing forces air into the centre. A loose, gentle roll leaves a porous ball that breathes in the oil.
- **Prick each ball** with a toothpick if you are nervous.
- **Rest on a cloth.** The surface dries a little and seals.
- **Fry low.** High heat turns small amounts of moisture into sudden steam.

And stand back for the test batch. It tells you everything before the whole tray goes in.

## When are seedai offered?

Gokulashtami, Krishna's birth night, falls on the eighth day of the waning moon in the Tamil month of Avani, usually August or September. Seedai are offered at midnight or late evening, with butter, milk, fruit and [sweet aval for Krishna](/blog/aval-for-krishna-janmashtami). Many homes draw small footprints in rice flour from the front door to the shrine, as if the child had just walked in. The seedai is one part of the night; [Krishna Janmashtami at home](/blog/krishna-janmashtami-at-home) covers the whole evening.

Other seasons have their own signature offerings, from the [six tastes of Ugadi pachadi](/blog/ugadi-pachadi-six-tastes) to [ada pradhaman for Onam](/blog/ada-pradhaman-for-onam), and for an everyday morning, [temple-style ven pongal](/blog/ven-pongal-temple-style).

## Roll on the weekend, fry after work

Gokulashtami often lands on a school night. If the pooja is on a weeknight, split the work. Roll the balls on the weekend, let them dry covered on a cloth, and store them in an airtight box in the fridge. On the evening itself, fry a small batch after work and offer them warm.

Try giving your full attention to the rolling, even for ten minutes. Loose hands, no pressing, no hurry. It is the same quality the whole recipe asks of you.
