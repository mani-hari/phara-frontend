In one telling, Brahma finished making the world and found it silent. Nothing sang, nothing spoke; the rivers lay still. Then Saraswati appeared with her veena, played one note, and the rivers began to murmur, the birds to call, and people to speak. The story is about creation. The festival is about the first sound of spring. That is Vasant Panchami significance in a sentence: a day for the goddess of speech and learning, kept just as the land begins to wake.

## Vasant Panchami significance: spring and speech

Vasant Panchami falls on the fifth day of the bright half of Magha, usually late January or February. Vasant means spring, and panchami, the fifth lunar day. In North India the cold is starting to break, and the mustard fields turn yellow almost overnight. In Bengal and much of the North, the day is Saraswati Puja, when schools, homes and neighbourhood clubs set up images of the goddess. In Punjab it is Basant, and the sky fills with kites.

The two ideas belong together. Spring is the season when the world starts speaking again after winter. Saraswati is the goddess of speech. The festival does not need to explain the connection; the fields make it.

## Why wear yellow?

On Vasant Panchami, people wear yellow, cook yellow and decorate in yellow. The obvious reason is the mustard flower. But yellow is also the colour of ripening grain and of the sun gathering strength after winter. Sweets turn saffron-yellow too: kesari bhaat, sweet rice coloured with saffron or turmeric, boondi and halwa.

In older Sanskrit poetry, spring belongs to Kamadeva, the god of desire, whose closest companion is Vasanta, spring personified. Poets describe him arriving with mango blossom for arrows and the hum of bees for a bowstring. Some regions still remember Kama and his wife Rati on this day. It seems strange to put the god of desire next to the goddess of learning, until you notice that both are about longing: one for another person, one for understanding. Spring stirs both.

## Saraswati, books and first letters

On the day, families place books, pens, notebooks and musical instruments before an image of Saraswati and do not use them until the puja is over. Students ask her blessing before exams; artists before a performance. In parts of India, small children write their first letters on this day, tracing them in rice or on a slate, much as South Indian families do at [Vidyarambham on Vijayadashami](/blog/vidyarambham-first-letters).

Look at how Saraswati is shown. She wears white. She sits by water, on a lotus, with a white swan beside her. The swan, in the old poetry, can separate milk from water, and so stands for discernment: the ability to tell what matters from what does not. She holds a book, a rosary and a veena. Nothing in the image is about marks or ranks. It is all about attention, practice and judgement.

That is worth saying to a child before an exam. The goddess is not a score-raising service. What learning asks is attention, not only prayers for marks. [A Saraswati mantra for students](/blog/saraswati-mantra-for-students) works best as a way to settle the mind before study. South Indian families also honour knowledge through Hayagriva, whose festival falls on [Avani Avittam](/blog/avani-avittam-meaning); the pairing is explained in [Saraswati and Hayagriva](/blog/saraswati-and-hayagriva).

## A river before a goddess

Saraswati is a river before she is a goddess. In the Rig Veda she is praised as the best of rivers, the best of mothers, a great flowing body of water in the north-west. Only later does the river become the goddess of speech and knowledge, as though the old poets understood that knowledge, like water, has to keep moving.

Knowledge that stops moving stops being knowledge. It becomes a certificate. The winter festivals around this one, from [Arudra Darshan's cosmic dance](/blog/arudra-darshan-meaning) to the sun's turn at Ratha Saptami, are all about motion, and even [the household wedding of Tulsi Vivah](/blog/tulsi-vivah-meaning) marks a season turning; this one is about the motion of the mind.

So on Vasant Panchami, pick up the instrument you set down. The guitar in the cupboard, the language app you abandoned, the book with a bookmark still in chapter three. Play one note. That is how, in the story, the whole world began to speak.
