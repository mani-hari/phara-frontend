If your body cannot sit on the floor, stand for aarti, the waving of the lamp before the deity, or fast on Ekadashi, your practice has not failed. It needs a different shape, and the tradition already has several. Spiritual practice with chronic illness is not a lesser version of the real thing. In many ways it is closer to what the rituals were always pointing at: attention, offered by whatever body you have today.

## Spiritual practice with chronic illness: what bends

Almost everything in a daily puja can bend without breaking.

- **Sit on a chair before the shrine.** The floor is traditional, not required. I have written about [why we sit on the floor to pray](/blog/sitting-on-the-floor-to-pray), but a chair facing the lamp is still facing the lamp. Raise the shrine to a shelf at eye level if bending hurts.
- **Shorter japa, repetition of a mantra, on the bad days.** If you usually chant a full mala of a hundred and eight, do eleven. Or three. Or one, with full attention. Keep the thread unbroken; the length can change.
- **Light the lamp from bed.** Keep a small lamp or an electric diya within reach. On the days you cannot get up, switch it on, look at it for a minute, and let that be the puja.
- **Let someone else do the physical parts.** A partner, a child, a friend can light the lamp and ring the bell while you sit and watch. Watching is participation.

On the good days too, a short fixed form helps; [the five-minute daily puja](/blog/five-minute-daily-puja) fits easily around fatigue.

## What is manasa puja?

Manasa puja means worship done entirely in the mind. Nothing is lit, poured or placed. Everything is imagined, and offered.

The best-known text for it is the Shiva Manasa Puja, a hymn attributed to Adi Shankara. It begins by offering Shiva a jewelled seat, a bath in cool Himalayan water, divine garments, sandal paste, flowers, incense and a lamp, and then a meal of rice, sweets and fruit, all of them imagined. By the last verse, the poet goes further: my self is you, my body your temple, my sleep your meditation, every movement of mine a circumambulation, every word a hymn.

That last verse is the key for anyone living with a chronic illness. Nothing is missing. The worship is complete even if the body never leaves the bed. The tradition put this hymn in the canon precisely because it knew that not everyone, on every day, can stand at a shrine.

Try it like this. Close your eyes. Imagine the deity you love in front of you. Offer a seat. Offer water. Offer a flower, and see its colour. Offer a lamp. Offer a small sweet. Bow. It takes about a minute. Many people find that on the worst days it is the most vivid prayer they have.

## Do you have to fast when you are ill?

No. This is one of the clearest points in the tradition. Fasting when sick is not expected; the sick, the elderly, pregnant women, children and those on medication have always been exempted from strict fasts.

If you take medicine with food, if your blood sugar needs managing, if fatigue worsens without regular meals, follow your doctor. The fast was never meant to harm the body that keeps it; what it is actually for is the subject of [fasting and focus](/blog/fasting-and-focus).

If you want to mark a fast day anyway, let attention be the fast. Eat simply and slowly. Skip the screen at dinner. Give up a complaint for the day, or a habit of mind you want to loosen.

Some people also find a short peace chant settles the evenings, and [Om Shanti, said three times](/blog/om-shanti-shanti-shanti-meaning), is the simplest. And for those facing procedures, there is [a short prayer before surgery](/blog/prayer-before-surgery-hindu).

## One minute of manasa puja a day

Every morning for seven days, do one minute of manasa puja, in whatever position your body allows. Lying down is fine. Eyes closed, one deity, one seat, one flower, one lamp, one bow.

At the end of the week, notice two things: how easy it was to do on the bad days, and whether it changed the good ones. Your body is the first instrument of any practice. On some days it plays loudly and on some days very softly. Both are music.
