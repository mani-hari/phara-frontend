Why sit on the floor for puja when there is a perfectly good chair in the room? Because a low seat keeps the body still and brings your eyes level with the lamp. The tradition noticed this long ago and built it into worship. It also made room for bodies that cannot sit low: if your knees say no, a chair does the job too. The attention is what matters, and you can bring it from either seat.

## Why sit on the floor for puja?

In Sanskrit, the word asana means a seat as well as a posture. Before it meant a yoga pose, it meant the place you sit and the way you sit there. Much of the old instruction about worship is really instruction about the asana.

The tradition is quite specific. You sit on a mat, not on bare ground. The classic choices are darbha grass, a woollen blanket, or a folded cloth. Some explanations are about purity; some are about insulation, keeping the body from the cold or damp of the floor. Either way, you create a small, defined place that belongs to the prayer. Many families keep a dedicated asana mat for puja that is used for nothing else.

And the seat is low. That keeps the body still, since it takes effort to get up. It brings the gaze level with the shrine, because most home shrines sit low. And it places you in a posture of receiving rather than presiding. You are not at a desk. You are at the feet of something.

## What does the body notice on the floor?

Try sitting cross-legged for five minutes and pay attention to what happens.

Crossed legs in sukhasana, the easy pose, ground the hips. The weight settles down through the sitting bones into the floor. There is a feeling of being planted.

A stable base lets the breath drop lower. When you sit on a chair and slump, the breath tends to stay high in the chest. When the hips are grounded and the spine rises from them, the belly has room to move. Many people find the breath slows on its own. From here, a few rounds of [pranayama before pooja](/blog/pranayama-before-pooja) come easily.

There are fewer distractions. This one is subtle but real. When getting up takes effort, you are less likely to jump up to check the stove or answer a message. The body commits, and the mind often follows. The same is true of [using a japa mala](/blog/how-to-use-a-japa-mala), which is easier to settle into when you are not perched on the edge of something.

Sukhasana for prayer does not need to be perfect. Put a folded blanket under the hips so the knees can drop below them. Sit against a wall if the back tires. Comfort helps stillness.

## Can I do puja sitting on a chair?

Yes, and without apology.

Bad knees, stiff hips, pregnancy, age, recovery from an injury or surgery: any of these is reason enough. The tradition has always made room for the body as it is. Elders in temples sit on benches. People who are unwell pray from their beds. Nobody's prayer is second-class because of the furniture.

If you use a chair, a few things help. Sit upright, away from the backrest if you can, so the spine holds itself. Keep the feet flat on the floor, which gives you the grounding the crossed legs would have given. And if your shrine is on the floor, raise a small one to eye level, on a shelf or a side table, so you are still looking straight at the lamp rather than down at it.

What you are trying to create is the same in both seats: a body that is still, a spine that is upright, and eyes that rest on one thing. The difference between [meditation and puja](/blog/meditation-vs-puja) is smaller than people think, and both come down to where the attention goes. Cooking can be the same; [cooking as an offering](/blog/cooking-as-an-offering) looks at the kitchen as another kind of seat.

## Floor or chair: a week's test

On some days, sit on a folded blanket on the floor for five minutes of prayer. On other days, sit on a chair, feet flat, spine upright. Keep everything else the same: the lamp, the time, the words.

Notice which lets you be most still. For some people, it will be the floor. For some, the chair. For some, it will change with the day and the weather. That is useful to know. The seat serves the prayer, not the other way around.
