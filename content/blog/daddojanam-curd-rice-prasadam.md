Daddojanam is curd rice made carefully enough to offer. This daddojanam recipe is only rice, curd, a little milk and a tempering, but it is one of the classic prasadams, food offered to the deity and then shared, at Venkateswara temples and Perumal (Vishnu) temples across the south. Try making it once with full attention, from the rice to the last curry leaf, and notice how differently you eat something you have just offered.

## Daddojanam recipe: rice, curd, milk and a tempering

**Ingredients (serves three or four)**

- 150 g raw rice (¾ cup)
- 250 g fresh, mild curd (1 cup)
- 60 to 120 ml milk (¼ to ½ cup), boiled and cooled
- Salt to taste
- 1 tablespoon ghee or oil
- 1 teaspoon mustard seeds
- 1 teaspoon urad dal
- 1 green chilli, finely chopped
- 1 teaspoon grated ginger
- A sprig of curry leaves
- A pinch of asafoetida
- Pomegranate seeds or finely diced cucumber, optional

**Method**

1. Cook the rice with a little extra water until very soft.
2. Mash it lightly while warm, then let it cool until it is just warm to the touch, not hot.
3. Stir in the curd, then the milk, until the texture is loose and creamy. Add salt.
4. Heat the ghee, add mustard seeds, and when they splutter add urad dal. When the dal turns golden, add green chilli, ginger, curry leaves and asafoetida.
5. Pour the tempering over the rice and fold it through.
6. Scatter pomegranate or cucumber on top if you like.

## Why is curd rice offered at Vishnu temples?

At Tirumala, daddojanam is one of the traditional prasadams, alongside pulihora, the tamarind rice, and chakkera pongali, the sweet pongal. Many other Venkateswara and Perumal temples follow the same pattern. The temple has its own story, told in [the story of Tirupati Balaji](/blog/tirupati-balaji-temple-story).

Why curd rice? Tradition counts it among sattvic foods, simple and calm to prepare, and thinks of it as cooling and gentle. Whatever you make of those ideas, there is something honest about offering the plainest food on the table. It is what most South Indian meals end with, and here it is placed at the beginning, before the deity.

Notice what that does. The ordinary dish, the one you might eat standing at the counter, becomes the one you slow down for.

## How do you keep it from souring before the pooja?

Curd keeps working after you mix it, and by the time the pooja is over a carefully made daddojanam can taste sharp. A few habits help:

- **The milk trick.** The splash of milk slows the souring and keeps the rice creamy. Add a little more if it will wait an hour.
- **Mix close to offering time.** Cook and cool the rice earlier, keep the curd in the fridge, and combine them twenty minutes before the pooja.
- **Keep it covered** and out of the sun.
- **Separate the offering first.** Before you check the salt, spoon a portion into a small bowl for the lamp and cover it. Taste only from the family pot, never from the offering.

That last habit is worth keeping for every dish. It turns the offering into something you set apart with intent rather than whatever is left over.

## A week of weekday curd rice

For one week, make your ordinary curd rice with the same care you would give an offering. Soft rice, fresh curd, a proper tempering, even on a Tuesday. Before you eat, put a spoonful aside. Then eat the first few spoons slowly, without your phone.
