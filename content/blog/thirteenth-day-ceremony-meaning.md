For twelve days after a death, a Hindu household lives in suspension. No festivals, no temple visits, simple food, the lamp kept burning, visitors who come and sit. On the thirteenth day it is allowed to return to the world. If you have been asked to attend or plan the 13th day ceremony after death in a Hindu family, the first thing to understand is that it marks the end of mourning, not the end of love.

## The Garuda Purana's journey of twelve days

The Garuda Purana, the text most often read in homes after a death, tells a story about those days. The one who has died becomes a preta, a newly departed spirit, without a body and without a place. Over the first ten days the family offers a pinda, a ball of rice, each day, and each pinda, the text says, builds part of a new subtle body: the head on the first day, the neck and shoulders on the second, and so on, until by the tenth the body is complete, and hungry. Then the long journey begins towards the world of the ancestors.

The story says the dead are travelling. The story is really about the living needing a measured time to grieve. Ten days of making something with your hands, one offering at a time, gives shape to days that otherwise have none. Grief is given a task and a timetable. That is not coldness. It is kindness, from people who had seen a great deal of grief. There is a separate piece on [grief in Hinduism](/blog/grief-in-hinduism) that looks at how the body carries these days.

## What is the 13th day ceremony?

The order varies by community, but a common sequence runs like this. On the eleventh or twelfth day comes sapindikaranam, the rite in which the one who died is joined with three generations of ancestors: a parent, a grandparent and a great-grandparent. Four pindas are made; the new one is divided and merged into the three older ones. After this, the person is no longer a wandering preta but a pitr, an ancestor, remembered along with the others.

The thirteenth day then celebrates that transition. In many Tamil Brahmin homes it is called subha sweekaram, receiving the auspicious, and other Tamil communities have their own names and customs; in much of North India it is terahvi, from the word for thirteen. Some communities complete these rites on the eleventh day, some on the sixteenth. If your family's customs differ from your neighbour's, neither of you is wrong. Ask the elders, and ask [a priest who knows your tradition](/blog/finding-a-hindu-priest-usa).

For families abroad, the rites can be done at a temple or at home with a priest, and relatives in India can join by video. The practical side of the first days, in a funeral home far from India, is covered in [a Hindu funeral in the USA](/blog/hindu-funeral-in-the-usa), and [when a parent dies abroad](/blog/when-a-parent-dies-abroad) is written for families caught between two countries.

## Why do the doors open again?

On the thirteenth day there is a feast, often the first full meal with sweets since the death. The family wears new clothes. The house has been cleaned from floor to ceiling. There is a first visit to a temple, and the evening lamp is lit, not as a vigil now, but as the ordinary lamp of an ordinary house. In some homes, gifts are given to the priest and to those who helped.

It can feel abrupt. Yesterday we were mourning; today there is payasam. But the tradition is saying something careful here. Grief without an end point can swallow a household, and the elderly and the very young are the first to suffer from it. The thirteenth day says: you may come back now. Nobody will think less of you for laughing at dinner.

## A door with a date on it

The tradition gave grief a door with a date on it, and then it gave permission to come back through that door. Every month in the first year, and every year afterwards on the tithi of death with [the annual shraddham](/blog/annual-shraddham-meaning), the family returns to remember. The thirteenth day is not the day you stop missing someone. It is the day the household is allowed to carry that missing into ordinary life, which is where, in the end, all of us have to carry it.
