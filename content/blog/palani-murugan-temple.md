The Murugan of Palani holds only a staff and wears only a loincloth. On a video call from Chennai, my mother once called him "the boy who walked out", and she meant it as praise. Palani Murugan temple history begins with that walk: a young god who lost a contest, left his parents' home on Kailash, and chose to stand alone on a hill. Tamil devotees have climbed that hill ever since, not to comfort him, but to learn from him.

## What is the story behind the Palani Murugan temple?

It is the story of a fruit, made famous by the Tamil tellings of Murugan more than by the Skanda Purana. Narada brought the jnana pazham, the fruit of wisdom, to Kailash, and Shiva and Parvati promised it to whichever son went around the world first; Murugan flew off on his peacock, and Ganesha simply walked around his parents and won. [The mango story of Ganesha and Kartikeya](/blog/ganesha-kartikeya-mango-story) tells it in full, from the elder brother's side, and the brother who won is worshipped as the giver of knowledge itself at [the Brahma Vidya Ganapati temple](/blog/brahma-vidya-ganapati-temple).

Murugan came back, saw what had happened, and left. He came south and stood on the hill. Shiva and Parvati followed him and said, "Pazham nee", you yourself are the fruit. Pazham nee became Palani, and the hill kept its name. I like that the parents went after him. Nobody in the story says he was wrong to be hurt.

## Dandayudhapani: the god who renounced

At Palani Murugan is Dandayudhapani, the one who holds a staff as his only weapon. His head is shaven, his body bare. He is not the general with the spear or the bridegroom of Thiruparankundram; he is the renouncer, and he is the most loved of the [six abodes of Murugan](/blog/six-abodes-of-murugan) for many Tamil families, including mine.

Why would people love a god who walked out? I think it is because most of us have walked out of something: a job, a city, a house full of relatives who meant well. My own leaving was a flight from Chennai to Newark. Dandayudhapani gives dignity to that moment. Leaving is not always defeat. Sometimes it is how you find out who you are without the fruit.

Then there is Idumban, who carried two hills south on a pole across his shoulders until, at Palani, the load would not budge because Murugan was standing on one of them. He became Murugan's devotee, and his pole became the kavadi, the subject of [the meaning of Thaipusam and the kavadi](/blog/thaipusam-kavadi-meaning).

## The navapashanam deity and the panchamritham

Tradition says the siddha Bhogar fashioned the image of Dandayudhapani from navapashanam, nine herbal and mineral substances bound together by a knowledge that has not been fully explained. Devotees hold this as a sacred tradition of the siddhas rather than something to test, and the temple guards the image with great care.

The abhishekam, the ritual bathing of the deity, uses panchamritham, a thick sweet mixture of hill banana, jaggery, ghee, honey and cardamom. After it is offered, the panchamritham is shared as prasadam, blessed food. If you want to make a home version for your own shrine, [panchamrutham at home](/blog/panchamrutham-at-home) walks through it.

But ask anyone who has been, and they will not talk first about the image or the prasadam. They talk about the climb. Hundreds of steps, bare feet, "Haro Hara" on everyone's lips, an old woman ahead of you who does not stop once. And then the first sight of the renouncer at the top, very small, very still, after all that effort.

## Joining from far away

My father cannot climb those steps any more, and I am on the wrong side of the world. What we do instead is ask for an abhishekam in the family's name and wait for the jar of panchamritham to arrive. If you are unsure how the timing works across continents, [joining a puja across time zones](/blog/joining-a-puja-across-time-zones) is a practical place to start.

One small thing for this week: when a jar of panchamritham reaches you, open it at the dinner table. Share a spoonful with whoever is there, and say his name once. Dandayudhapani. The boy who walked out, and was followed.
