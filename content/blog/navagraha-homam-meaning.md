The nine grahas are not nine gods to be feared. In the Navagraha homam they are nine forces invited to one fire, one after another, as guests. Each is named, each is given its own wood and grain, each is fed. Navagraha homam benefits, as the tradition describes them, come from exactly that orderliness: nobody is left out, nobody is flattered at the expense of another. It is, if you like, a very well-run dinner party.

## What happens in a Navagraha homam?

The Sanskrit word graha is often translated as planet, but it means something closer to "that which seizes" or holds. The nine are Surya the sun, Chandra the moon, Angaraka or Mars, Budha or Mercury, Guru or Jupiter, Shukra or Venus, Shani or Saturn, and the two shadow points Rahu and Ketu. In Jyotish, the Hindu astrological tradition, they are seen as the forces that shape the texture of a life.

The homam itself follows a pattern familiar from other fire rituals. If fire rituals are new to you, begin with [what a homam is](/blog/what-is-a-homam).

1. **Sankalpam.** The priest states the intention, with the devotee's name, birth star and gotra, the family lineage.
2. **Ganapati first.** As in almost every ritual, Ganesha is worshipped before anything else, so that the rite begins without obstruction.
3. **The nine seats.** Nine kalasams, pots of water that hold the presence of each deity, are arranged, or a mandala of nine squares is drawn, each square with its own colour and its graha placed in its traditional direction. Surya sits at the centre.
4. **The offerings.** Each graha is invoked with its own mantra, and offerings of ghee, grain and wood go into the fire in turn.
5. **Purnahuti.** The final full offering closes the rite, and the kalasam water is often sprinkled on the family.

## Nine woods, nine grains

This is where the homam becomes a little poem. Each graha is traditionally paired with a samidh, the small sticks of sacred wood fed to the fire:

- Surya: arka
- Chandra: palasha
- Angaraka: khadira
- Budha: apamarga
- Guru: ashvattha, the peepul
- Shukra: udumbara, the cluster fig
- Shani: shami
- Rahu: durva grass
- Ketu: kusha grass

Alongside them comes navadhanyam, the nine grains, each also linked to one graha: wheat for the sun, rice for the moon, toor dal for Mars, green gram for Mercury, chickpea for Jupiter, field beans for Venus, sesame for Saturn, black gram for Rahu, horse gram for Ketu. The details vary by tradition and by priest, and nobody should be anxious if their family pairs them differently.

The lists look like ritual bookkeeping. They are really about giving each force its due. Saturn does not get the peepul meant for Jupiter. Rahu, the shadow that swallows the sun in eclipse stories, receives the humblest grass of all, and it still counts as an offering. Nothing in a life, the homam seems to say, is too difficult to be fed.

## What are the Navagraha homam benefits, traditionally?

Families traditionally arrange a Navagraha homam at three kinds of moments. The first is a new beginning, a marriage, a move, a new house. The second is a demanding stretch in the chart: a difficult dasha, the long planetary periods described in [how dasha periods work](/blog/dasha-periods-explained), or a transit such as Shani's. The third is no special reason at all, simply once a year, the way one might visit an elder.

The tradition speaks of balance and steadiness. It does not promise that a promotion will arrive or that an illness will lift, and nobody sensible should read it that way. Families weighing when to book it can read [understanding the Navagraha homam](/blog/understanding-navagraha-homam); the idea is the same. The homam is less a lever on fate than a gesture of courtesy towards everything that moves you.

If you are unsure whether the Navagraha homam is the right one at all, there is a separate piece on [how to choose a homam](/blog/how-to-choose-a-homam) by need.

## Taking part from far away

If you live abroad, the homam can be performed at a temple in India in your name. You give your name, birth star and gotra for the sankalpam; the priests do the rest.

At home, at the hour of the homam, sit before a lamp. Name each graha once, slowly, in order, from Surya to Ketu. Nine names, perhaps a minute. It is the smallest possible version of the same dinner party: every guest greeted, none forgotten.
