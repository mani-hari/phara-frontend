When someone falls ill, a relative will sometimes say it must be karma, and the word lands like a verdict. Is illness karma? The tradition's own answer is far gentler than that sentence. It says that things have causes, some of them old and many of them ordinary, and it never tells you to blame the person lying in the hospital bed. To see why, it helps to look at what the texts actually say, and at one saint who was very ill for a long time.

## Is illness karma, according to the texts?

Karma, in the simplest sense, means action and its consequences; [karma explained simply](/blog/karma-explained-simply) sets out the basics. The tradition divides it into kinds. One of them is prarabdha, the portion of past action that is already in motion in this life, like an arrow that has left the bow and cannot be called back.

Some texts do speak of illness as one of the ways prarabdha ripens. But they rarely speak of it alone. The Charaka Samhita, the great classical text of Ayurveda, lists many causes of disease: food, season, age, injury, the strain of an unsuitable life, the imbalance of the body's humours. Karma appears among them. It does not replace them. A fever that follows a monsoon, a joint that wears with age, an injury from a fall are treated in these texts for what they are, with medicine, diet and rest.

What the stories never do is reduce a body to a moral scorecard. The idea that a sick child is being punished, or that a good person should not get sick, is not found in the tradition's wisest voices. It is found mainly in anxious relatives.

## The saint who did not ask why

Ramana Maharshi, the sage of Tiruvannamalai, spent the last years of his life with a painful cancer in his arm. He was operated on more than once. Devotees who had come to him for peace watched him suffer, and many of them wept.

He did not, the accounts say, ask why. He did not treat the illness as a mystery to be solved or a debt to be paid. When people grieved, he is remembered as saying, in effect, that the body was the thing that was ill; they were taking the body for him. And when they begged him not to leave, he answered that he was not going anywhere: where could he go?

The story says a saint was calm in pain. The story is really about where to stand while the body suffers. Ramana did not deny the illness, refuse treatment or pretend it did not hurt. He accepted surgery and medicine. What he refused was the extra suffering of blame, the story that the pain meant something had gone wrong with him as a person.

That is a hard place to stand, and most of us will not stand there. But it shows the direction.

## Karma as a way forward, not a verdict

Here is the part of karma that is usually forgotten when relatives use the word. Karma does not only look back. It looks forward. Kriyamana karma, action being done now, also shapes what comes. That is the whole point of the idea: that what you do matters.

So seeking treatment is karma. Resting is karma. Accepting care from others is karma. Taking the medicine on time, going to the follow-up appointment, eating what the doctor suggests: all of it is action, and all of it counts. The Ayurvedic texts treat illness with medicine and regimen, not with blame, and in that they are entirely in line with the rest of the tradition.

For the family, the forward-looking karma is care. Caregivers carry much of this, and [spiritual strength for caregivers](/blog/caregiver-spiritual-strength) speaks to them directly. And for the person facing an operation, there is [a short prayer before surgery](/blog/prayer-before-surgery-hindu).

Those who believe in rebirth sometimes find comfort in the longer view; [reincarnation in Hinduism](/blog/reincarnation-in-hinduism) explains what that view holds. Those who do not may prefer to stay with the plain present.

## Causes, not blame

Karma explains that things have causes. That is all it explains. It never tells you to blame the person in the hospital bed, and it certainly never tells you to blame yourself.

If a prayer helps while you sit with illness, the [Mahamrityunjaya mantra](/blog/mahamrityunjaya-mantra-meaning) has been said at bedsides for a very long time. It asks for release from death, gently, like a ripe fruit from the vine. It asks nothing about who deserved what.
