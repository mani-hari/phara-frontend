During my father's knee recovery, it slowly became clear that the patient was not the only one in the house who needed patience. I kept thinking of how the Thirukkural on patience begins, with the ground under our feet. The earth, it says, holds up even the people who dig into it; in the same way, the best of us bear with those who wound us.

## Poraiyudaimai (Forbearance): what the chapter asks

In the Tirukkural, the Tamil book of ethics in short couplets, the chapter is called Poraiyudaimai, possessing forbearance. It sits in the first book, on virtue, and it opens with one of Valluvar's most physical images. Kural 151:

*Agazhvaaraith thaangum nilampola thammai*\
*igazhvaarp poruththal thalai.*

Like the earth that bears those who dig it, to bear with those who insult us is the highest virtue.

I like that the earth is not described as passive. It is being cut into, and it still holds the digger up. There is weight in that line, and work.

The very next couplet goes further. Kural 152 says: forgive always the excesses of others; and better than forgiving, forget them. Valluvar ranks the two. Forgiveness is good; forgetting is better, because forgiveness can still keep a ledger, and forgetting finally closes it. Kural 153 goes on to call bearing with the foolish the strength of strengths, and later couplets praise those who answer harsh words from the arrogant with restraint.

## Thirukkural on patience in a recovery room

My father's knee operation went well, as these things go. The recovery did not feel like it went well. For weeks he was in pain, frustrated, and embarrassed to need help getting to the bathroom. We spoke on video most evenings, his mornings, during or just after his physiotherapy.

I saw things on those calls that I think he did not want me to see. He snapped at my mother over a glass of water set down on the wrong side. He snapped at the physiotherapist. He was short with me when the connection kept freezing, and I was short right back, which I regret. At one point my mother simply turned the phone away from him, looked at me, and smiled, and I understood that she had been doing this, bearing his bad days, for weeks.

That is forbearance in the Kural's sense. Not the sick man's patience, though he needed that too, but the quiet work of the people around him. The earth under the digger. My brother, who kept dropping off food on his way home, the neighbour who sat with him so my mother could go to the temple for an hour. None of them was keeping score. It reminds me of what the tradition means by [annadhanam, feeding people as worship](/blog/annadhanam-feeding-as-worship): care handed over without an invoice.

## Is forbearance the same as putting up with anything?

No, and it matters to say so clearly. Valluvar's forbearance is strength, not surrender. The earth in kural 151 is the most solid thing in the picture. Bearing an insult is a choice made by someone steady enough to make it; it is not the same as being unable to leave.

Forbearance does not require staying in harm. Nothing in the chapter asks anyone to remain where they are being hurt, and nobody should read it that way. What it asks is subtler: that we do not let another person's worst moment decide our own behaviour. The chapter even suggests that the forbearing protect themselves, because a grudge nursed for years is a wound we keep reopening. The Hindu idea of [rebirth and the long journey of the self](/blog/reincarnation-in-hinduism) is often told as a story of carrying things forward; forgetting an insult is one way of putting down luggage you do not need to carry into the next room, let alone the next life.

Two neighbouring chapters help here. The [Tirukkural on love](/blog/tirukkural-on-love) says a body without love is only bone wrapped in skin, which may be why patience with the people we love is the hardest kind. And the [Tirukkural on hospitality](/blog/tirukkural-on-hospitality) is, in its way, forbearance with the doorbell.

## Wait a day before you answer

When someone's words sting this week, a family member, a colleague, a stranger online, delay your reply by one full day. Do not rehearse it. Just wait. The next day, look at the sting again and see how much of it is left. Often it is far less than you expected, and what remains is easier to answer kindly. My father, I should add, apologised to my mother in the end. She told him she had already forgotten. I believe her.
