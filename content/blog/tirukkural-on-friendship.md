The best-known line in the Thirukkural on friendship says a true friend is like the hand that rushes to catch a garment as it slips, before anyone has noticed. No thought, no decision, just the hand. I first saw that happen in Thatha's cloth shop in Kumbakonam, to a veshti sliding off a customer's waist at the counter. Years later I watched it happen to a man.

## Natpu (Friendship): what Valluvar asks of a friend

The Tirukkural gives friendship not one chapter but a small cluster of them, in its second book, Porul, which deals with public life, work and the running of a household in the world. There is Natpu, friendship itself; then a chapter on testing friends; then chapters on long-standing friendship, bad friendship and false friendship. Valluvar clearly thought this was a subject where people get hurt.

The couplet everyone quotes is kural 788:

*Udukkai izhandhavan kaipola aange*\
*idukkan kalaivadhaam natpu.*

Friendship is removing distress on the spot, the way the hand of a man whose garment slips flies to catch it.

The key word is *aange*, right there, at once. The hand does not wait to be asked. It does not first check whether anyone is watching or whether the cloth deserves saving. And it acts before embarrassment spreads, which is the whole kindness of it. A friend steadies you before the room has noticed you wobble.

## Thirukkural on friendship at the shop counter

Thatha's shop sold cotton saris and veshtis, and in my memory it was always either very busy or completely still. One of the stillest seasons was a year when the rains failed and nobody in the villages around Kumbakonam had money for new clothes.

Thatha's oldest friend was a weaver who supplied him. That season the weaver came in one afternoon, stood at the counter, and could not bring himself to say what he needed. I was a boy, sitting on a stack of folded dhotis, and I remember the silence more than anything. Thatha did not let it go on. He opened the cash box under the Lakshmi and Ganesha picture, counted out money for yarn, wrote one line in his ledger, and told the weaver to bring the next lot of cloth whenever he could. An advance, given without a word.

I did not understand it then. I think I do now. That was the hand catching the cloth. Thatha did not calculate; friendship, for him, was a reflex, not a transaction. It is what the old word dharma looks like behind a shop counter, and it sits comfortably beside the idea of [dharma as a way of living ordinary days](/blog/dharma-meaning-in-everyday-life). Money moved that afternoon, but it was never really about money. The goddess of wealth, people say, does not stay long in one house, which is [why Lakshmi is called the restless goddess](/blog/lakshmi-restless-goddess). A friendship like theirs stayed put.

## How do you test a friend, according to the Kural?

Valluvar is warm about friendship, but he is not naive. The chapter after Natpu is about examining a friend before you commit. Kural 791 says there is no greater harm than befriending someone without examination, because once the friendship is made, the good-hearted cannot leave it. In other words, choose slowly, because you will stay loyal quickly.

And how do you examine? Kural 796 gives an answer I find almost comforting:

*Kettinum undor urudhi kilaignarai*\
*neetti alappadhor kol.*

Even misfortune has one benefit: it is a measuring rod that shows you who your true kin are.

Most of us have learned this the hard way, usually from the people who turned up. The cousin in Chennai who sat with my mother through my father's surgery, the colleague who covered for me without being asked, the college roommate who called every week one bad winter. Hard seasons sort people. That is patience of a kind too; the [Tirukkural on patience](/blog/tirukkural-on-patience) talks about bearing things the way the earth does, and old friends bear a great deal of us. Knowing who stays is its own kind of learning, the sort the [Tirukkural on learning](/blog/tirukkural-on-learning) says has no bottom.

## Call the friend who caught the cloth

Think of one friend who once caught your slipping cloth before you could be embarrassed. Call them this week, without a reason, and tell them you remember. Thatha's weaver and he never spoke of that afternoon again, but every Deepavali, until the end, a bundle of the finest cloth arrived at the shop with no bill attached.
