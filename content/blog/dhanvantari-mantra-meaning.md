The Dhanvantari mantra is a prayer to the physician of the gods, the form of Vishnu who carries the pot of amrita, the nectar of immortality. Used well, it is a practice of the body as much as the voice: slow breath, even chanting, a few minutes a day, alongside the doctor's advice and never instead of it. Here is what the words mean and how to begin.

## Who is Dhanvantari?

When the gods and demons churned the ocean of milk, a long series of treasures rose from the water. Last of all came a figure holding a pot. That was Dhanvantari, and the pot held amrita. The tradition honours him as a form of Vishnu and as the source of Ayurveda, the old Indian system of medicine, and many Ayurvedic physicians still begin their work with his name.

Notice where he appears in that story. Not at the start. The poison rises first, then the wish-granting cow, the horse, the elephant, Lakshmi herself. The healer comes at the very end, after everything else. Healing, in the story, is the last thing to arrive and the thing everyone was waiting for. [The churning of the ocean](/blog/samudra-manthan-meaning) tells the whole sequence, and [the Dhanvantari homam and the body as an instrument](/blog/dhanvantari-and-healing) covers the ritual side of the same figure. This piece stays with the mantra.

## The Dhanvantari mantra, word by word

The most commonly chanted form runs like this:

Om Namo Bhagavate Vasudevaya Dhanvantaraye Amrita Kalasha Hastaya Sarvamaya Vinashanaya Trailokya Nathaya Sri Maha Vishnave Namaha.

Here is what the Dhanvantari mantra means, piece by piece.

- Om Namo Bhagavate Vasudevaya: salutations to the Lord Vasudeva, a name of Vishnu.
- Dhanvantaraye: to Dhanvantari.
- Amrita Kalasha Hastaya: who holds in his hand the pot of amrita.
- Sarvamaya Vinashanaya: who removes all illness. Amaya is an old word for disease.
- Trailokya Nathaya: lord of the three worlds.
- Sri Maha Vishnave Namaha: salutations to the great Vishnu.

So the verse is, in plain English, a greeting to the healer who holds the nectar, asking him to remove illness. Longer and shorter versions exist in different families. Use the one you were taught, or this one.

## How do you chant it when you or someone you love is unwell?

This is where the body comes in. Sit comfortably, on the floor or on a chair with both feet down. If you are the one who is unwell, sit propped up in bed; nothing about the practice requires you to be strong.

Take a few breaths first and let the exhale grow longer than the inhale. Many people find that a longer exhale helps the body settle. Then begin chanting at an even pace, not rushing. Eleven or twenty-one repetitions is a common count, or a full round of 108 on a mala if you have the time and energy. When you finish, do not jump up. Sit for a minute in silence and notice your breath.

If you are chanting for someone else, it is traditional to say their name and, if you know it, their birth star before you begin, as a kind of dedication. You can do this from the other side of the world. People do.

The tradition places this prayer alongside medicine, not against it. Dhanvantari is, after all, the god of doctors. Keep every appointment. Take what is prescribed. Ask your questions. The mantra is for the part of the day that medicine does not reach: the waiting, the worry, the hour at night when there is nothing practical left to do. If the person you are chanting for is a parent, [praying for a sick parent](/blog/praying-for-a-sick-parent) and [finding strength as a caregiver](/blog/caregiver-spiritual-strength) speak to that particular kind of waiting.

## A week of morning and evening chanting

Morning and evening, a few minutes each: breath first, then the mantra, then a minute of silence. Do not measure the practice by how the illness responds; that is for your doctor to follow. Measure it by what happens in you. Many people find the practice steadies the one who chants, whatever else the week brings. That steadiness is worth having in the room.
