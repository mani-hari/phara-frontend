A poor Brahmin walks all the way to Dwaraka to see his childhood friend, now a king. Tied in a corner of his cloth is a handful of beaten rice, the only gift his wife could scrape together, and when he sees the palace he is too ashamed to offer it. Krishna finds the bundle, unties it, and eats with joy. Akshaya Tritiya significance, in many tellings, begins with that story: a gift so small it was nearly hidden, on a day named for what never runs out.

## Akshaya Tritiya significance: what 'akshaya' means

Akshaya Tritiya falls on the third day, *tritiya*, of the bright fortnight of Vaishakha, usually in April or May. *Akshaya* means that which does not diminish, the inexhaustible. The belief attached to the day is simple: whatever you begin, give or do today keeps growing. That is why it is a favourite day for starting ventures, laying foundations, beginning a course of study, or making a gift.

It is also one of the rare days that many families treat as auspicious in its entirety, without needing an astrologer to find the right hour. The whole day is good. For the date in any given year, check the current year's panchangam or a [Hindu festival calendar for the USA](/blog/hindu-festival-calendar-usa). It sits in a bright stretch of the year, a few weeks after [Ram Navami's noon puja](/blog/ram-navami-puja-at-home) and close to [Chitra Pournami, the full moon of the record-keeper](/blog/chitra-pournami-meaning).

## Which stories belong to this day?

Several, and they gather around the same idea. The stories themselves are old, but tying them to this particular day is mostly popular tradition rather than something the texts spell out.

The first is Sudama and the *aval*, the beaten rice, from the Bhagavata Purana: the friend who asked for nothing and walked home to find his hut turned into a mansion. The full story, and the sweet it left on Krishna Jayanthi plates, is in [aval for Krishna Jayanthi](/blog/aval-for-krishna-janmashtami). The gift was tiny. What came back did not run out.

The second is the *Akshaya Patra*. In the Mahabharata's forest book, the exiled Pandavas are followed into the woods by sages and guests they cannot feed. Yudhishthira prays to the sun, and Surya gives him a vessel that produces food each day, enough for every guest, until Draupadi herself has eaten. After that, it is empty until the next day.

The third is Parashurama, the sixth avatar of Vishnu, whose birth many families remember on this day. And there is a much-loved tradition that on Akshaya Tritiya, Vyasa began dictating the Mahabharata to Ganesha, who agreed to write only if the poet never paused. An inexhaustible story, begun on the inexhaustible day.

## Gold, or giving?

Today, Akshaya Tritiya is widely associated with buying gold. The logic is easy to follow: if what you acquire today keeps increasing, why not acquire something that holds its value? Jewellers are busy. There is nothing wrong with this, and many families enjoy it.

But the older practice points somewhere else. The traditional observance is *dana*, giving, and the recommended gifts are strikingly practical for the season. Akshaya Tritiya falls at the start of the hottest part of the year in much of India, so the texts and customs speak of giving water pots, hand fans, umbrellas, sandals, curd rice and cool drinks. You give what helps someone get through the heat. There is a thoughtful piece on [dana, the Hindu idea of giving](/blog/dana-in-hinduism) if you want to go further.

Look again at the stories. Sudama brings rice. The Akshaya Patra is a cooking pot. Neither is gold. The story says the day multiplies whatever you acquire. The story is really about the vessel, not what it holds. The inexhaustible thing is the willingness to give, the open hand, the pot that keeps being filled because it keeps being emptied for others. The same logic runs through the South's summer festivals, from Chitra Pournami to [Vaikasi Visakam, Murugan's birthday](/blog/vaikasi-visakam-murugan), where the best part of the day is the food handed out.

## Feed someone first

Here is the detail in the Akshaya Patra story that I keep returning to. The vessel fed everyone, the sages, the guests, the brothers, and it stopped only after Draupadi had eaten. The host ate last.

So a gentle suggestion for the day. Feed someone before you buy anything. It can be a meal given to a person who needs one, a donation to a food bank, a cold drink handed to the delivery driver at your door. Then, if you still want the gold, buy it. But begin with the pot.
