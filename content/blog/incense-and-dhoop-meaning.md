Light an incense stick and sit still for two minutes. Smell reaches you before you have decided to notice it, and that is the short answer to why incense is used in puja: fragrance is an offering that goes straight past the thinking mind. The tradition put it in the middle of worship because it changes a space, and the person in it, faster than almost anything else.

## Why incense at all?

In the classical sixteen-step pooja, the shodashopachara, dhoopa is one of the named offerings: fragrance offered to the deity, just as you offer water, flowers, light and food. Its place in the full sequence is clearest in [the sixteen steps of puja](/blog/sixteen-steps-of-puja). Incense sits right before the lamp, in the sequence that moves from the senses of smell to sight to taste.

Tradition links smell with the earth element, prithvi, the most grounded of the five. The rising smoke is read as prayer moving upward, visible for a moment and then gone. You do not have to take either idea literally to feel the effect. Many people simply find that a particular fragrance marks a space as different. The corner where the incense burns becomes the corner where you pray.

Try noticing this. The body reacts to smell before the mind has named it. The shoulders drop, or tighten. Memory arrives uninvited: a grandmother's house, a temple corridor, a festival morning. Ritual uses that. It builds a doorway you can walk through every day by striking a match.

## What is the difference between dhoop and agarbatti?

People use the words loosely, but they are different things.

- Agarbatti is the familiar stick: a thin bamboo core coated with a paste of fragrant material.
- Dhoop is stickless, usually a cone or a short, thick rod of pressed resin and herbs. It burns more densely, with more smoke and a stronger scent.
- Sambrani is benzoin resin, smoked on hot coals in a small clay or brass holder. In many Tamil homes it is carried through every room on Friday evenings, and sometimes used to dry a child's hair after a bath.
- Loban and guggul are other resins, each with its own sharper or earthier smell. Camphor, though burnt at the same altar, is really a flame offering, and [camphor in aarti](/blog/camphor-aarti-meaning) has a meaning of its own.

So, dhoop vs agarbatti: sticks are gentle and easy for apartments; dhoop and sambrani are heavier and more traditional. There is also a difference between natural incense, made from resins, woods and herbs, and heavily perfumed sticks that rely on synthetic fragrance. Many people find the natural kind easier to sit with for longer.

## How do you use it with attention, not habit?

Most of us light incense the way we switch on a light: quickly, without noticing. Slow it down.

Light one stick, not a bundle. More smoke is not more devotion. Light it after the lamp, from the lamp's flame if you can. Wave it three times clockwise before the deity, then set it in its holder and let it burn. If you finish with a flame, the guide to [how to do aarti at home](/blog/how-to-do-aarti-at-home) picks up from there. Incense usually travels with fresh blooms, and [which flower for which god](/blog/flowers-for-pooja) covers that half of the offering.

Some practical care. Open a window. Keep incense away from smoke alarms, and away from curtains. Children and pets with sensitive lungs may do better without it, and so may you on some days. There is nothing wrong with a smokeless practice. A flower and a lamp are complete offerings on their own. The same stick, by the way, is waved at a new car during a [vahana pooja](/blog/vahana-pooja-new-car), which tells you how universal the gesture is.

Incense for meditation at home works the same way. One stick, lit before you sit, becomes a timer and a signal. When it burns down, you are done.

## Stay until the smell disappears

For seven days, light one stick at the start of your practice. Sit and pay attention to the smell. Stay until you can no longer notice it, until the nose has stopped reporting it. That point usually comes sooner than you think. Then begin your prayer, or your breathing. You will find that you arrived somewhere while you were waiting.
