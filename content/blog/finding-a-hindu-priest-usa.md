Finding a Hindu priest in the US usually starts with one phone call to the nearest temple. Most temples keep a list of priests who do home poojas, and the office staff often know which one speaks your language and follows your family's tradition. After that, the right priest is simply the one who explains before he chants. I did not know that the first time. I learned it at a friend's homam in Houston.

## Where to start looking

The temple office is the easiest place. Call on a weekday, not during the weekend rush, and say what you need: a griha pravesh, a Ganapathy homam, a Satyanarayan puja, a star-birthday archana for a child. Many temples have resident priests who also visit homes; others will give you names. US temples often serve many communities at once, which is one reason [they house many gods under one roof](/blog/why-us-temples-have-many-deities), and it also means their priests come from several traditions.

Then, the community groups: the Tamil sangam, the Telugu association, the parents' group from school. Ask friends who have hosted a housewarming or a homam recently. A name that comes with a story, "he was patient with the kids", is worth more than a list.

For families far from any temple, some priests conduct poojas over video, and some families book the ritual at a temple in India instead, as described in [how online puja from the USA works](/blog/online-puja-from-usa-explained).

## What should you ask a priest before booking?

A short list covers most of it.

- **Tradition and language.** A Tamil Smartha priest, a Sri Vaishnava priest, a Telugu or Gujarati priest will each do things a little differently. If your parents will be watching on a call, they will notice. Ask which tradition he follows.
- **What he brings and what you buy.** Some priests bring everything; many send a list. Ask for it in writing a week ahead.
- **How long it takes.** A simple puja may take under an hour; a homam can take two or three. Plan the food accordingly.
- **Fire.** Is there a homam, a fire ritual, involved? If so, talk about the smoke alarm, ventilation and a fire-safe base. The difference between [a homam and a puja](/blog/homam-vs-puja) matters most in a house with carpet.
- **Dakshina.** The offering to the priest. Discuss it openly beforehand. Good priests are comfortable with the question, and it spares everyone an awkward envelope at the door.

## On the day

Clear a space on the floor, put down a sheet, and keep the items on a tray. The priest will begin with the sankalpam, [the spoken statement of intention](/blog/what-is-sankalpam), naming the family members, their gotra, or lineage, and their birth stars. Have these written down; everyone forgets their grandmother's star under pressure.

Children will wander. Let them. At my friend's homam in Houston, his daughter kept asking what each thing was, and the priest stopped after every offering to tell her: this is ghee, it is for Agni, the fire carries it to the gods. By the end, half the adults in the room were listening to him explain things to a seven-year-old. That is the priest you want.

And afterwards, tea. Sit with the priest for ten minutes. You will learn more about your own traditions over that cup than you did during the ritual.

## One small thing to carry

Keep a note on your phone, and on paper: the priest's name and number, and your family's gotra and birth stars. Next time is easier. The time after that, you will be the one a friend calls to ask.
