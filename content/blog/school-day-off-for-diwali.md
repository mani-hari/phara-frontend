The first time I wrote to my son's teacher about Deepavali, I overthought it for a week. I drafted, deleted, and worried about sounding demanding. The reply came in ten minutes. She said of course, and asked whether he could tell the class about the lamps. If your district does not have a school holiday for Diwali, asking for a day off is usually simpler than you fear: a short note, sent early, and an offer to share.

## Is Diwali an excused absence?

In many US school districts, absences for religious observance are excused, often with a note from a parent. A growing number of districts now close for Diwali altogether, particularly where there are large Hindu, Sikh and Jain communities, and the list keeps growing. Policies differ widely, so check your district's attendance policy and the school calendar when they are published, usually before the school year begins. Look for a phrase like "religious holiday" or "religious observance".

Diwali itself moves. It falls on the new moon of the lunar month of Ashvin or Karthika, usually in October or November, and the note on [when Diwali falls in the USA](/blog/when-is-diwali-in-the-usa) explains why the day can differ between India and America.

## School holiday for Diwali: writing the note

Three sentences are enough: the festival, the date, the request. Something like this:

"Our family celebrates Deepavali, the Hindu festival of lights, on (the day). We would like (your child's name) to be excused from school that day to celebrate with family. We would be happy to share a small clay lamp or a sweet with the class if that would be welcome."

Send it at least two weeks ahead. If there is a test or a project due that day, ask whether it can be moved. Most teachers appreciate the notice more than anything else.

The same note works for Pongal, Navratri, Ganesh Chaturthi, Holi, or any festival your family keeps, and for family ceremonies too, such as [a child's first haircut](/blog/mundan-first-haircut) on a trip to India. Some families choose one or two days a year and send the children to school on the others. That is a reasonable balance, and nobody is keeping score. Putting all the days in one place helps, and the [Hindu festival calendar for the USA](/blog/hindu-festival-calendar-usa) is a good place to start.

## Helping your child explain the day

Children are often asked, "What is Diwali?" and freeze. Give them one line: "It is the festival of lights, when we celebrate light winning over darkness." Then something to show: a clay diya, a photo of the kolam at the door, a piece of burfi. And one detail from the morning, because children remember mornings: the oil bath before sunrise, the new clothes, the first sparkler. The story behind that early bath is in [the Naraka Chaturdashi oil bath](/blog/naraka-chaturdashi-oil-bath), and there are ideas for younger children in [explaining Diwali to kids](/blog/explaining-diwali-to-kids).

My son is about eleven now, and his version for his class is better than mine. He talks about the lamps on the video call with his grandmother in Chennai, about staying up late, about the sweets. He does not mention Narakasura at all, and nobody misses him. Tiruvalluvar says, in Kural 100, that choosing harsh words when kind ones are at hand is like eating raw fruit when ripe fruit is right there. My son, without knowing the verse, picks the ripe fruit every time.

## One small thing to carry

In the week school starts, sit down with the family calendar and a panchangam and put the lunar festival days for the school year into it. Then write the teacher notes in one sitting and set a reminder to send each one two weeks ahead. It takes an hour in late summer and saves a week of worrying in autumn. And one day, when your child is a student far from home, as in [a Hindu student in a dorm room](/blog/hindu-student-in-a-dorm), they will already know how to ask.
