Your spouse does not have to believe anything to sit beside you at the lamp. They only have to be there, and presence is the first offering in any puja. Doing puja with a non-Hindu spouse or partner works best when it starts small: give them one gesture, explain it in a sentence, and let the rest be silence. Most partners are not resisting the ritual. They are worried about doing it wrong, or about being asked to pretend. Remove both worries and something easier opens up.

## Can a non-Hindu join a puja?

In most home traditions, yes. A home puja is not a test of membership. It is closer to hosting.

The classic sequence of worship, the sixteen upacharas or offerings, treats the deity as an honoured guest: you invite, offer a seat, water to wash the feet, water to drink, a bath, clothes, fragrance, flowers, incense, light, food, and a farewell. There is a clear walk-through of [the sixteen steps of puja](/blog/sixteen-steps-of-puja) if you want the whole structure. Read it with this in mind: the entire ritual is an act of hospitality. And a guest can welcome a guest. Your partner, sitting beside you, is part of the household that is receiving.

Some temples and some very orthodox rites have their own rules about who may perform certain parts. At home, you set the tone.

## Roles that work for a non-Hindu spouse

Here are gestures that work well, each with the one sentence you might say.

- **Ringing the bell.** "This calls the attention in, ours and the deity's."
- **Lighting the lamp.** "Light is the simplest offering: we are awake, we are here."
- **Offering a flower.** "Something beautiful, given without asking for anything back."
- **Holding the aarti plate**, or waving it. "We circle the light before the deity, then take its warmth back to ourselves." There is more on this in [the meaning of the camphor aarti](/blog/camphor-aarti-meaning).

Do not explain everything at once. One gesture per week is plenty. Many partners find that doing something with their hands is far easier than being asked to watch, because watching feels like being on the outside, and doing feels like taking part.

Avoid asking them to recite Sanskrit before they want to. A mispronounced mantra in front of an expert spouse is a lonely feeling. If they ask to learn one, start with a single line.

## Try this for a week

Pick an evening time, the same each day, and keep it to five minutes. A small shelf in the apartment is enough; the lamp kept clear of the smoke alarm and away from curtains.

1. One of you lights the lamp. The other offers a flower.
2. Sit for one minute in silence. No phones, no plans for dinner.
3. One line of thanks, spoken aloud by either of you. It can be to a deity, to the day, to each other.
4. Blow out the lamp, or let it burn if it is safe.

That is all. The [five-minute daily puja](/blog/five-minute-daily-puja) has more variations if you want to build on it later.

Afterwards, once or twice in the week, ask your partner a different question from the one you might expect. Not "Do you believe in this?" but "What did you notice?" You may hear: the smell of the ghee, the way the flame moved, that the minute felt long and then short. Attention is the heart of puja. If your partner is noticing, they are already practising.

Couples who pray together find that the practice changes the evenings as much as anything spiritual. [Praying together as a couple](/blog/praying-together-as-a-couple) picks up from there.

## Let it be a place you both arrive at

The puja is not a test of your partner, or of your marriage. Some partners, over months, will start to pray in their own way. Some will simply sit, week after week, and seem to like it. Some will step away and come back at Deepavali. Each of these is welcome.

What matters is that the lamp is lit and that you are not alone in front of it. That alone changes the quality of a room. Try it for seven evenings and see what you both notice.
