After my father's knee operation, my mother became his nurse, and I became a voice on the phone from New Jersey. Both of us were caregivers. Neither of us was praying for ourselves. That is the usual pattern, and it is why a prayer for caregivers matters: the person holding on also needs holding, and the tradition, read closely, agrees.

## Why caregivers need prayer too

For weeks my mother did everything. The exercises, the medicines, the meals without salt, the nights when my father could not sleep. On our calls she told me about his pain, his progress, his moods. When I asked how she was, she changed the subject.

A great deal of caregiver burnout begins exactly there, with the carer disappearing from their own prayers. We pray for the patient. We light lamps for the patient. The one doing the lifting becomes invisible, including to herself.

The Tirumantiram, Tirumoolar's great Tamil work, has a line many Tamil families know: anbe sivam, love is Shiva. The verse says that the ignorant think love and Shiva are two, but the wise know they are one. I read it now as permission. If love is Shiva, then the care itself is worship. The bedpan, the pill box, the third reheating of the same rasam are not a distraction from the spiritual life. They are it.

That changes the question. The caregiver is not someone who has no time for God. She is someone doing a very demanding kind of puja, and she needs rest and support the way a priest needs water.

## What can a faraway caregiver do?

Many of us here are caring for elderly parents from abroad, and the guilt of the ocean in between is heavy. The practical side is in [caring for parents from abroad](/blog/caring-for-parents-from-abroad). Here is what I learned about the spiritual side.

- **The call at the same hour each day.** Not a long call. A fixed one. My mother came to rely on it more than I realised, not for advice but for the certainty that it would come.
- **Arrange help locally.** A cook for a month, a physiotherapist who visits, a cousin who comes on Sundays. Paying for help is not a failure of love. It is a way of taking weight off the person carrying it.
- **Light a lamp at their evening.** My mother lights hers at six. I lit a tea-light at the same moment, which was my morning, before work.
- **Put down the guilt about distance.** It does not help anyone. My father said so, quite sharply, on one call, and he was right.

If the parent is still unwell, [praying for a sick parent from abroad](/blog/praying-for-a-sick-parent) may be the better place to start.

## A prayer for the one who is tired

Here is what I eventually suggested to my mother, and what I now suggest to friends.

**One line on waking.** Before getting up, before the day's list begins, say one line for yourself. "Give me strength for today" is enough. So is a god's name.

**A Hanuman prayer for strength.** Hanuman is the god of tireless service, the one who carried the mountain. Many caregivers find the Hanuman Chalisa, or even its first verse, a natural mantra for strength. Many families keep it for Saturdays, as I described in [the Hanuman Chalisa on Saturdays](/blog/hanuman-chalisa-on-saturdays).

**Five minutes that belong only to you.** A cup of tea on the balcony. A walk to the end of the street. Not for the patient, not for the house. For you. At night, some carers end the day with [three slow shantis](/blog/om-shanti-shanti-shanti-meaning), one each for the body, the people and the things that cannot be fixed.

And the practical prayers, which are prayers too: rest when someone offers to sit with the patient; respite care if it is available; a doctor's visit for yourself if you are not sleeping, not eating or not coping. A caregiver who falls ill cannot care for anyone.

Some caregivers also carry the question of why this is happening at all. [Whether illness is karma](/blog/illness-and-karma) takes that question on, and many families find its answer kinder than they expected.

## One small thing to carry

This week, ask one person to take one task off your list. Only one. The shopping. The pharmacy run. The Tuesday evening. Say it plainly: "Can you do this for me?"

Then let that be part of the prayer. Accepting help is not the opposite of devotion, and when the illness lifts, [thanksgiving rituals after recovery](/blog/gratitude-after-recovery) can include the helpers too. My mother, eventually, let my cousin take the Sunday shift, and on the first free Sunday afternoon in months she went to the temple by herself, and came home and slept for three hours.
