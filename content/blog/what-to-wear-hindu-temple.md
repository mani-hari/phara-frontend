Wear what lets you sit on the floor for twenty minutes without thinking about your clothes. That is most of the answer to what to wear to a Hindu temple. Shoulders and knees covered, nothing tight or see-through, clean clothes. Traditional wear is welcome and lovely, but at most temples in the US it is not required. The rest is about attention, and that part is more interesting than any dress code.

## What to wear to a Hindu temple, in general

The basic guidance is the same for everyone.

- **Cover shoulders and knees.** Long skirts, salwar kameez, sarees, loose trousers, kurtas, full shirts.
- **Nothing tight or sheer.** Not because anyone is policing you, but because you will be bending, sitting cross-legged and bowing.
- **Clean clothes.** Many people bathe before going, and the clothes follow the same logic: you arrive fresh.
- **Layers in winter.** A shawl or scarf is useful. Halls can be cold, and you will leave your shoes and often your socks at the door.

For women, the temple dress code in most American temples is simply modest: a kurta with leggings or palazzo, a long dress with a shawl, a saree if you enjoy one. For men, what to wear to a Hindu temple can be as simple as a collared shirt and trousers. On festival days you will see veshtis, dhotis and silk kurtas, and nobody minds if you are not in one.

Leather is worth a thought. Some temples ask that belts, wallets and bags of leather be left outside the sanctum. Many people just leave them in the car.

## Can you wear jeans to a Hindu temple?

At most American temples, yes. You will see jeans every weekend and nobody will say a word.

But try an experiment. Go once in stiff jeans and once in loose cotton, and notice what changes. In jeans, most people sit on the floor for a few minutes, shift, lean against a wall, then stand. In loose cotton the legs fold easily, the spine lifts on its own, and the breath goes lower into the belly. You stay longer without deciding to. The tradition was never only about looking respectful. It was about letting the body settle into the space.

This connects with something older. Sitting on the floor to pray keeps the body close to the ground, the hips open, the attention low and steady. Why the floor matters at all is in [sitting on the floor to pray](/blog/sitting-on-the-floor-to-pray).

## Why does it matter at all?

Clothing is a signal to your own mind. You wear one thing to work, another to sleep, another to run. Each shift tells the nervous system what kind of time this is. Changing into temple clothes says: this is a different space, slow down.

Try watching what happens at the door. You take off your shoes. Your feet touch cool stone or carpet. Then maybe you [ring the temple bell](/blog/why-ring-a-temple-bell) on the way in. Each of these small acts is a threshold, and clothes are the first of them. If you arrive in the same outfit you wore to a stressful meeting, some part of you is still in that meeting.

A note for travel: some temples in South India follow stricter traditional rules. At certain Kerala temples, for instance, men enter bare-chested in a veshti, and women are expected in a saree or long skirt. Rules differ from temple to temple, so check before you visit, and carry a simple veshti or a long skirt in your bag.

If you are going for the first time, the practical walk-through of [a first visit to a Hindu temple in the US](/blog/first-visit-hindu-temple-usa) covers the shoe rack, the shrines and the prasadam line.

## Try this for a month

Keep one set of temple clothes. Loose, simple, cotton if you can, something you wear only for the temple or your home puja. Wash it, fold it, keep it apart. Before each visit, change into it, even if it takes two minutes you do not feel you have.

After four weeks, notice. Do you sit longer? Do you rush less between shrines? Does the drive home feel different? Many people find the clothes start working before they reach the door. That is the whole point: not a rule, but a reminder you wear.
