My grandmother in Thanjavur told this story as if it were town gossip: a young wife, two mangoes, a husband who ran away, and a woman who decided what she would look like for the rest of her life. The Karaikkal Ammaiyar story is the story of Punithavathi, a merchant's wife from the coastal town of Karaikal, who became one of the earliest Tamil poets of Shiva and one of only three women among the sixty-three Nayanmars, the Shaiva saints. Shiva himself, the story says, called her Mother.

## The Karaikkal Ammaiyar story in the Periya Puranam

Sekkizhar tells it in the Periya Puranam, the great Tamil book of the Nayanmar lives. Punithavathi was born into a merchant family in Karaikal and married a merchant named Paramadattan. She was devoted to Shiva from childhood and fed any of his devotees who came to her door.

One day her husband sent home two mangoes. Before he returned for his meal, a hungry devotee of Shiva arrived. There was no cooked food ready, so Punithavathi gave him one of the mangoes with rice.

At lunch Paramadattan ate the remaining mango, liked it, and asked for the second. Punithavathi went into the kitchen, knowing there was nothing there, and prayed. A mango appeared in her hand. She served it. He found it far sweeter than the first and asked where it had come from.

She told him the truth. He did not believe her. Ask your god for another, then, he said. She prayed again, and another mango appeared, and then vanished from his hand.

## Why did she ask to lose her beauty?

My grandmother always paused here, the way you pause before the good part of gossip. Paramadattan was not grateful. He was frightened. He had married a woman, and she had turned out to be something he could not understand.

He left quietly on a trading voyage, settled in another city far to the south, married again, and named his daughter Punithavathi. When her family finally found him and brought her to him, he fell at her feet in front of everyone and worshipped her as a goddess.

Punithavathi understood that her marriage was over. Her beauty had been kept for a husband who no longer wanted it. So she asked Shiva to take it away. She prayed to be given the form of a pey, a gaunt spirit of the cremation ground, all bones and sinew, so that she could live for devotion alone and never again be looked at in the old way. The prayer was granted.

As a boy I thought this was the sad part. My grandmother did not. She said Punithavathi was the first person in the story who got to choose.

## Who calls a skeleton Mother?

The story goes on. Punithavathi set out for Kailasa, and when she reached the holy mountain, she refused to let her feet touch it. She climbed the last stretch on her head. Parvati, watching, asked who this strange being was. Shiva answered: this is our mother, Ammai, who comes to us with love. That is how Punithavathi became Karaikkal Ammaiyar, the Mother from Karaikal.

When Shiva asked what she wanted, she asked for no rebirth, and if she must be born again, never to forget him. She asked also to sit beneath his feet and sing while he danced. He sent her to Tiruvalangadu, where she sang her hymns under the dancing foot, the dance that [Nataraja symbolism](/blog/nataraja-symbolism) unpacks. Among the women Nayanmars, she is the one whose own songs survive, and scholars count them among the oldest poems of Tamil Shaiva devotion.

In Karaikal the mango festival still honours her, with mangoes showered from rooftops on the procession of the deity in the Tamil month of Aani. It is one of the loveliest festivals I know of, where the offering is exactly what the story needs: a fruit given freely.

## One thing to carry into the week

My wife, when I told her this story, said it sounded like a woman walking out of a room where people only looked at her. That is close. Karaikkal Ammaiyar stopped performing for an audience that had never really seen her. [Shakti in daily practice](/blog/shakti-in-daily-practice) looks at how that strength lives in ordinary days, and [women in Hinduism](/blog/women-in-hinduism-goddess) takes the broader view.

Most of us are not asked to become skeletons. But most of us do something every day mainly to be seen a certain way: a sentence rewritten to sound smart, a photo retaken, a coat chosen for a meeting. This week, notice one of them. Then try one small act where how you look does not matter at all. Feed someone. Sing badly in the car. The Mother of Karaikal would understand.
