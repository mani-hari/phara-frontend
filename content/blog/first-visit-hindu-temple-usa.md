The first time I walked into a temple in central New Jersey, it looked like a warehouse from the parking lot, and inside it smelled exactly like my grandmother's house in Thanjavur. Camphor, jasmine, ghee, something frying in the canteen. If this is your first time visiting Hindu temples in America, whether you grew up with one or never have, you will be fine. Take your shoes off, walk slowly, and watch what others do. Nobody is grading you.

## What to expect your first time visiting Hindu temples

At the entrance there is a shoe rack, sometimes a whole room of them, with small numbered cubbies or just long shelves. Shoes off, socks optional. There is usually a sink nearby, and many people wash their hands before going in.

Inside, most American temples are one large hall with several shrines along the walls and a main sanctum at the centre or the far end. You will hear bells, a priest chanting somewhere, a child asking loudly for a snack. It is not hushed like a library. People come and go, sit on the carpet, talk quietly with friends, pray, and leave. Nobody checks your credentials at the door, and nobody will ask whether you are the right kind of Hindu.

What to wear is a question of its own, answered practically in [what to wear to a Hindu temple](/blog/what-to-wear-hindu-temple). The short version: clean, modest, easy to sit in.

## How do you pray at the shrines?

There is a loose order, and it helps to know it. Most people greet Ganesha first, because he is the remover of obstacles and the one who opens every beginning. If you want the story, [why Ganesha comes first](/blog/why-ganesha-comes-first) tells it. Then the main deity of the temple, then any others you feel drawn to.

At each shrine, stand a little to one side of the doorway rather than directly in front, so you are not blocking the view of the priest or others. Palms together. A moment of silence. You can say a name, a sentence, nothing at all. Many people ring the bell as they arrive (my son rings it too hard), and [why we ring a temple bell](/blog/why-ring-a-temple-bell) explains what the sound is for.

Some people walk clockwise around a shrine, keeping the deity on their right. That is pradakshina, circumambulation, and the [meaning of pradakshina](/blog/pradakshina-meaning) is worth reading once. When the priest brings out the aarti, the lamp waved before the deity, people pass their palms over the flame and touch their eyes. You can do the same, or simply bow.

## Offerings and prasadam

You do not need to bring anything. If you wish, fruit, flowers or a coconut are traditional, and there is always a donation box, a hundi, for whatever you want to give. Many temples also have a counter where you can request an archana, the chanting of a deity's names with your own name and family star included.

Prasadam, food that has been offered to the deity, is handed out after aarti: a spoon of sweet pongal, a piece of fruit, a few drops of holy water poured into your palm. Receive it with the right hand, or both hands cupped. Eat it there or take it home.

Then there is the canteen, which for many families is half the reason to come. Dosa, sambar, curd rice, a paper plate balanced on a knee. My son rates temples by their vadai.

A quiet word about photographs: most temples do not allow photos of the sanctum. There are usually signs. When in doubt, keep the phone in your pocket. You will remember it better anyway.

If you are bringing someone who is not Hindu, I have written separately about [bringing a non-Hindu friend to the temple](/blog/bringing-a-friend-to-temple).

## One small thing to carry into the week

Go once on a weekday evening, when it is quiet. After you have greeted Ganesha and the main deity, sit on the floor at the back for five minutes. Do not pray unless you want to. Just listen to the bells and the shuffle of feet. Let the place become familiar before a festival crowd arrives, and the next time you come, it will already feel a little like yours.
