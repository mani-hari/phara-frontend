Atman is the self you actually are. Brahman is the reality everything is made of. The boldest claim of the Upanishads is that atman and Brahman are the same. You cannot settle that by argument; people have been arguing about it for a very long time. But you can do something more useful. You can look, carefully, at who is asking.

## Atman and Brahman in one breath

Atman is the innermost self: not your body, not your thoughts, not your moods, but whatever is aware of all of them. Brahman is the ground of everything that exists, the one reality beneath the many forms. The Mandukya Upanishad says it in three words: "Ayam atma brahma", this self is Brahman.

Notice that this is not a claim that you are important. It is almost the opposite. The small self, the one with opinions and a to-do list, is not what the text is pointing at. It is pointing at something quieter behind it. The philosophy built on this idea has its own piece, [Advaita explained simply](/blog/advaita-simply-explained). If the world of many things then starts to look like a trick, there is more on that in [what maya really means](/blog/maya-meaning-illusion).

## Salt in water: Uddalaka's lesson to Shvetaketu

In the Chandogya Upanishad, a young man named Shvetaketu comes home after twelve years of study, proud of how much he knows. His father, Uddalaka, asks him a simple question he cannot answer, and then gives him a lesson in the kitchen.

He tells his son to drop a lump of salt into a cup of water and leave it overnight. In the morning: "Bring me the salt." Shvetaketu looks. It is gone. "Taste the water from the top." Salty. "From the middle." Salty. "From the bottom." Salty. The salt is everywhere, and you cannot see it anywhere. Then comes the line the Upanishad returns to again and again: "Tat tvam asi." You are that.

What I find useful in this story is that the father does not argue. He gives his son an experience: taste this. The unity is something to be seen, not merely believed.

## Who is asking who I am?

Here is a practice. Sit for ten minutes, back straight but not stiff, eyes closed or resting on the floor.

Notice a thought as it arises. Do not follow it. Just notice: a thought. Then notice a sensation, the weight of your hands, the air on your face. Then a mood: restless, flat, calm. Each time, ask quietly: who is noticing this?

You will not find an answer in words. What you may find is that the noticing itself does not seem to come and go the way thoughts and moods do. They move; something watching them does not. That is the direction the Upanishads are pointing.

This is the self-inquiry associated with Ramana Maharshi of Tiruvannamalai, who taught people to hold the question "Who am I?" until the questioner dissolves. You do not need to accept any doctrine to try it. Treat it as an experiment, not a belief. If you already keep a daily puja and wonder how this fits, the short answer is that they are not rivals; [meditation and puja](/blog/meditation-vs-puja) gives the long one. Nor do you need to leave home for it, as the tradition's picture of [the household and the forest](/blog/householder-and-forest-dweller) makes clear.

Why bother? Because most of what troubles us happens to the small self: an insult, a worry, an old regret. The tradition says that is not all you are, and that seeing this is a kind of freedom. The fuller idea is in [what is moksha](/blog/what-is-moksha).

## Ask who is irritated

Once a day, when you feel irritated, and you will, pause and ask quietly: "Who is irritated?" Do not answer. Just ask, and watch what happens to the irritation. Many people find it loosens, not because they have pushed it down, but because for a moment they have stepped back far enough to see it.

Keep a line in a notebook each evening about what you noticed. At the end of the week, read the seven lines. You may find the question has started asking itself.
