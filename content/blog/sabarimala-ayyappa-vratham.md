The forty-one days before Sabarimala are the pilgrimage. The walk up the hill to Ayyappa's shrine in the Kerala forest is only the last few hours of it. That is the first thing to understand about the Sabarimala Ayyappa vratham: it is a discipline you live in your own home, at your own job, in your own body, for a full mandala of forty-one days, and the climb is where it ends, not where it begins.

## What does the Sabarimala Ayyappa vratham involve?

The vratham, a vow of observance, begins when the devotee wears the mala, a string of tulsi or rudraksha beads, usually put on by a guru or an elder pilgrim at a temple. From that moment certain disciplines apply. The details vary by family and by guru, but the core is widely shared:

- Black or blue clothes, simple and unfussy.
- Vegetarian food, often without onion and garlic, no alcohol, no tobacco.
- Celibacy for the whole period.
- Sleeping simply, on a mat or the floor rather than a soft bed.
- Bare feet where possible, and no shaving or haircuts.
- Prayers morning and evening, often with a bath before each.

One more custom does a great deal of work. During the vratham every devotee is called Swami, and calls every other devotee Swami too. The bank clerk, the bus driver and the film star in the queue all answer to the same name. Rank drops away with the beard.

Curious about the beads themselves? Start with [rudraksha and what it means](/blog/rudraksha-meaning).

## Why forty-one days?

A mandala is traditionally forty-one days, and it is used across Indian practice as the length of a full cycle of discipline. It is long enough for a habit to stop being an effort and start being part of the body. The first week you notice every craving. The second, you negotiate. Somewhere in the third or fourth week, something settles. By the end, the new way of eating, sleeping and speaking is simply how you live.

Try this, even if you never plan to go to Sabarimala. Pick one of these disciplines, just one: no screens after nine, vegetarian meals, sleeping on a firmer surface, a lamp every evening. Keep it for forty-one days. Notice what happens to your cravings, your sleep and your temper. [Fasting and focus](/blog/fasting-and-focus) looks at the same mechanism from the side of food.

## Who is Ayyappa, and what is the irumudi?

Ayyappa is Hariharaputra, the son of Hari and Hara, Vishnu and Shiva. The story says he was born when Vishnu took the form of Mohini, the enchantress, and was found as a baby by the king of Pandalam, who raised him as a prince. He is the forest prince who rides a tiger, the lord of the Bhutas, the wild spirits of the hills. That he is born of both gods matters to his devotees. He belongs to Shaivas and Vaishnavas alike.

The irumudi is the two-part bundle every pilgrim carries on their head. One side holds offerings for the god, most importantly a coconut filled with ghee, which is poured over Ayyappa at the shrine. The other side holds what the pilgrim needs for the journey. You cannot climb without it, and you cannot put it down along the way.

The irumudi is the vratham made visible. On your head, together, are what you give and what you need. Neither is allowed to crowd out the other.

## A shortened vratham at home

The mandala season begins in the Tamil month of Karthigai, around the time of [Karthigai Deepam](/blog/karthigai-deepam-significance), and ends with the Makaravilakku around [Makar Sankranti](/blog/makar-sankranti-meaning), when the sun turns north. If you cannot travel, you can still keep a version at home.

Try this for the season: a simple diet, a lamp lit every morning and evening, and the chant Swamiye Saranam Ayyappa, repeated slowly, for a few minutes each time. Keep it for forty-one days, or for as many as you honestly can. You may find, as pilgrims do, that the discipline was the destination all along.
