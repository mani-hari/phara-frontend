A japa mala is a string of 108 beads with one larger bead, and learning how to use a japa mala takes about two minutes. The deeper part takes longer, and that part you only learn by doing. So try one round tomorrow morning: one bead per breath, one mantra per bead. Notice how different it feels from counting in your head. The fingers take over the arithmetic, and the mind is left with nothing to do but listen.

## How to use a japa mala, bead by bead

Japa means repetition, the practice of saying a mantra or a divine name again and again. A mala is a garland. Together they are a tool for keeping count without thinking about counting.

Hold the mala in your right hand. Drape it over the middle finger or the ring finger, depending on the tradition you follow; both are common. Let the rest of it hang loosely.

Find the larger bead. It is called the meru, after the mountain at the centre of the world, and it is often called the guru bead. Start on the bead beside it.

Say your mantra once, aloud, in a whisper or silently. Then use your thumb to move that bead toward you, over the finger, and arrive at the next one. Say the mantra again. Move again. That is the whole technique. After 108 beads, you will arrive back at the meru, and one round is complete.

Many people find the breath settles into the rhythm without being asked. One bead, one breath, one mantra. A minute of [pranayama before pooja](/blog/pranayama-before-pooja) before the first bead makes that easier.

## Why not cross the guru bead?

When you reach the meru at the end of a round, you do not pass over it. You turn the mala around in your hand and go back the way you came, starting a new round in the opposite direction.

There are two reasons, and both are worth keeping. The traditional one is respect: the meru represents the teacher, and you do not step over your teacher. The practical one is the body. Turning the mala is a physical cue that a round has ended. Your hand knows, even if your mind has wandered off. That small moment of turning is a chance to come back.

Japa mala rules like this can look fussy from outside. From inside, they are mostly about attention. Each one gives the body a clear signal, so the mind does not have to hold it.

## Which beads, which finger, which mantra?

People often ask about rudraksha vs tulsi mala. Traditionally, rudraksha seeds are used for Shiva mantras, such as Om Namah Shivaya. Tulsi, the holy basil, is used for Vishnu and Krishna mantras, including the [Hare Krishna maha mantra](/blog/hare-krishna-maha-mantra). Sandalwood is common for Devi and general practice; some traditions prefer crystal. If you already have a mala from a grandparent or a temple, that is the one to use.

Which finger to use for mala is the other common question. The index finger is traditionally kept away from the beads. One explanation is that the index finger represents the ego, the finger we point with. Whether or not you hold that meaning, keeping it clear does make the grip steadier.

As for the mantra: choose one and stay with it. It can be a single name, a short line, or a longer verse given by a teacher. If you are not sure where to begin, [chanting for beginners](/blog/chanting-for-beginners) walks through a few simple options.

Regional variations are fine. Some families use the left hand for counting on the fingers instead of beads; some wrap the mala in a cloth bag while chanting. None of this is a test. Pick a way and be consistent.

## A round before breakfast

One round each morning. Same seat, same time if you can, same mantra. Before you start, sit for a moment and let the body settle. Then begin beside the meru and go.

Do not worry about speed. A round of a short mantra may take ten minutes or more, and that is part of the point. Let the fingers keep count so the mind can listen. At some point it will wander, and you will notice that your thumb is still moving. Come back to the word. Many people find that [a morning of silence](/blog/mouna-vratam-silence) after the round is where the practice really lands. Try both, and see which bead you are on when the mind first goes quiet.
