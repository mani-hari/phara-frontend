Nachiketa was a boy who kept asking the god of death questions until Yama, impressed, told him the secret of the self. The Katha Upanishad makes a stubborn teenager its hero. That is worth remembering if you are a parent living with a teenager questioning Hinduism, one argument at a time, at the dinner table. Doubt is not a leak in the tradition. It is one of its oldest doors. The texts are full of young people who refuse easy answers, and the tradition kept their questions, word for word, for thousands of years.

## Is questioning Hinduism a kind of heresy?

The short answer is no, and the texts themselves say so. Hinduism has no single creed a person must sign, and so no clean idea of heresy in the way some traditions have. What it has instead is a long habit of asking.

Look at the names. The Kena Upanishad is named for its first word, kena, which means "by whom?": by whom is the mind directed, by whom does the breath move? The Prashna Upanishad literally means the Upanishad of questions; six students come to a teacher, and he makes them wait a year before they may ask anything. The whole philosophical tradition was built on shastrartha, formal debate, where you had to state your opponent's position fairly before answering it, a practice that amounted to [debate as devotion](/blog/debate-as-devotion-shastrartha).

So a teenager saying "I'm not sure any of this is true" is doing something the Upanishads would recognise. Many adults quietly hold that position too; [being Hindu without believing in God](/blog/hindu-without-believing-in-god) looks at how common it is.

## Arjuna argued too

The Bhagavad Gita, the most read of all Hindu texts, is not a sermon. It is a conversation with a man who refuses to act. Arjuna, on the battlefield, drops his bow and says he will not fight. He argues. He asks Krishna to be clearer. He changes the subject. He asks the same question again in different words.

Krishna does not lose patience. He answers across eighteen chapters, from many angles, and then, in the last chapter, he does something remarkable. He says, in effect: I have told you everything. Now reflect on it fully, and do as you wish. Vimrishyaitad asheshena, yathecchasi tatha kuru. The greatest teacher in the tradition ends by handing the decision back to the student.

The story is really about respect. Krishna does not demand that Arjuna agree. He trusts him to think.

## How parents can listen without losing the thread

Where do the questions come from? For many teenagers in America, from ordinary places. A world religions unit in high school that describes Hinduism in three paragraphs and gets half of it wrong. Atheist friends with sharp arguments. Classmates asking why they do not eat beef, or whether Hindus worship cows, or what the dot on their mother's forehead means. The pressure to explain a whole tradition, at fifteen, to people who find it strange.

A few things seem to help. Separate belief from belonging. A teenager can doubt that Ganesha exists and still love the festival, still want to be at the family puja, still belong. Tell them that plainly. Invite rather than insist: ask them to come to the temple with you, and accept "not this time". Small questions about practice, [what to wear to a temple](/blog/what-to-wear-hindu-temple) or whether they have to fast, can become larger conversations if you do not win them too quickly; [whether children should fast](/blog/should-kids-fast-hindu) is one of those.

Grandparents can help here too; a teenager often hears from [a grandparent on a video call](/blog/grandparents-on-video-calls) what they will not hear from a parent. And share your own doubts. Most teenagers assume their parents have never questioned anything. Tell them about the time you did. It will be the most honest thing you say to them all year.

## Staying in the conversation

Nachiketa, Arjuna, the students in the Prashna Upanishad: in every case, the teacher stays. Yama could have sent the boy home. Krishna could have ordered Arjuna to fight. Neither did. They stayed in the conversation until the student found his own footing. The story is really about staying.

Here is one small thing to try. This week, ask your teen one real question, not a quiz, not a lesson in disguise. "What do you think happens after we die?" "Is there anything about our festivals you actually like?" Then listen, and do not correct the answer. You may be surprised. You will certainly be trusted a little more the next time they have a question of their own.
