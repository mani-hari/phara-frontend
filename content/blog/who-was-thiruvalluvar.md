We know almost nothing certain about the man who wrote 1,330 couplets on how to live. So who was Thiruvalluvar? The honest answer is the poet who wrote the Tirukkural, the great Tamil book of ethics, and very little else can be said for sure. The story says he was a weaver in Mylapore, with a patient wife named Vasuki. The story is really about how a book can grow bigger than its author, until every community in Tamil Nadu wants to claim him.

## Who was Thiruvalluvar, as far as anyone knows?

Start with the name. Valluvar may be a personal name, or it may point to a community, the Valluvars, traditionally associated with priestly or astrological work, or with royal proclamations. Thiru is simply an honorific, like "sri". Beyond that, the name tells us little.

His dates are no better. Scholars have placed him centuries apart, some very early, some much later, and there is no agreement at all. That is why no years are given here. What matters is the work: 133 chapters of ten couplets each, in three books on virtue, wealth and love, written in a compressed metre called kural venba, each couplet only seven words long. Whoever he was, he had an extraordinary gift for saying a great deal in very little.

This is the history of Thiruvalluvar that can be stated with any confidence. Everything else is legend.

## The legends: a loom, a wife named Vasuki, a plank that shrank

The popular thiruvalluvar biography, as it is told in Tamil homes, begins in Mylapore, in what is now Chennai. Valluvar is a weaver, sitting at his loom. Wisdom comes to him not in a forest or a monastery but while he works cloth.

His wife, Vasuki, is the heroine of several stories. The best known says that at every meal she placed a needle and a small cup of water beside his plate. She never asked why. Many years later, near the end of her life, she finally asked. He explained: if a single grain of rice fell from the plate, he would pick it up with the needle and rinse it in the water, so nothing would be wasted. In all their years, not one grain had ever fallen. Her serving had been that careful. The story is really about attention inside a household, and about a wisdom that grew out of ordinary married life rather than escape from it.

A later tale takes the Kural to Madurai, to the legendary Sangam, the academy of poets. The poets sat on a magic plank that would only make room for the worthy. When the Kural was placed on it, the plank shrank until there was room for the book alone, and the proud poets tumbled into the water. The legend is not history. It is a way of saying that this one book outweighed an entire assembly.

## Why does every faith claim the Kural?

Read the Tirukkural and you notice something missing. It never names a sectarian god. There is no Shiva, no Vishnu, no Jina by name. The first couplet, Kural 1, begins:

*Agara mudhala ezhuththellaam aadhi*\
*bhagavan mudhatre ulagu.*

As the letter A is the first of all letters, so the Primal God is first of the world. That is as specific as it gets. A reader can hear Shiva in it, or Vishnu, or the Jain Tirthankara, or simply the first principle of existence.

So everyone has claimed him. Jain scholars point to the Kural's strong stress on non-violence and vegetarianism. Shaiva and Vaishnava readers find their own god in the Primal One. Secular readers find a practical book of ethics that needs no god at all. Each reading has its arguments, and none has won. To begin reading it, try [the Thirukkural for daily life](/blog/tirukkural-for-daily-life); for one of its most practical sections, [the Thirukkural on wealth](/blog/tirukkural-on-wealth). Its chapter on honest trade is also at the centre of [Hinduism and business ethics](/blog/hinduism-business-ethics).

## The gift of anonymity

We tend to want our great books tied to great lives. We want to know the author's childhood, his teacher, his crisis. The Tirukkural gives us none of that, and perhaps that is the gift.

A book with no certain author cannot be judged by his reputation, his caste, his creed or his era. It can only be judged by what it says. The Kural asks to be read that way, couplet by couplet, and tested against life. The legends of the loom and the needle add warmth, but they do not add authority. The authority is in the lines.

Perhaps that is why a poet grandmother like [Avvaiyar](/blog/avvaiyar-wisdom-sayings), in Tamil legend, is sometimes called his sister: the tradition wanted its wisest voices in the same family. Whoever Valluvar was, he left behind a book that belongs to anyone who reads it.
