Ram Navami arrives when the heat does, and the naivedyam, the food offered to the deity, knows it. Two drinks carry the festival: panakam, sweet and peppery, and neer mor, thin buttermilk with ginger. This panakam recipe and its buttermilk companion take five minutes each and no stove, which makes them some of the easiest offerings in the whole year. You mix, you strain, you offer a cup at the lamp, and then you pour for everyone.

## Panakam recipe: jaggery, dry ginger, cardamom, water

**Ingredients (about four glasses)**

- 100 g grated jaggery (½ cup)
- 750 ml cold water (3 cups)
- ½ teaspoon dry ginger powder (sukku)
- ¼ teaspoon cardamom powder
- A pinch of crushed black pepper
- Juice of half a lemon
- A few tulsi leaves, optional
- A pinch of salt

**Method**

1. Stir the jaggery into the water until it dissolves completely.
2. Strain through a fine sieve or cloth; jaggery often carries a little grit.
3. Add dry ginger, cardamom, pepper, lemon juice and salt. Stir well.
4. Tear in the tulsi leaves just before offering.

Taste it once, for the family jug only. It should be sweet first, then warm at the back of the throat. That second note is the pepper and dry ginger, and it is what makes panakam different from plain sweet water.

## Neer mor: buttermilk made thin and bright

Neer mor means water buttermilk, and that is exactly what it is: curd thinned right down until it runs like water, then seasoned.

**Ingredients**

- 125 g plain curd (½ cup)
- 500 ml cold water (2 cups)
- Salt to taste
- A small piece of ginger, crushed
- 1 green chilli, slit
- A few curry leaves and a little chopped coriander
- Optional tempering: ½ teaspoon mustard seeds and a pinch of asafoetida in a spoon of oil

**Method**

1. Whisk the curd until smooth, then whisk in the water and salt.
2. Add the ginger, chilli, curry leaves and coriander. Let it sit ten minutes so the flavours move into the liquid.
3. Pour over the tempering if using, and strain before serving if you prefer it clear.

On Ram Navami, these two drinks usually come with vada paruppu: soaked moong dal drained and tossed with grated coconut, diced cucumber, a little green chilli, salt and lemon. No cooking there either.

## Why are these offered on Ram Navami?

Ram Navami celebrates the birth of Rama on the ninth day of the waxing moon in the month of Chaitra, usually March or April. Across much of India this is the start of real heat, and tradition treats both panakam and neer mor as cooling drinks for the season. In temples and homes, they are poured out to anyone who comes, and in some towns volunteers stand on street corners handing out cups.

Panakam has an older fame too. At the Panakala Narasimha temple in Mangalagiri, panakam itself is the offering, poured for the deity in quantity. If you want the fuller shape of the festival day, [Ram Navami puja at home](/blog/ram-navami-puja-at-home) walks through it, and [Rama as maryada purushottama](/blog/rama-maryada-purushottama) explains the ideal of a person who keeps to what is right.

Offer each drink in a small vessel before the lamp, then pour it out for the household and whoever visits.

## One jug of each, and an open door

Try this on the next hot evening, festival or not. Make one jug of panakam and one of neer mor. Offer a cup of each at the lamp, sit for a minute, and then hand out the rest to whoever walks in.

Notice how people respond to being handed a cold drink without asking. That small act of hospitality is most of what the festival is doing. The same no-fuss spirit runs through [sweet aval for Krishna Jayanthi](/blog/aval-for-krishna-janmashtami) and the crisp [seedai made for Gokulashtami](/blog/seedai-for-gokulashtami), when the next festival comes round.
