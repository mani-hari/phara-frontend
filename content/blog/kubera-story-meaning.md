Kubera is called the god of wealth, but in most stories he does not own the treasure. He guards it. The Kubera story, as the epics and Puranas tell it, is about a yaksha king who lost a golden city to his own brother, became treasurer of the gods, and was once humbled by a small, very hungry child. Read together, the tales draw a clear line between having wealth and keeping it, and that line is the whole point.

## The Kubera story in the epics

Who is Kubera? His family name is Vaishravana, son of the sage Vishrava, and he is lord of the yakshas, the half-divine spirits who live in forests, mountains and hidden places where treasure lies. In the Ramayana and in the Puranas that retell it, Kubera wins Brahma's favour through long austerity and is given riches, a place among the guardians of the world, and a wonderful flying chariot, the Pushpaka.

He also rules Lanka, the golden city by the sea. Then his half-brother arrives. Ravana, son of the same father by a different mother, is ambitious and far stronger. He drives Kubera out, takes Lanka for himself, and seizes the Pushpaka. At the end of the Ramayana the same chariot changes hands again: after Ravana falls, it is the Pushpaka that carries Rama home to Ayodhya.

The detail is telling. The god of wealth could not hold on to his own city. The riches were real, but they passed to whoever had the power to take them. Wealth in these stories is never quite owned.

## Treasurer of the gods and guardian of the north

Kubera settled in Alaka, a city on the slopes near Kailasa, close to Shiva. There he became what later tradition calls the treasurer of the gods, the keeper of the nine treasures, the nava nidhi. He is one of the guardians of the directions, the dikpalas, and his direction is the north.

That is why many homes and shops honour the north for him. Cash boxes, account books and safes are traditionally placed facing that way, and some households keep a small image or yantra of Kubera there. At Diwali, especially on the evening of Dhanteras, Kubera is worshipped beside Lakshmi. The pairing makes sense once you notice the difference between them. Lakshmi is fortune itself, arriving and leaving as she chooses, which is [why Lakshmi is called restless](/blog/lakshmi-restless-goddess). Kubera is the steward who keeps what has arrived. A Kubera Lakshmi puja asks for both: that wealth should come, and that it should be kept wisely.

## What happened when Kubera fed Ganesha?

There is a folk story, told in many regions, that sharpens the point.

Kubera, proud of his riches, decided to give a feast so grand that everyone would see how much he had. He invited Shiva and Parvati. Shiva declined, but sent his young son Ganesha in his place.

Ganesha sat down and began to eat. He ate the first course and the second. He ate the sweets, then the savouries, then everything prepared for the other guests. The kitchens emptied. The storerooms emptied. The boy was still hungry. He began to eat the decorations, the furniture, the gold plates. Finally, the story says, he turned to Kubera himself.

Kubera ran to Kailasa and begged Shiva for help. Shiva smiled and handed him a small handful of roasted rice. Give him this, he said, with love. Kubera did. Ganesha ate it and was full at last.

The story of Kubera and Ganesha says the child ate the whole feast. The story is really about what feeds. Kubera's feast was a display, and a display can never be enough, because its purpose is not to satisfy but to impress. A handful of rice given without pride did what a kingdom's worth of food could not.

## Keeping, not having

Put the stories side by side and a pattern appears. Kubera loses Lanka because wealth goes to whoever holds power. He becomes truly himself only as treasurer, the keeper of goods that are not really his. And his one attempt to show off his riches is swallowed whole by a child.

That is an old idea in Hindu thought: wealth is held in trust. Valluvar says something similar in the Tirukkural, and [honest trade at Thatha's shop](/blog/honest-trade-thathas-shop) shows how it looked at an ordinary cloth counter. For those who pray about livelihood, there is also [a prayer for a job](/blog/prayer-for-a-job-hindu), and for families far from home, [Lakshmi puja abroad](/blog/lakshmi-puja-at-home-abroad).

Try thinking of one thing you own, a car, a savings account, a skill, as something you are keeping for a while rather than something you have. Notice how that changes how you use it. Kubera, the story suggests, was happiest when he remembered he was the guardian, not the owner.
