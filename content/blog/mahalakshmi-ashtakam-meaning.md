The Mahalakshmi Ashtakam opens with the words Namastestu Mahamaye, salutations to you, great Maya, the power that creates and enchants, and tradition puts those words in the mouth of Indra, king of the gods, who had lost his fortune through his own carelessness. The Mahalakshmi Ashtakam meaning is easiest to grasp once you know that. It is a hymn of eight verses sung by someone who had everything, threw it away without noticing, and is now learning how to ask.

## Why did Indra sing to Lakshmi?

The story is told in several Puranas, and it begins with a garland. The sage Durvasa gave Indra a garland from the gods' own trees, Indra let his elephant trample it, and Durvasa cursed him to lose Sri, prosperity itself; how completely she left is the story of [why Lakshmi is called the restless goddess](/blog/lakshmi-restless-goddess). She came back only when gods and demons together churned the ocean of milk, a long tale of its own in [the churning of the ocean and what it means](/blog/samudra-manthan-meaning), and rose from it seated on a lotus.

The Mahalakshmi Ashtakam story, as it is usually told, says the hymn comes from the Padma Purana, sung by Indra in gratitude and humility once Lakshmi had returned. Whether or not the attribution is historical, it tells you how to read the hymn. This is not a rich man praying for more. It is a man who lost everything, praying with the memory of loss still fresh.

## Mahalakshmi Ashtakam meaning, verse by verse

The eight verses are short, and each ends with the same line: Mahalakshmi namostute, salutations to you, Mahalakshmi.

The opening verses greet her as a warrior. She stands on the seat of Sri, worshipped by the gods, holding the conch, the discus and the mace. She rides Garuda, the eagle, and she is the terror of the demon Kolasura. The Lakshmi of the first verses is not only the gentle goddess on the lotus. She is armed.

The middle verses widen the picture. She knows everything and grants every boon. She removes all sorrow. She gives siddhi and buddhi, accomplishment and understanding, and also bhukti and mukti, the enjoyments of the world and liberation from it. The tradition does not force a choice between the two. She is without beginning or end, the first power, born of yoga and known through it. She is gross and subtle, fierce and vast.

The closing verses return to stillness. She is seated on the lotus, of the nature of the supreme Brahman, mother of the world. And finally, she is clothed in white, adorned with ornaments, sustaining the world. A goddess of wealth dressed in white is an interesting image. White is not the colour of display.

That is the arc of the hymn. It begins armed and ends in stillness.

## What does the final blessing promise?

Like many hymns, the ashtakam ends with a phala shruti, a passage describing the fruit of recitation. Read once a day, it says, the hymn destroys great wrongs; twice a day, it brings wealth and grain; three times a day, it overcomes great enemies, and Mahalakshmi remains pleased and gracious.

It is tempting to read this as a transaction: recite so many times, receive so much. I would read it differently. Remember who is singing. Indra's problem was never a lack of prayers. It was a lack of attention. He did not notice the garland. The phala shruti is a picture of steadiness returning to someone who has learned to notice, once a day, twice, three times, what he has and who gave it.

Fortune, in all of Lakshmi's stories, stays where it is honoured, and honouring begins with noticing.

## Honouring what you already have

Indra did not win Lakshmi back by force. He could not. He had to wait, work alongside his rivals at the churning, and learn to honour what he had been handed. The hymn he sang afterwards is gratitude with a long memory.

If you would like to try it, read one verse each Friday evening with its meaning, the day many households keep for Lakshmi. If you want a setting for it, [a simple Friday Lakshmi pooja](/blog/friday-lakshmi-pooja) is enough. Before you begin, think of one garland you have been handed and laid down without looking. Then say the first line.
