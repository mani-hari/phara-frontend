My son came home from school asking why his friend had a red and gold thread on his wrist. I started to say "his sister tied it" and then stopped, because the real Raksha Bandhan meaning is bigger than that. Raksha means protection and bandhan means a bond. The festival is a thread that asks for care and promises care back. Brothers and sisters are its best-known form, but the stories behind it involve a queen and a king, a woman and a friend, a goddess and a demon king. I told him the shorter version in the car. This is the longer one.

## What does Raksha Bandhan mean, beyond brothers and sisters?

Raksha Bandhan falls on the full moon of Shravana, usually in August, the same full moon that many South Indian families keep as [Avani Avittam, the renewal of the sacred thread](/blog/avani-avittam-meaning). It is a day of threads, in more than one sense.

In most North Indian homes, a sister ties a rakhi, a decorated thread, on her brother's right wrist, puts a tilak on his forehead, offers him a sweet, and he gives her a gift and a promise to look out for her. But families have always stretched it. Sisters tie rakhis on each other. Cousins, neighbours and friends who are "like a brother" receive them. In some places a rakhi is tied on a soldier, a teacher, even a tree. The shape is always the same: I ask you to look after me, and by tying this I tell you that I will look after you too.

In our Tamil home in Chennai we did not grow up with rakhis; the thread-tying we knew was the Karadaiyan Nombu thread and its sweet [adai offered to the goddess](/blog/karadaiyan-nombu-adai). Rakhis came to us later, through North Indian friends, and now arrive in our mailbox most years.

## Which stories sit behind the thread?

There are three I like.

The one most often cited as the earliest is in the Bhavishya Purana, where Krishna tells it to Yudhishthira. Indra is losing a war with the demons, and on the Shravana full moon his wife Indrani, also called Sachi, guided by Brihaspati, the gods' teacher, ties a protective thread, charged with mantras, on his wrist. He wins. Here the thread is a wife's prayer, not a sister's.

The best-loved is a popular telling from the Mahabharata world. Krishna cuts his finger, and Draupadi, without a thought, tears a strip from her sari to bind it. Krishna remembers. Years later, when she is dragged into the Kaurava court and they try to strip her, her sari does not end. A strip of cloth given freely comes back as endless cloth.

The third is about Lakshmi. Vishnu had gone to guard the gates of King Bali, who had won his devotion. Lakshmi, missing her husband, came to Bali's house disguised as a woman with no family, and on the Shravana full moon tied a thread on his wrist. Bali asked what gift she wanted. She asked for her husband back, and Bali, bound by the thread, let him go.

Notice that none of these is simply about siblings. Each is about a bond that someone chose to honour.

## Rakhi across oceans

For families like ours, Raksha Bandhan is mostly a postal event. Rakhis from India need to be mailed early, sometimes weeks early, and every year someone's arrives late. The time difference helps and hurts: India's evening is our morning, so a sister in Chennai, at her dinner table, can watch her brother in New Jersey tie on the rakhi she mailed, over his breakfast. Cousins in the same American city often meet on the nearest weekend instead.

When a colleague asks about the thread on your wrist, one sentence works: "It is a festival where siblings, or friends, tie a thread to promise to protect each other." Most people like that. The festival falls in a busy season, just before [Krishna Janmashtami at home](/blog/krishna-janmashtami-at-home) and then [Ganesh Chaturthi in the USA](/blog/ganesh-chaturthi-in-the-usa), so it is often a small thing done quickly. That is all right. It is meant to be small. Raksha Bandhan has an autumn cousin in [Bhai Dooj](/blog/bhai-dooj-meaning), at the end of Deepavali, and if your children want a part in either, [festival rituals for kids](/blog/festival-rituals-for-kids) has simple ideas.

## One small thing to carry into the week

The poet Rahim wrote a couplet I first heard from a Hindi teacher in school: do not snap the thread of love with a jerk, because once broken it will not join again, and if it does, there will be a knot. Rahiman dhaga prem ka, mat todo chatkaay.

This week, send a message to someone who has looked out for you, even if no thread is involved. A sister, a friend, a colleague who covered for you once. Say thank you plainly. Keep the thread whole before it needs a knot.
