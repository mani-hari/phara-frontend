In India, a village temple usually belongs to one deity. Murugan lives on this hill, Amman in that grove, Vishnu in the big temple by the river. In America, the same hall may hold Venkateswara, Shiva, Durga, Murugan, Ayyappa, the Navagrahas and Sai Baba, each in a shrine along the wall. Ask why American Hindu temples have many deities and you get a simple answer and a deeper one. The simple answer is that many communities built them together. The deeper one is that the tradition had already made room for this long ago.

## Why American Hindu temples have many deities

Picture a suburb some decades back. A few hundred Tamil families, a few hundred Telugu, some Gujarati, Punjabi, Bengali and Malayali households, all wanting a temple within driving distance. No single group could raise the money or the congregation for a temple of its own. So they pooled.

And each group brought its ishta devata, the chosen form of God a family feels closest to. The Telugu families wanted Venkateswara, the Lord of Tirupati. The Tamil families wanted Murugan and Shiva. The Malayalis wanted Ayyappa. The Bengalis wanted Durga. Someone's grandmother insisted on the Navagrahas, the nine planetary deities, because what is a temple without somewhere to pray on a difficult Saturday? Someone else asked for Sai Baba.

So the temple became a map of the congregation. Walk around the hall and you are reading the migration history of a town: who came, from where, and what they could not leave behind. Every family seems to have one favourite among them, which is [what an ishta devata is](/blog/ishta-devata-meaning).

## Is this new?

Less new than it looks. In the eighth century, Adi Shankara is credited with popularising panchayatana puja, the worship of five deities together: Shiva, Vishnu, Devi, Surya and Ganesha, with one placed at the centre according to the worshipper's preference. Families across South India still keep five small stones or images on the home altar in this arrangement. The idea that one household might honour several forms of God at once is old.

Older still is a line from the Rig Veda: ekam sat vipra bahudha vadanti, "truth is one; the wise call it by many names." The line does not say there are many truths. It says there is one, and that the names multiply because people do. That idea is explored further in [many paths, one truth](/blog/many-paths-one-truth).

And the Shiva-Vishnu temple, so common in America, has precedents too. Temples like Sankaranarayana in Tamil Nadu honour a form that is half Shiva and half Vishnu in one image. The story says the gods argued about who was greater. The story is really about how silly the argument was.

So the American temple is not a compromise. It is an old idea in a new place, with the parking lot added.

## How to move through a many-shrine temple

This is where newcomers get anxious. Twenty shrines. Where to start? Must you visit all of them?

The usual order is simple. Ganesha first, because he is the lord of beginnings. Then the main deity of the temple, whose shrine is usually at the centre or the far end. After that, go to whichever others you feel drawn to. Walking clockwise around a shrine is traditional, and the idea behind [pradakshina, walking around the deity](/blog/pradakshina-meaning), is a lovely one.

Nobody needs to visit every shrine. You will see some people do a full round every week, and others who greet Ganesha and the Navagrahas and go straight to the canteen. Both are praying in their own way. If you are taking a friend for the first time, the practical guide to [a first visit to a Hindu temple in the US](/blog/first-visit-hindu-temple-usa) covers the rest.

## Not confusion but hospitality

Many shrines under one roof can look like confusion, especially to a visiting friend who asks which one is the real God. It is not confusion. It is hospitality. The Tamil family made room for the Telugu God, and the Telugu family made room for the Malayali one, and the grandmother got her Navagrahas.

Try this. Each time you visit, pick one deity whose story you do not know. Greet that one, and look up their story when you get home. In a year you will know the whole hall, and the town that built it.
