Near the end of chapter 2 of the Bhagavad Gita, Arjuna asks a surprisingly practical question. He does not ask what wisdom is. He asks what a wise person looks like: how does someone of steady wisdom speak, how do they sit, how do they walk? Krishna's answer is a portrait, and the sthitaprajna meaning becomes clearest when you treat that portrait not as a statue to admire but as a set of habits you can try on, one at a time.

## Sthitaprajna meaning

Sthita means steady, established, standing firm. Prajna means wisdom or insight, the kind of understanding that sees things as they are. A sthitaprajna is a person whose insight is established, who does not lose it when life gets loud.

The description comes in the closing verses of chapter 2, Sankhya Yoga, often called the sthitaprajna lakshana, the marks of the steady one. They are among the most loved verses in the Gita, and some traditions recite them daily. They answer Arjuna's question exactly: this is how such a person behaves, not only what they believe.

The marks are quite concrete. They are not shaken by sorrow and do not crave pleasure. They are free from attachment, fear and anger. They are content in themselves. They meet good and bad without elation or hatred.

## The tortoise and the ocean

Krishna gives two images, and both are worth sitting with.

The first is the tortoise. When a tortoise senses danger, it draws its limbs into its shell. In the same way, the steady person can withdraw the senses from their objects. Notice what this is not. It is not refusing to see or hear. The tortoise still has legs; it walks around most of the day. It is the capacity to pull back when needed, rather than being dragged wherever the senses go.

The second is the ocean. Rivers pour into it all the time, and yet the ocean stays full and does not overflow or churn. Desires enter the steady person the same way. They arrive; they are not refused. But they do not disturb the depth. The ocean does not need the rivers to stop in order to be at peace.

Put these two together and you have a fairly exact picture of equanimity in the Gita: the ability to withdraw attention when you choose, and a depth that absorbs what comes in without being overturned.

## Can an ordinary person become steady?

Many people read these verses and feel defeated. That is not me, they think. I get angry in traffic. I crave things constantly.

It helps to read the same chapter a few verses further. Krishna describes how steadiness is lost, and the description is almost clinical. When you dwell on an object of the senses, attachment arises. From attachment comes desire. From desire, when blocked, comes anger. From anger comes confusion, from confusion the loss of memory, from that the loss of discernment, and from that, ruin.

That chain is the key. It means steadiness is not a personality type you either have or lack. It is a trained capacity, and the training happens at the start of the chain. The earlier you notice yourself dwelling on something, the easier it is to let it go. By the time you reach anger, the momentum is huge. At the first moment of dwelling, a single breath can interrupt it.

This is why some people find it useful before high-stakes moments, like a [prayer before an interview](/blog/prayer-before-an-interview), to take a minute to withdraw like the tortoise. [Dharma in everyday life](/blog/dharma-meaning-in-everyday-life) is another good companion: steadiness is what makes right action possible when you are under pressure.

## Try this for a week

Once a day, when something pulls at you, catch it. It might be a notification, a craving for something sweet, a comment that stings. At that moment, do what the tortoise does. Withdraw your attention for ten breaths. Do not act, do not reply, do not reach. Just breathe and let the pull be there.

After ten breaths, decide. You may still reply, or eat the sweet, or open the message. That is fine. The practice is not refusal. It is inserting a gap between the pull and the action. Do this for seven days and notice how the gap starts to widen on its own.
