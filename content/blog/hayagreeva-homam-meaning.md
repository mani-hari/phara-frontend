A horse's head on a god's body sounds strange until you notice where the tradition places it: at the beginning of learning. A horse is speed, strength and, above all, steady breath over a long road. Those are exactly what a mind needs when it sets out to know something. The Hayagreeva homam, the fire ritual to this horse-headed form of Vishnu, is performed for students, teachers and scholars, and its story explains why.

## What happens in a Hayagreeva homam?

A homam is a fire ritual, offerings made into a consecrated fire while mantras are chanted. There is a separate piece on [what a homam is](/blog/what-is-a-homam) and how the fire works as a messenger.

The Hayagreeva homam procedure follows the familiar outline.

1. **Sankalpam.** The priest states the intention, with the devotee's name and birth star.
2. **Ganapati first,** so the rite begins without obstacle.
3. **The kalasam.** A pot of water is set up for Lakshmi Hayagreeva, the god with the Goddess seated on his lap.
4. **The mantras.** The Hayagreeva mantra is chanted, along with verses from the Hayagreeva Stotram of Vedanta Desika, the Sri Vaishnava poet-philosopher. Its first verse is widely known: "jnanananda mayam devam, nirmala sphatikakritim, adharam sarva vidyanam, hayagrivam upasmahe", we worship Hayagreeva, made of knowledge and bliss, clear as spotless crystal, the ground of all learning.
5. **The offerings.** Ghee, cardamom-scented sweets and white flowers go into the fire.
6. **Purnahuti,** the final full offering.

## Why is Hayagreeva white?

The story comes in several versions in the Puranas and the Mahabharata. The best known goes like this.

At the end of a cosmic age, Brahma, the creator, was resting, and the Vedas lay beside him. Two asuras, Madhu and Kaitabha, crept in and stole them, and carried them down into the depths of the ocean. Without the Vedas, Brahma could not create. Knowledge itself had gone missing from the world.

Vishnu took a new form to go after them: a body like a man's, and the head of a horse. He dived into the waters, found the asuras, recovered the Vedas and returned them to Brahma. Then he chanted them aloud, in the Sama Veda's melodies, so that creation could begin again.

The images that follow are all about clarity. Hayagreeva sits on a white lotus, dressed in white, as clear as crystal. In his hands he holds a book and a rosary; one hand is raised in blessing or in the gesture of teaching. Lakshmi sits beside him.

The story says a god recovered stolen books. The story is really about how knowledge arrives. The horse's head is the breath, the snort and neigh that carry sound. The Vedas were never written down in the old way of learning; they lived in breath, passed from teacher to student by voice. Hayagreeva is that breath made visible. The whiteness is what the mind looks like when it is ready to receive: not blank, but clear.

## Who performs the Hayagreeva homam, and when?

Traditionally, students before exams, teachers at the start of a term, and scholars before a long work. Sri Vaishnava families in particular hold Hayagreeva close, and many children in those homes learn the first verse of the stotram before they learn their times tables.

The great day is Hayagreeva Jayanti, which falls around the full moon of Shravana, the same day as [Avani Avittam](/blog/avani-avittam-meaning), when the sacred thread is renewed and Vedic study symbolically begins again. The pairing makes sense. On the day learning is restarted, you honour the god who restarted it.

Families sometimes ask whether to choose Hayagreeva or Saraswati. [The Saraswati homam for students](/blog/saraswati-homam-for-students) sets the two side by side, and [Saraswati and Hayagriva](/blog/saraswati-and-hayagriva) asks why the tradition keeps two patrons of learning at all. Tradition, more than any rule, usually decides.

And for those drawn to the fiercer end of the homam tradition, [the Chandi homam](/blog/chandi-homam-meaning) is its opposite in mood: seven hundred verses of battle rather than one quiet verse of crystal.

## Learning as worship

Underneath all this, learning in this tradition is not separate from worship. It is a form of it. The student bent over a textbook and the priest chanting at the fire are doing related things: giving attention, breath by breath, to something larger than themselves.

So the smallest form of the Hayagreeva homam needs no fire. Before you sit down to study, or to teach, or to read something difficult, light a lamp. Say Hayagreeva's name once. Take one slow breath, the way a horse does before a long run. Then begin.
