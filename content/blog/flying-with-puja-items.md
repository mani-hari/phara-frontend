The suitcase home from India always carries a small temple. Ghee from my mother in a steel tin, kumkum and turmeric from an aunt, a packet of vibhuti from a temple in Madurai, a box of laddus from wherever we stopped on the way to the airport. Each item travels differently. Carrying puja items on a flight is mostly a matter of knowing which are liquids, which are powders, which are food, and which are better bought after you land.

## Liquids and powders

Ghee and oils are usually treated as liquids or gels by airport security, so carry-on liquid limits apply. Ghee is soft at room temperature and turns liquid in a warm car; security officers do not care that it looked solid in Chennai. The simplest answer is to pack ghee and oils in sealed tubs, inside a zip bag, inside checked baggage, wrapped in clothes. My mother's steel tin now travels with a layer of cling film under the lid and a rubber band around it, after one memorable suitcase.

Kumkum, turmeric and vibhuti are powders, and powders in carry-on bags may get extra screening in the US. They are usually allowed, but they may be opened. Pack them sealed, in checked bags, and label them in English. Before you fly, check current guidance from the TSA and from your airline, because rules change, and the rules for leaving India may differ from those for arriving in the US.

## Can you carry coconut and camphor on a plane?

Dry coconut, the hard kernel, travels well in checked baggage. A fresh coconut with water inside is heavy, may leak, and may be questioned as a plant item at customs. For most poojas, a coconut from the Indian store near you works exactly as well; the gods have not complained.

Camphor is the one that surprises people. It is flammable, and airlines and security agencies often treat it as a hazardous item, so it is frequently refused or confiscated. Buy camphor locally. Brass lamps are fine to carry, but they are heavy, and most Indian stores in the US sell them. If you are deciding what to burn at home, [ghee lamps and oil lamps](/blog/ghee-lamp-or-oil-lamp) compares the two.

Carrying a loved one's ashes is a different matter altogether, with its own papers and care, and it has its own guide to [immersing ashes in India from abroad](/blog/immersing-ashes-from-abroad).

## Customs, prasadam and declaring food

When entering the US, declare food: sweets, seeds, dried fruit, spices, and plant items such as tulsi leaves or flowers. Declaring does not mean it will be taken away; many packaged sweets are allowed through. Not declaring is what causes trouble. Keep laddus and other prasadam sealed, in their original box if possible, with an English label. Ask for current customs guidance rather than trusting old stories from a cousin's trip.

Some items simply cannot come in, and some are not worth the worry. For those, it helps to know that [prasadam can be delivered to the USA](/blog/prasadam-delivery-to-the-usa) from temples in India. In the ninth chapter of the Gita, Krishna says a leaf, a flower, a fruit or a little water, offered with love, is accepted. What you carry matters less than how you offer it.

## One small thing to carry

Before your next trip, make a short list of what you can buy at the Indian store in Edison, or wherever you live: camphor, wicks, incense, kumkum, lamps, coconuts, even ghee. Then look at your suitcase and carry only what cannot be replaced. For me that is two things: my mother's ghee, because she made it, and a packet of vibhuti from our family temple, because of where it came from. Everything else is available here.
