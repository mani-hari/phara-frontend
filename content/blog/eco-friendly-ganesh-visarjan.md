Visarjan means letting go. You can do it in a bucket on your back porch and lose nothing of the meaning; you may even understand it better, because you watch the whole return. An eco friendly Ganesh visarjan has three parts: an image made of plain, unbaked clay, a short farewell puja, and a bucket of water that slowly takes the form back. The clay then goes into your garden. No lake, no river, no permit, and nothing left behind that the earth cannot absorb.

## Eco friendly Ganesh visarjan starts at the store

Most of the work happens before the festival begins, when you choose the image. Look for shadu, the natural river clay traditionally used in Maharashtra, or any unbaked clay without a hard glaze. Avoid plaster of Paris; it does not dissolve and it clouds water for a long time. Avoid oil paint and glitter for the same reason. An unpainted image, or one coloured with turmeric, kumkum or natural pigments, is closer to the old practice anyway.

Some makers now press a few seeds into the base, so the clay can go straight into a pot and sprout a basil or marigold plant. Children tend to love this.

And choose small. The smaller the image, the easier the goodbye, in every sense: it dissolves faster, it fits in a bucket, and there is less to mourn. If this is your first time bringing Ganesha home, start earlier in the festival with [keeping Ganesh Chaturthi in the USA](/blog/ganesh-chaturthi-in-the-usa).

## How do you do visarjan at home in a bucket?

The farewell is called the uttara puja, the concluding worship. It is short.

1. Light the lamp and do a last aarti, waving the flame before Ganesha while everyone sings or chants.
2. Offer akshata, rice grains mixed with turmeric, and a modak or any sweet.
3. Say a sankalpam, a spoken intention, asking Ganesha to forgive what was missed and to return when the festival comes round again. Simple words are fine: "Thank you for staying with us. Please come back."
4. Move the image gently, a sign that he is leaving, then carry him to a clean bucket of water, ideally outdoors.
5. Lower him slowly, all together, chanting "Ganapati Bappa Morya", the Marathi farewell chant.

Let everyone hold the image for a moment before it goes in. For children, this is often the part they remember for years; there are more ideas in [festival rituals for kids](/blog/festival-rituals-for-kids) and in [raising Hindu kids in America](/blog/raising-hindu-kids-in-america).

## What to do with the water and the clay

Leave the bucket where it can stay undisturbed for a day or two. Natural clay softens within hours and slumps into a soft mound; stir gently once or twice with your hand or a wooden spoon to help it along.

When it has fully dissolved, pour the water into plants or a garden bed. It is just water and earth. Keep a pinch of the clay if you like, and press it into the soil of a potted tulsi or a favourite plant, so something of the festival stays in the house. Flowers, durva grass and leaves from the puja go into the compost. Anything that will not break down, a cloth or a decoration, can be kept for next time or disposed of respectfully; the same thinking applies to [old idols and photos](/blog/old-idols-and-photos) you are unsure about.

## Try this: stay with the bucket

The late-summer festivals move fast, from [Raksha Bandhan](/blog/raksha-bandhan-meaning) to [the welcome of Onam](/blog/onam-mahabali-meaning) to this one, and it is easy to finish the immersion and walk straight back inside to the dishes.

Try this instead. After the image goes in, sit with the bucket for five minutes. Do not talk. Watch the edges blur, the trunk soften, the form lean and fold into the water. You may feel a small grief. Let it be there.

That is the practice. The festival spent ten days, or three, or one and a half, teaching you to love a form. Visarjan teaches you to let it go back to where it came from, without drama and without clinging. Most of life asks for that skill sooner or later. Here, at least, you get to rehearse it with a bucket and a little clay.
