Ask a Hindu whether they worship a stone and most will say no, gently. They worship God, and the image is the place where attention meets the divine. So why do Hindus worship idols, then? Partly because the word idol is the wrong word. In the tradition, the image is a murti, a form, a body lent to the formless so that human beings, who are creatures of form, have somewhere to look. The difference matters, and it is easier to understand through a story than an argument.

## Why do Hindus worship idols, or is that the wrong word?

Murti is a Sanskrit word meaning form, embodiment, a shape that something takes on. Murti does not mean "statue of a god". It is closer to "the form in which God agrees to be present here". A murti can be carved stone, cast bronze, painted wood, or something as humble as a cone of turmeric paste or a smooth river stone. What makes it a murti is not the material or the craftsmanship. It is what happens around it.

There is an old story told in many forms. A devotee asks a teacher, "Is God in that stone?" The teacher answers, "Is God anywhere that is not?" The story says the stone is holy. The story is really about the eye of the one who looks. If the divine is everywhere, then it is also here, in this particular shape, and giving it a particular shape is a kindness to the human mind, which cannot hold "everywhere" for long.

So is murti puja idol worship? From the outside it may look like it. From the inside, almost nobody thinks the stone itself is God. They think the stone is where God can be met.

## What makes an image sacred?

A murti in a shop is a sculpture. A murti in a temple has been through prana pratishtha, the consecration ceremony. Prana means life breath and pratishtha means establishing. Prana pratishtha, then, means the establishing of life: the ritual by which priests invite the divine presence to dwell in the image. The eyes are often the last part to be opened, sometimes with a golden needle, and the first thing the newly awakened deity sees is sometimes a mirror, or a cow, or the devotees.

After that, the murti is not treated as an object. It is treated as a guest who lives in the house. It is woken in the morning, bathed, dressed, decorated, fed, sung to and put to rest at night. These are the [sixteen steps of puja](/blog/sixteen-steps-of-puja), the same courtesies you would offer an honoured visitor. The care is not for the stone. It is a discipline of attention for the devotee.

## Formless and with form

Hindu thought has two words for this. Nirguna means without qualities: God beyond form, name and description, the infinite that no image can hold. Saguna means with qualities: God with a face, a story, a favourite food. The Upanishads lean toward nirguna. The temples, the festivals and most of the poetry lean toward saguna. Most Hindus live comfortably with both.

Think of a photograph of someone you love, perhaps a parent who has died. Nobody believes the photograph is the person. And yet nobody would tear it up, step on it or throw it in the bin. You might keep it on a shelf, dust it, put a flower beside it on their anniversary. The photograph is paper, and it is also not merely paper. The murti works in much the same way, with one difference: the devotee believes the one in the picture can actually look back.

This is why a single temple, especially in the US, may hold many deities side by side; [why US temples have many deities](/blog/why-us-temples-have-many-deities) explains how that came about. And the tradition itself argued about all of this, openly and at length; [debate as devotion](/blog/debate-as-devotion-shastrartha) shows how.

## What should a guest watch for?

If you visit a temple as a guest, do not look only at the image. Watch what the worshippers do. The woman who closes her eyes the moment she reaches the sanctum. The old man who touches the step before he climbs it. The child lifted up to see. The answer to the question is not in what the image looks like. It is in their attention.
