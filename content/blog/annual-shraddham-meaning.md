The annual shraddham is kept on the tithi, the lunar day, on which a person died. That is why its date wanders across the calendar from one year to the next, while the day of remembering stays fixed in the sky. Families abroad often find this confusing. It becomes less confusing once you see that the tradition is not tracking a date at all. It is tracking the Moon.

## Why is the annual shraddham kept on the lunar tithi?

The Moon is the old keeper of time in India. Months were counted from new moon to new moon, or from full moon to full moon, and each lunar month was divided into thirty tithis, fifteen in the waxing fortnight and fifteen in the waning one. Each fortnight is a paksha. When someone dies, the family notes three things: the month, the paksha and the tithi. The shraddham falls on that same combination every year.

Which month depends on the region. In many parts of India, the lunar month is used. In many Tamil homes, the month is the solar Tamil month, such as Aadi or Purattasi, while the tithi still comes from the Moon. That is why the family astrologer, or a panchangam, the traditional almanac, is consulted every year to find the day. The guide to [how to read a panchangam](/blog/how-to-read-a-panchangam) explains the tithi and how it is listed. If the tithi falls at an awkward hour, there are rules about which day to observe, and a priest or an elder usually settles it.

## The rice balls and the sand at Gaya

At the centre of a shraddham is the pinda, a ball of cooked rice mixed with sesame, sometimes with ghee or honey, offered to the one who died and to the generations before. Offering it is called pinda daan, the giving of the pinda.

There is a story told at Gaya, the town on the Phalgu river long associated with ancestral rites, and in several retellings of the Ramayana. Rama and Lakshmana went to fetch what was needed for their father Dasharatha's shraddham and were delayed. The hour for the offering came. Dasharatha's hands appeared, the story says, asking for food. Sita had nothing but the sand of the riverbank, so she shaped balls of sand and offered them, with the river, a cow, a ketaki flower and a banyan tree as her witnesses.

The story says the dead need food. The story is really about the living needing to feed someone. Grief leaves the hands empty, which is where most thinking about [grief in Hinduism](/blog/grief-in-hinduism) has to begin. A ritual that says make this, offer this, now, gives the hands something to do, and it does not wait until everything is perfect. Sand will do, if sand is what you have.

## What happens at an annual shraddham?

The family invites a priest, and in some homes elders or Brahmins who stand in for the ancestors and are fed. The karta, the one performing the rite, wears the sacred thread over the right shoulder, reversed, as is done for ancestral rites. A ring of darbha, a sacred grass, is worn on the finger. Black sesame and water are offered with the names of three generations: the one who died, their parent and their grandparent. The pindas are made and offered. A meal is cooked, often including the person's favourite dishes, and a portion is set out for crows, who in many families are seen as messengers to the ancestors.

The shraddham sits within a wider calendar of remembrance: [the new moon and the ancestors](/blog/amavasya-and-ancestors), the fortnight of [Pitru Paksha for families abroad](/blog/pitru-paksha-for-nri-families), and the first ceremonies after a death, including [the thirteenth day](/blog/thirteenth-day-ceremony-meaning).

## A dinner invitation

Families who cannot host a full rite often keep the day simply: a lamp lit, a meal cooked, a plate set aside, the names said aloud. That is not a lesser shraddham. It is the heart of one.

A shraddham is often described as a debt paid to the ancestors, and the language of debt is there in the texts. But it may be better understood as a dinner invitation. You find the day, you cook, you set the plate, you say the names. For an hour, the table has room for everyone who ever sat at it.
