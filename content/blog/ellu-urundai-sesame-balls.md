Ellu urundai asks for one thing: speed at the right moment. The syrup is ready, the sesame is warm, and you have about two minutes before it sets. This ellu urundai recipe, sesame and jaggery rolled into small balls by hand, is one of the simplest sweets in the Tamil kitchen, and one of the most traditional offerings for Saturdays. Try it once and you will find your whole attention in your hands.

## Ellu urundai recipe: sesame, jaggery, hands

**Ingredients (about twelve balls)**

- 100 g black or white sesame seeds (¾ cup)
- 100 g grated jaggery (½ cup)
- 2 tablespoons water
- ¼ teaspoon cardamom powder
- A little ghee or a bowl of water for your palms

**Method**

1. Dry-roast the sesame in a heavy pan on low heat, stirring constantly, until the seeds start to crackle and pop and smell nutty. Spread them on a plate to cool.
2. In the same pan, melt the jaggery with the water. Strain it if it is gritty, then return it to the heat.
3. Boil the syrup, stirring, until it reaches the soft-ball stage. Test it by dropping a little into a bowl of cold water: if it gathers into a soft ball you can roll between your fingers, it is ready.
4. Turn off the heat. Add cardamom and the roasted sesame and mix quickly.
5. Grease or wet your palms. While the mixture is still warm, take a spoonful and press it firmly into a ball. Work fast.
6. If the mixture sets before you finish, warm it on the stove for a few seconds.

Black sesame gives a deeper, slightly bitter flavour; white is milder and sweeter. Many families use black for Saturday offerings.

## Why is sesame linked with Shani?

In Jyotish, the tradition of Indian astrology, black sesame is associated with Shani, the planet Saturn, who governs time, discipline and delay. For that reason many families light sesame oil lamps on Saturdays and offer sesame sweets at Navagraha shrines, the altars to the nine planets. Families who take the offering further go to [worship at Navagraha temples](/blog/navagraha-temple-worship-guide), or to [Thirunallar, the Saniswaran temple](/blog/thirunallar-saniswaran-temple), Shani's own shrine.

Read this as custom, not as a remedy with a promised result. The tradition gives Saturday a texture, a flavour and a small act to perform. What you do with that is up to you. One useful reading: Shani governs discipline, so use the day to keep one discipline you have been putting off.

Tradition also treats sesame and jaggery as warming foods for the cold months, and ellu urundai turns up through Karthigai and Margazhi in many homes.

## How do you make ellu urundai as an offering, not a snack?

The ingredients do not change. Your attention does.

- **Cook with attention.** Stay at the stove for the syrup stage. It is the whole skill.
- **Do not taste** before offering. Test the syrup in water, not on your tongue.
- **Offer it on a Saturday** at a lamp, before Hanuman, or at a Navagraha shrine. Many people pair it with [the Hanuman Chalisa on Saturdays](/blog/hanuman-chalisa-on-saturdays).
- **Give it away.** Sharing is part of the custom. Sesame sweets are traditionally distributed rather than hoarded.

Karthigai Deepam and the Tamil festival months each have their own sweets, and the same principle runs through them, from [Varalakshmi Vratham naivedyam](/blog/varalakshmi-vratham-naivedyam) to the steamed [Karadaiyan Nombu adai](/blog/karadaiyan-nombu-adai).

## A dozen on Saturday morning

This week, make a dozen on Saturday morning. Light a small sesame oil lamp, offer three or four of the balls, and sit for a minute.

Then give most of them away: to a neighbour, a colleague, someone at the temple. Notice how it feels to make something with such concentrated care and then not keep it. That feeling is most of what the offering is for.
