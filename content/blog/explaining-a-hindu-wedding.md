Hindu wedding rituals explained in one breath: a prayer to Ganesha, the groom's arrival, the giving of the bride, vows made before a sacred fire, seven steps taken together, and the tying of a sacred necklace. Three days before my cousin's wedding, an American colleague stopped by my desk and asked, very quietly, "Is it rude to leave during the fire part?" She had been invited, she wanted to do it right, and nobody had told her anything. She deserved a simple guide. So do your guests.

## Hindu wedding rituals explained in order

Customs vary widely by region and family, but most Hindu weddings follow a recognisable arc.

**Ganesha puja.** The day begins with a prayer to Ganesha, the remover of obstacles, because every Hindu beginning starts with him; the reason is told in [why Ganesha comes first](/blog/why-ganesha-comes-first). Many families also hold a Ganapathy homam, a small fire offering, the day before.

**The arrival.** In North Indian weddings the groom arrives in a lively procession called the baraat, often with music and dancing. The couple may exchange flower garlands, the jaimala.

**Kanyadaan.** The bride's parents place her hand in the groom's. It is often the most emotional moment for the family.

**The sacred fire.** The couple sits before Agni, the fire, which is treated as the witness to the marriage. Offerings of ghee and grains are made while the priest chants. If your guests are curious about the fire itself, [what a homam is](/blog/what-is-a-homam) explains it simply.

**Saptapadi.** The seven steps, taken together around or beside the fire. In many traditions, this is the moment the marriage becomes complete.

**Mangalsutra and sindoor.** The groom ties a sacred necklace, called the thali in Tamil homes, and in many communities applies red sindoor to the parting of the bride's hair.

The timing of all this is set by a muhurtham, an auspicious window chosen by the family's astrologer; this piece on [what a muhurtham is](/blog/what-is-a-muhurtham) tells guests why the ceremony might start at an unusual hour.

## What should guests know before they come?

Dress brightly. Colour is welcome, and so is Indian clothing if a guest wants to try it. Many families gently avoid all-black and all-white outfits, which are associated with mourning in some communities. Shoulders covered is a safe choice.

Shoes come off near the mandap, the decorated canopy where the rituals happen, so slip-on footwear helps. Food is usually vegetarian, often plentiful, and sometimes eaten with the hands off a banana leaf. Nobody minds if a guest asks for a spoon.

And to my colleague's question: no, it is not rude. Ceremonies can run for hours. Guests chat quietly, step out for tea, and come back for the big moments. The rule of thumb is to be present for the garlands, the saptapadi and the tying of the thali, and to relax about the rest. ## Explaining the meaning, not just the steps

Steps without meaning feel like watching someone else's family photos. The meaning is what turns a watcher into a participant.

The fire is a witness: the couple is not making promises only to each other, but in front of something that cannot be fooled. The seven steps are often explained as seven vows, which in plain English sound something like this: we will nourish each other, grow strong together, prosper together, share joys and sorrows, raise a family with care, be companions through every season, and stay friends for life.

Regional variations are part of the charm. A Gujarati wedding might include the playful hast milap, the joining of hands. A Tamil wedding might include the kasi yatra, where the groom pretends to leave for a life of study until the bride's father persuades him to stay. At many Tamil Vaishnava weddings, the family sings Andal's "Varanam Ayiram", in which she dreams her own wedding: "A thousand elephants surrounded him as he came." It is a poem about longing, and at a wedding it sounds like arrival.
