The name Sri Kalahasti is a riddle in three words. Sri is the spider, kala the serpent, hasti the elephant. The Sri Kalahasti temple, on the banks of the Swarnamukhi river in Andhra Pradesh, is named after three devotees who were not human. It is also one of Shiva's five element shrines, the one where he is worshipped as wind, and the place where a hunter once offered his own eye. Every story told here asks the same question in a different voice: what does devotion look like when it does not know the rules?

## Why is the Sri Kalahasti temple called the wind lingam?

South Indian tradition counts five Pancha Bhuta sthalas, shrines to the five elements: earth at Kanchipuram, water at Thiruvanaikaval, fire at Tiruvannamalai, ether at Chidambaram, and wind at Kalahasti. Ether, at Chidambaram, is worshipped as [an empty space behind a curtain](/blog/chidambaram-nataraja-temple), and the fire shrine has its own festival in [watching the Tiruvannamalai Deepam from afar](/blog/tiruvannamalai-deepam-from-afar).

At Kalahasti the lingam is Vayu, the wind. Tradition points to a lamp in the sanctum whose flame flickers although no draught can reach it, as if the god were breathing. You can take that as a sign or as a poem. Either way the image is right. Wind is the element you cannot see, only its effects: the flame bending, the leaf turning. A god worshipped as wind is a god you know by what moves when he is near.

## The spider, the snake and the elephant

The story says three creatures worshipped the same lingam, each in its own way. The spider wove a web over it as a canopy, to keep off sun and dust. The snake laid a gem upon it, the most precious thing a serpent owns. The elephant carried river water in its trunk and washed the lingam clean each day.

In the common telling, things went wrong. The spider's web caught fire from the temple lamp, and in one version the spider died trying to save it; the details vary from telling to telling. The snake came and found its gem washed away and wet leaves in its place, and took that for defilement. The elephant came and found a gem sitting on its clean lingam, and took that for defilement too. They fought. The snake crawled into the elephant's trunk, and both died. Shiva granted all three release, and the temple took their names.

The story is about animals. The story is really about how devotion looks different in every body. A web, a jewel, a trunkful of water: each offering was perfect from the inside and an insult from the outside. The snake and the elephant never questioned the lingam. They only questioned each other. Shiva, in the story, does not take sides.

## What did Kannappa offer?

The Periya Puranam, the Tamil lives of the sixty-three Nayanmars, the Shaiva saints, tells of a hunter called Thinnan who came upon the lingam at Kalahasti in the forest. He knew no mantras. He brought the meat he had cooked and tasted to make sure it was good. He carried water in his mouth and spat it over the lingam as a bath. He tucked flowers in his hair and shook them down as an offering. The temple priest, arriving each morning, was horrified.

Then one day Thinnan saw the lingam's eye bleeding. He cut out his own eye and pressed it into place. When the other eye began to bleed, he put his foot on the spot so he would not lose it, and raised his arrow to his second eye. Shiva stopped his hand. Thinnan became Kannappa, the one who gave his eye, and the tradition counts him among the Nayanmars alongside priests and queens. Another of them, a woman who walked on her hands, has her own story in [the life of Karaikkal Ammaiyar](/blog/karaikkal-ammaiyar-story).

What the priest saw as pollution, Shiva received as love. That is the whole temple in one story.

## Kalahasti and the Rahu-Ketu pooja

Many devotees come to Kalahasti today for the Rahu-Ketu pooja, done for what Jyotish, traditional Indian astrology, calls Rahu-Ketu or sarpa dosha, an unfavourable placement of the shadow planets. [Rahu and Ketu dosha explained](/blog/rahu-ketu-dosha-explained) sets out what the tradition means by it. The serpent in the temple's name makes the link feel natural.

It is a tradition held with faith, not a promise. If you cannot travel, the pooja can be offered in your name from afar with a sankalpam, a spoken statement of your intention. Whatever you bring to it, bring it the way the spider or the hunter did: without worrying whether it looks correct.
