Ahimsa means non-harm, and it begins long before the dinner plate. I saw that one evening, though I did not have the word ready at the time. A spider had settled in the corner of our bathroom, and I reached for a shoe. My son, who is about eleven and has opinions, got there first with a paper cup and a postcard. He carried it out to the back step and let it go, and then looked at me as though I had nearly committed a crime. Maybe I had. The ahimsa meaning I grew up with was about food. His was about the shoe.

## Ahimsa meaning: more than not hitting

The word is a-himsa. Himsa is harm or injury. The prefix a- negates it. But the old commentators read it more finely: not only the absence of harm, but the absence of the wish to harm. You can avoid hitting someone and still spend the afternoon wishing them ill. By this reading, you have not practised ahimsa at all.

It is everywhere in the tradition. Patanjali's Yoga Sutras put it first among the yamas, the restraints that a practitioner begins with, before breath, before posture, before meditation. The Mahabharata has the phrase "ahimsa paramo dharmah", non-harm is the highest dharma. And Thiruvalluvar gave it a whole chapter of the Tirukkural, Innaa Seyyaamai, Not Doing Harm. One kural in that chapter says the best way to punish those who harm you is to shame them by doing them good and then forgetting both the harm and the good. I have never managed the forgetting part.

## Where does harm hide in an ordinary day?

When I started paying attention, I found very little harm at my dinner table and a great deal of it everywhere else.

It was in the email I sent at six in the evening, sharper than it needed to be, to a colleague who had missed a deadline because her mother was unwell. It was in the joke at a meeting that got a laugh and cost someone a little dignity. It was in my horn at a traffic light in Edison, a small burst of anger aimed at a stranger who was probably just tired. It was in the careless things I bought and threw away.

None of these is dramatic. That is the point. Ahimsa in daily life is less a dietary rule and more a filter on words and choices; [whether all Hindus are vegetarian](/blog/is-hinduism-vegetarian) is a separate and more regional question. The question is not "did I hurt anyone?" but "where did I add a little harm to the world today that I did not need to?" [Dharma in everyday life](/blog/dharma-meaning-in-everyday-life) comes at the same ground from the other side, asking what we owe rather than what we avoid.

## Is ahimsa the same as being a pushover?

This is the objection I hear most, usually from friends who work in hard industries. If I practise non-harm, do I just let people walk over me?

The tradition says no. Gandhi built a whole movement on non-violent resistance, which was not passive in the least. It asked people to refuse, publicly and at cost, without hating the people they refused. And the Bhagavad Gita, of all places, is set on a battlefield. Krishna tells Arjuna to fight. That is not a contradiction. The Gita distinguishes between harm done from hatred or greed and duty done to protect others. A parent who stops a child from running into traffic, roughly if necessary, is not violating ahimsa.

So non-violence in Hinduism is not the absence of strength. It is strength without cruelty. You can say no, set a boundary, even end a relationship, without wishing the other person harm. That is harder than either fighting or giving in. It is also one of the [many paths to the same truth](/blog/many-paths-one-truth) that the tradition keeps open.

## One small thing for the week

Pick one habitual harm, just one. It might be in speech: the sarcastic reply, the gossip at lunch. It might be in food: eating something you have been meaning to cut back on. It might be in hurry: the horn, the sigh, the rushing of a slow person in a queue.

For seven days, soften that one thing. Not eliminate it, soften it. Notice how often it comes up. You may find, as I did, that it arrives far more often than you thought. My son still reminds me about the spider. I have started keeping a paper cup in the bathroom. The old texts talk about ahimsa as the first step on a road that ends in [moksha, liberation](/blog/what-is-moksha). Mine, it turns out, started with a spider.
