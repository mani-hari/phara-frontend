Bhagavad Gita chapter 12 is the one I read in the hospital waiting room during my father's heart surgery. I had flown to Chennai with a small pocket Gita in my bag, meaning to read something serious. In the end I read only this chapter, over and over, for most of a long afternoon. It was short enough to finish between updates from the nurses, and kind enough to stay with when there was nothing else to do but wait. My father came through. The chapter stayed with me.

## What is Bhagavad Gita chapter 12 about?

Chapter 12 is called Bhakti Yoga, the yoga of devotion. It is only twenty verses, one of the shortest chapters in the Gita, and it comes right after the overwhelming chapter 11, in which Arjuna is shown Krishna's cosmic form and is terrified by it. After that vision, chapter 12 feels like someone opening a window.

It begins with Arjuna's question. Some people worship God with form, as a person they can love. Others meditate on the formless, the unmanifest, beyond all qualities. Which of these, Arjuna asks, knows yoga best?

Krishna's answer is generous. He says those who fix their minds on him with faith and devotion are the most steadfast. He also says that those who meditate on the formless reach him too. But the path of the unmanifest, he adds, is harder for embodied beings. Devotion to the personal is the easier road. Bhakti yoga in the Bhagavad Gita is presented not as the only way, but as the one most of us can actually walk.

I found that reassuring in the hospital. I was not in a state for meditating on the formless. I wanted someone to talk to.

## The ladder: if you cannot, then

The part of chapter 12 I love most is what I think of as the ladder. In a few verses, Krishna offers Arjuna a series of steps, each one easier than the last.

Fix your mind on me, he says. If you cannot do that, then practise, return again and again. If you cannot practise, then do your work for my sake. If you cannot even do that, then give up the fruits of your actions.

There is no scolding at any rung. Each "if you cannot" is followed by another open door. It is a teaching that meets people where they are: the scholar and the tired son in the waiting room both have a step that fits. The last rung, giving up the fruits of action, is the same idea as [the Gita's teaching on work without attachment](/blog/gita-on-work-without-attachment), met here from the side of devotion rather than duty.

## The devotee who is dear to Krishna

The closing verses of the chapter describe the person Krishna calls dear to him. Read them slowly and you notice something: there is not a single ritual in the list.

The dear devotee is without hatred toward any being. Friendly and compassionate. Free from possessiveness and ego. The same in pleasure and pain, and forgiving. The same toward friend and foe, in honour and dishonour, in heat and cold, in praise and blame. Silent when silence is right, content with whatever comes, not attached to a particular home.

These are the qualities of a devotee in the Gita: qualities, not rituals. Nothing about the right flowers or the correct number of rounds. Those things have their place, and I still love the lamp and the morning prayer. But chapter 12 is clear about what they are meant to produce.

When I look at that list, I recognise it. My mother, in her kitchen in Chennai, has most of it. I have maybe two items on a good day. If you are new to the text, [where to start with the Bhagavad Gita](/blog/bhagavad-gita-for-beginners) puts this chapter near the top of the list. For days when the waiting room feeling returns, [rituals for anxious days](/blog/rituals-for-anxious-days) may help. And if you want a practice that uses the body as well as the heart, [surya namaskar as a greeting](/blog/surya-namaskar-meaning) is a good morning companion to the chapter.

## One small thing for the week

Pick one quality from the closing verses of chapter 12. Just one. Friendliness, perhaps, or being the same in praise and blame, or forgiveness.

Then practise it on the most difficult person you meet this week. Not the easy people. The colleague who interrupts, the relative who criticises, the stranger who cuts in line. See whether you can hold that one quality, for one conversation. You will probably fail a few times. The ladder says that is fine. If you cannot do it for a whole conversation, do it for a sentence. There is always a lower rung.
