The meeting went sideways in the first ten minutes, on a Monday, and an old couplet came to mind before I could stop it. The Thirukkural on speech says that choosing a harsh word when a kind one is available is like reaching for unripe fruit when ripe fruit hangs within reach. Both are fruit. Both come from the same tree. One is simply better to eat. I have been thinking about it since.

## Iniyavai Kooral (Pleasant Words): what the chapter asks

In the Tirukkural, the Tamil book of ethics in two-line couplets, the chapter on speech is called Iniyavai Kooral, speaking pleasant words. Valluvar places it in the book of virtue, right after the chapter on hospitality. That order is not an accident. You welcome a guest with a warm face first, and then you open your mouth, and the words should continue the welcome.

The chapter begins with a definition. Kural 91 reads:

*Insolaal eeram alaiip padiruilavaam*\
*semborul kandaar vaaichchol.*

Sweet words, it says, are words soaked in love, free of deceit, spoken by those who have seen what is true. Notice the three parts. Love, honesty and truth. Sweetness here is not sugar sprinkled on top; it is what speech tastes like when those three are present. Other couplets add the cheerful face, the welcoming look that comes before the word, and kural 92 says a sweet word spoken with a smiling face is better even than a gift given with a glad heart.

And then, at the end of the chapter, kural 100:

*Iniya ulavaaga innaadha kooral*\
*kani iruppak kaai kavarndhatru.*

To speak harshly when pleasant words are at hand is like plucking the raw fruit while the ripe one is there.

## Thirukkural on speech in a meeting room

The Monday meeting was about a project that had slipped by two weeks. Everybody knew why; a supplier had let us down, and one of our team had not flagged it early enough. When our director asked what happened, that colleague went quiet, and then took the blame for the whole thing, including the parts that were not his.

I had an email half-written in my drafts that morning. It was accurate. Every sentence in it was true. It was also unripe fruit: hard, sour, and designed to make sure everyone knew it was not my fault. I did not send it. Instead, after the meeting, I walked over to his desk and said, "The supplier story is on me to explain upstairs. Let's fix the schedule together." Same facts. Different fruit.

What struck me later is that Valluvar never asks us to change the truth. He asks us to pick the ripe version of it. Most hard news has one. Words carry weight long after the meeting ends; the idea of [karma, explained simply](/blog/karma-explained-simply), is partly just this, that what we put into the world comes back around the office kitchen by Friday.

## Does kind speech mean never saying hard things?

No, and I think this is where people misread the chapter. Kural 91 pairs sweetness with truth. A kind lie is not sweet speech in Valluvar's sense; it fails the honesty test. The chapter is about how we say true things, not whether we say them.

Honest feedback can be ripe or raw. The difference is usually timing, tone and intent. Is this the right moment, or am I venting? Is my voice warm, or am I performing? Am I trying to help this person, or to be seen as right? The same sentence can pass or fail on those three questions. Friends know this instinctively, which is why the [Tirukkural on friendship](/blog/tirukkural-on-friendship) reads almost like a sequel: the friend is the one who tells you the hard thing in a way you can bear.

[Bringing Shakti into daily practice](/blog/shakti-in-daily-practice) makes a related point from another direction: the power in us is best used with care. And the words that cost us nothing, a plain thank you at the right moment, are the subject of the [Tirukkural on gratitude](/blog/tirukkural-on-gratitude).

## Rewrite the first sentence

Before you send one difficult message this week, an email, a text, a note to a family member, rewrite only the first sentence. Make it a sentence that would be easy to hear. Leave the rest of the message as it is, truth intact. Then send it and see whether the reply comes back differently. The fruit is still the same fruit. You just waited for it to ripen.
