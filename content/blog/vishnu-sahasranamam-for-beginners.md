My father chants the Vishnu Sahasranamam on our video calls. He does not announce it. If I call on a Saturday morning his time, he is often halfway through, and he simply keeps going while I wait, the phone propped against the brass lamp. For years I knew only the rhythm. When I finally tried to learn it as an adult, on a New Jersey commute, I learned one useful thing about the Vishnu Sahasranamam for beginners: you need small pieces, not the whole mountain.

## Where does the Vishnu Sahasranamam come from?

The hymn comes from the Anushasana Parva, the book of instruction, in the Mahabharata. The war is over. Bhishma, the old grandsire, lies on a bed of arrows, waiting for the right moment to die. Yudhishthira, now king and full of grief, comes to him with questions. Among them: who is the one god, what is the highest refuge, whose name should a person hold on to to cross the troubles of life?

Bhishma answers with a thousand names of Vishnu. Each name is a quality: the one who pervades everything, the one who sustains, the one who is the shelter. Centuries later Adi Shankara wrote a commentary on the hymn, explaining the names one by one, and it has been chanted in homes and temples ever since.

I find the setting moving. The hymn is taught by a dying man to a grieving one. It was never meant for people with easy lives.

## Vishnu Sahasranamam for beginners: a gentle plan

This is the plan that worked for me, after two false starts.

1. **Listen for a week.** Play a clear, unhurried recording every day. Do not try to follow a text. Let your ear learn where the breaths fall.
2. **Learn the opening verses.** The hymn begins with a short prelude before the names start. Learn a few lines of it with a transliteration in front of you, and chant along with the recording.
3. **Add a few verses each week.** Not more. Keep chanting what you have already learned from the start, then add the new lines.
4. **Keep the recording running.** Chanting along with a steady voice carries you through the parts you have not learned yet, and stops you rushing.

[Chanting for beginners](/blog/chanting-for-beginners) has more on building the habit, and if counting helps you focus, [how to use a japa mala](/blog/how-to-use-a-japa-mala) explains the beads.

## Fitting it into an American week

Here is how it fits into a week that has school runs and a Monday standup in it. On the train in, earphones on, I listen and chant under my breath. When I drove instead, I only listened; a hymn is not worth an accident, and nobody expects you to read a transliteration at a traffic light. On weekdays, a few verses are enough.

On Saturdays or Thursdays, both traditionally linked with Vishnu, I try the full recitation at home. It takes about half an hour at a comfortable pace. Some weeks I manage it. Some weeks I manage the first part, and I have stopped treating that as failure. A partial recitation said with attention is a real practice, not a failed attempt at the full hymn.

The best version, though, is the one my father and I sometimes do on a call: he chants, I follow a line behind. It is the closest thing we have to sitting in the same room. My son gets the same on Sundays with the Suprabhatam, which I wrote about in [grandparents on video calls](/blog/grandparents-on-video-calls). If your parents chant, consider [recording their chants](/blog/recording-elders-chants) while you can. I wish I had started sooner.

If you are drawn to the Goddess's thousand names as well, [the meaning of the Lalitha Sahasranamam](/blog/lalitha-sahasranamam-meaning) is its natural companion.

## One small thing for this week

The first name in the thousand is Vishvam, the universe. Bhishma begins not with a god in a temple but with everything that exists.

One small thing to carry into this week: learn that one word. Say it once at the start of every commute, before the email, before the news. Vishvam. It is a good way to remember that the train, the traffic, and the whole anxious morning are also part of what you are praying to.
