A somvar vrat, the Monday fast for Shiva, can be as simple as this: one light meal in the day, one lamp at dusk, and a few minutes of Om Namah Shivaya before you eat. That is the whole thing. Try it for four Mondays in a row and pay attention, not to the scale, but to what hunger teaches you about attention. Most people find that the fast is less about food than about noticing how often the mind reaches for something to fill a gap.

## Why is Monday Shiva's day?

Monday is Somavara, the day of Soma, the Moon. In the stories, Shiva wears the crescent moon in his hair, which is why he is called Chandrashekhara, the one crowned with the moon. So the Moon's day became Shiva's day. [Chandra and the mind](/blog/chandra-graha-worship) looks at why the tradition links the Moon with our moods.

Different regions keep Mondays with different intensity. In the north, the Mondays of the month of Shravan, during the monsoon, are especially important, and many people fast on each of them. In Tamil Nadu, the Mondays of the month of Karthigai, Karthigai Somavaram, are kept with special abhishekam at Shiva temples. And there is the solah somvar tradition, a vow of sixteen consecutive Mondays, which many young women and men keep with a particular prayer in mind.

You do not need to wait for a special month. Any Monday will do.

## Somvar vrat, step by step

Here is a simple version you can keep on a working day.

1. **Morning.** Bathe, and before you start the day, make a sankalpam, the spoken statement of intention. It can be one sentence: "Today I will keep this fast for Shiva, with attention." Say it quietly, even if no one else hears.
2. **Through the day.** Choose your level: fruit and milk only, one simple meal without grains, or a single evening meal after the pooja. Water is fine throughout. Many people eat sabudana, fruit, yogurt or milk. Salt is often limited to rock salt.
3. **Evening.** Light a lamp. Pour a little water or milk over a Shiva lingam if you have one, or offer it before a picture. Offer a few bilva leaves if you can find them; any fresh leaf or flower is fine if you cannot. Chant Om Namah Shivaya eleven or one hundred and eight times. Knowing [the meaning of Om Namah Shivaya](/blog/om-namah-shivaya-meaning) keeps the count from turning mechanical.
4. **Break the fast.** After the pooja, eat simply. Something warm, something light.

If a Monday falls on a pradosham, the twilight hour of the thirteenth lunar day, many devotees treat it as especially auspicious; see [pradosham's meaning](/blog/pradosham-meaning).

## How strict does the fast need to be?

Much less strict than people fear. The tradition offers levels, and the right one is the one your body can hold without strain.

- **Fruit and milk** through the day, one meal at night.
- **One meal**, taken at midday or evening, with no snacking before or after.
- **Simply no snacking.** Three ordinary meals, nothing between them. This is a real fast for most people, and a revealing one.

If you work at a desk, the one-meal or no-snacking versions usually fit best; there is a practical guide on [fasting with a desk job](/blog/fasting-with-a-desk-job).

Please skip fasting altogether if you are pregnant, diabetic, on regular medication, recovering from illness, or simply unwell, and talk to your doctor before starting any fast. The tradition is clear that a vrat is a discipline for the mind, not a punishment for the body. You can keep the lamp and the chant without the fast, and that is a complete practice too.

## Four Mondays

Try this for four Mondays: one simple meal, one lamp at dusk, five minutes of Om Namah Shivaya.

Watch what happens around eleven in the morning, when the usual snack does not come. Notice the small restlessness, the thought that you deserve something, the way the hand drifts toward the kitchen. Do not fight it. Just see it. That seeing is the practice. By the fourth Monday, many people find the restlessness arrives later and leaves sooner, and the evening lamp feels less like a task and more like a place to arrive.

Notice the day, not the scale.
