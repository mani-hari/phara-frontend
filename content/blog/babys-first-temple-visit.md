Most families take a baby to the temple for the first time after the first month or so, often around forty days. The priest's blessing takes a minute. The rest is the family standing still together in front of the shrine, which new parents rarely get to do. If you are planning a baby's first temple visit in the US, the rituals are simple, and the planning is mostly about choosing a quiet hour.

## When is the right time for the first visit?

Many families wait out the confinement period, commonly around forty days, when mother and baby are meant to rest at home. Others go after the naming ceremony, which in many South Indian homes is held around the eleventh or twelfth day, which in America means planning [the namakaranam](/blog/namakaranam-in-america) around work leave and visiting grandparents. The Sanskrit texts give this first outing its own samskara, nishkramana, literally "going out", traditionally in the third or fourth month, when the child is carried outside to see the sun and then taken to a temple. The overview of [the sixteen samskaras](/blog/sixteen-samskaras-explained) shows it sitting between the naming and [the first rice](/blog/annaprashan-first-rice).

In practice, there is no single right day. Ask the elders, ask your doctor about outings, and pick what suits the baby. When our son was born, the two grandmothers had two opinions, and in the end the baby had the deciding vote by sleeping well one particular morning.

## What happens at the temple?

Not much, and that is the point. You give the priest the baby's name and birth star, and he performs an archana, a short recitation of the deity's names, in the child's name. He may touch a little vibhuti, the sacred ash, or kumkum to the baby's forehead; if the baby's skin is sensitive, you can ask him to place it on the parent instead. Often the baby is held up briefly before the shrine. Then prasadam, a slow walk around the sanctum, and home.

Some families make a particular journey. Couples who prayed at Garbharakshambigai, the goddess at Thirukarugavur near Thanjavur who is worshipped as the protector of the womb, often return with the baby to give thanks. [The story of the Garbarakshambigai temple](/blog/garbarakshambigai-temple-story) explains why. If you cannot travel, a thanksgiving archana at your local temple carries the same intention. In the months that follow, many Tamil families begin planning the first haircut and [the ear-piercing ceremony](/blog/karnavedha-ear-piercing), often for the same temple morning.

Andal, in the Tiruppavai, asks only to stand near and serve. I think of that line with new parents at a temple. They are not asking for much. They want to stand near, for a minute, with the child they waited for.

## A gentle visit at a busy US temple

US temples are busiest on weekend mornings, when everyone has the day off. For a newborn, a weekday morning is gentler: fewer people, less noise, a priest with time to talk. If you or your partner can take a morning off, take it.

A few small things helped us, and have helped friends since:

- **Call ahead.** Ask when the priest is free. The temple office will know, and the note on [finding a Hindu priest in the USA](/blog/finding-a-hindu-priest-usa) has more on what to ask.
- **Feed first.** Find a quiet corner to feed before you go in. Most temples have a side room or a bench away from the main hall.
- **Dress light.** Bring a thin cloth for the baby rather than a heavy blanket; temples can be warm with all the lamps.
- **Bring the grandparents.** Set up a video call. My father watched from Chennai on my brother's phone, holding it closer to his face than necessary, and recited a verse under his breath the whole time.

## One small thing

When the priest asks for the name and star, say it yourself. Full name, star, and gotra if your family uses one. It will feel strange the first time. It is the first time you will speak for your child at a temple, and you will do it many more times: at birthdays, before exams, on the morning they leave home. Start saying it clearly now.
