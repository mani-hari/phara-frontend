The Thirukkural on hard work contains a line I have now quoted at my own kitchen table: even when fate will not allow success, honest effort still pays the wages of the body's labour. My son needed to hear that the evening he did not make the school team. As it turned out, so did I.

## Aalvinaiyudaimai (Perseverance): what the chapter says

The chapter is called Aalvinaiyudaimai, which roughly means possessing manly effort, or in plainer modern English, perseverance. It sits in the second book of the Tirukkural, the book of wealth and public life, and it is ten couplets of steady encouragement. If you are new to the poet, start with [who Thiruvalluvar was](/blog/who-was-thiruvalluvar).

The chapter opens by telling us not to lose heart just because something is hard; effort itself brings greatness. Kural 616 draws the line sharply: effort creates prosperity, and the absence of effort brings in poverty. Across the chapter, Valluvar keeps saying the same thing in different ways. Sustained effort is a person's true wealth. Idleness, not failure, is the real misfortune.

And then comes the couplet I needed. Kural 619:

*Dheivaththaan aagaa theninum muyarchidhan*\
*meivaruththak kooli tharum.*

Even if, by fate, a thing cannot be achieved, effort will still pay the wages of the body's toil.

The last couplet goes further still. Kural 620 says those who strive without slackening and without losing heart will see even fate turn its back.

## Thirukkural on hard work, at the kitchen table

My son had practised all summer for the school soccer tryouts. Every evening in the park, cones laid out on the grass, me in the goal pretending I could stop anything. He did not make the team. He came home, sat at the kitchen table, and did not want dinner.

The same week, a proposal I had worked on for two months was turned down at work. Politely, with thanks, but turned down. I had not told anyone at home. So there we were at the table, father and son, both with the same face.

I tried to say something useful and realised I did not really believe it myself. That night, after he went to bed, I looked up kural 619, which my father used to recite, and read it again. The wages of the body's labour. What had my son actually earned from that summer? He could run longer than in June. His left foot was better. He had learned to get up after a bad drill and try again. None of that was on the team list. All of it was real.

And my proposal? I understood the business far better than before I wrote it. Two people I had never worked with now knew my name. The result had not come. The wages had.

The next morning I told him. He shrugged, the way eleven-year-olds do. A week later he asked me to come back out to the park.

## Does the Kural reject fate?

No, and this surprises people. Valluvar also has a whole chapter called Oozh, destiny, in the book of virtue, and it is sobering; he admits that there are forces larger than our efforts. He does not pretend we control everything.

What he refuses to do is let either idea excuse the other. Fate is no excuse for idleness; effort is no guarantee against fate. You hold both, and you work anyway. It is very close to the Bhagavad Gita's famous counsel that you have a right to your action but not to its fruits. Full action, loose grip. The old teaching of the [four purusharthas, the four aims of life](/blog/four-purusharthas), places effort toward wealth and success inside dharma for exactly this reason: striving is honourable, clinging is not.

I have written more generally about [using the Tirukkural in daily life](/blog/tirukkural-for-daily-life); this chapter is probably the one I return to most, because it is the one that gets me up.

## Twenty honest minutes

Pick one task you gave up on. A course half-finished, a musical instrument in the closet, a difficult letter you meant to write. Give it twenty honest minutes on one evening this week. Not to finish it. Just to collect the wages. And if the week goes badly anyway, do something for someone else with the energy you have left; feeding someone is the oldest version of that, as in [annadhanam, feeding people as worship](/blog/annadhanam-feeding-as-worship). Effort given away still pays.
