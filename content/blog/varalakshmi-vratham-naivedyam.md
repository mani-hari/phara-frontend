Varalakshmi Vratham always seems to fall on a Friday with a nine o'clock meeting. In our house my wife dresses the kalasam, the brass pot that becomes the seat of Lakshmi for the day, the night before, and I have learned that my job is the kozhukattai dough. If you are wondering what to cook for Varalakshmi Vratham naivedyam, the food offered to the Goddess before anyone eats, the honest answer is: whatever your family's list says, and as much of it as the morning allows.

## Varalakshmi vratham naivedyam: what families cook

Every family's list differs, but a typical Tamil spread looks something like this:

- **Kozhukattai, sweet and salt.** The sweet ones are filled with poornam, coconut and jaggery; the salt ones with ulundu, a spiced urad dal filling.
- **Sakkarai pongal**, the sweet rice with jaggery and ghee.
- **Paal payasam**, rice slowly cooked in milk.
- **Vadai**, crisp urad dal fritters.
- **A sundal**, usually chickpeas or green gram.
- **Plain rice with dal and ghee**, the simplest dish on the plate.
- **Fruit, betel leaves and areca nut.**

Telugu homes often add pulihora, the tamarind rice, and bobbatlu, a sweet flatbread stuffed with lentils and jaggery. Some families make nine items, some five, some two.

My mother in Chennai reads her list out on the video call every year, and every year it is slightly different from what I remember. When I point this out, she says the Goddess has not complained yet.

## When is the vratham, and why food for Lakshmi?

Varalakshmi Vratham is kept on the Friday just before the full moon of Shravana, the month that falls in Aadi or Avani in the Tamil calendar, usually July or August. Check the current panchangam for the exact Friday. For the full pooja, see [keeping Varalakshmi Vratham at home](/blog/varalakshmi-vratham-at-home), and for the pot at its centre, [what the kalasam means](/blog/kalasam-meaning). Fridays belong to the Goddess in many forms, from Lakshmi at home to [Kamakshi of Kanchipuram](/blog/kanchi-kamakshi-temple).

The story told in the vratha katha, which families attribute to the Skanda Purana, is of Charumathi, a devoted wife who was told in a dream to welcome Lakshmi with a simple vratham. She did, the women of her town joined her, and prosperity followed them all. The yellow thread tied on the wrist after the pooja carries that welcome forward.

Why food? Because you do not invite a guest without feeding her. The story behind Adi Shankara's Kanakadhara Stotram is of a poor woman who had only a single dried gooseberry to give a young monk at her door, and gave it. The verses that followed, the story says, brought golden rain. The point was never the gooseberry.

## How do you manage it on a US workday?

We have tried many versions. This is what works for us:

- **The night before:** cook the poornam filling and the sundal. Soak the dal for vadai if you are making it.
- **At dawn:** steam the kozhukattai, make the pongal, and do the pooja before logging in.
- **The short version:** on a heavy day, two items only, sakkarai pongal and a sundal. It still counts.
- **The rule we keep:** the naivedyam goes before the kalasam before anyone eats. My son now guards this rule more strictly than I do.
- **The evening:** neighbours come for thamboolam, the gift of betel leaves, fruit and turmeric, and a cup of whatever payasam remains.

The kozhukattai technique is the same as for [modak at Ganesh Chaturthi](/blog/modak-for-ganesh-chaturthi), and [paal payasam cooked slowly](/blog/paal-payasam-naivedyam) is worth the extra half-hour if you have it.

## One small thing to carry into the week

If the day is packed, cook one dish well. Offer it with the lamp, tie the thread, and log in. Let the evening be for guests. The Goddess who came to Charumathi came to a simple house.
