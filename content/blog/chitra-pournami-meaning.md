One night a year, tradition says, the keeper of everyone's accounts has a festival of his own. That night is Chitra Pournami, the full moon of the Tamil month of Chithirai, and it honours Chitragupta, the divine record-keeper who notes down every deed. You can treat it as a curiosity from mythology. Or you can use it: before anyone else reviews your ledger, sit down under the moon and keep your own accounts first.

## What is Chitra Pournami?

Chitra Pournami is the *pournami*, the full moon, of Chithirai, the first month of the Tamil year, usually falling in April or May. On this night the moon sits near the star Chitra, which gives the festival its double name. It is often the brightest, clearest full moon of early summer, before the monsoon clouds arrive. For the date in any given year, check the current year's panchangam or a [Hindu festival calendar for the USA](/blog/hindu-festival-calendar-usa).

In Tamil Nadu the night is crowded with devotion. At Tiruvannamalai, huge numbers of people walk the girivalam, the barefoot circuit around the sacred hill, through the night under the full moon. In Madurai, the full moon arrives in the middle of the great Chithirai festival, around the celestial wedding of [Meenakshi Thirukalyanam](/blog/meenakshi-thirukalyanam-festival) and Kallazhagar's entry into the Vaigai river. Shiva temples across the South hold special worship; among the older shrines, the [Sri Kalahasti temple](/blog/sri-kalahasti-temple-guide) is a good one to know.

## Who is Chitragupta, and why does he keep a ledger?

Chitragupta is the assistant of Yama, the lord of death and of dharma. His job is simple and enormous: he records the deeds of every living being, good and bad, so that when a life ends, the account is ready.

There are several stories of his birth. A much-loved Tamil version says Parvati once drew a picture of a boy, so beautiful that Shiva breathed life into it. The drawing, *chitra*, became a living person, and the boy became the keeper of records. Another tradition says he emerged from the body of Brahma after a long meditation, already holding a pen and palm leaves. One of the few temples dedicated to him stands in Kanchipuram, and it is especially busy on this full moon.

Notice what Chitragupta is not. He is not a punisher. He does not judge. He writes down what happened, accurately and without comment. Some people find the idea frightening. Try looking at it the other way. A record-keeper is someone who takes your life seriously enough to notice all of it.

## Chitra Pournami at home: moonlight, rice and a review

The home observance is lovely in its simplicity. Families cook *chitrannam*, the variety rices: lemon rice, tamarind rice, coconut rice, curd rice, sometimes all four. Then, in the old custom, they carry the food outdoors, to a terrace, a riverbank or a courtyard, and eat under the full moon together. A lamp is lit and a small offering is made to Chitragupta, often with a notebook and a pen placed before it.

You can do a version of this anywhere. Make one variety rice. Eat it outside if the weather lets you, or near a window with the moon in view. Light a lamp. And then do the part that most people skip: spend ten minutes looking honestly at your own ledger.

Take a sheet of paper. On one side write what you did well over the past month. On the other, what you would do differently. Do not argue with yourself. Do not justify. Just write it down, the way Chitragupta would, accurately and without comment. Many people find this oddly calming. The fear of being judged is usually worse than looking.

This is a natural cousin to the idea of what never runs out, celebrated within a few weeks of this night on [Akshaya Tritiya](/blog/akshaya-tritiya-significance). One day asks what you give; this night asks what you did.

## Two questions for seven nights

Starting on the full moon, try this for seven nights. Each night, just before sleep, name two things: one thing you did well today, and one thing you would redo. Say them aloud or write them down, then sleep.

Watch what it does. Watch whether you fall asleep more easily when the day has been accounted for. Watch whether the thing you would redo starts showing up less often by the end of the week. Chitragupta keeps the big ledger. The small one, the daily one, is yours.
