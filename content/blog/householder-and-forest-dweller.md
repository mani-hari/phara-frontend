A forest ascetic burned a bird with an angry glance and felt very holy about it. Then a housewife and a butcher taught him what holiness looked like. The four ashramas of life, the classic Hindu stages of student, householder, forest dweller and renunciant, are often described as a ladder toward the forest. The Mahabharata tells a story that tips the ladder sideways, and it is a good place to start.

## What are the four ashramas of life?

The ashramas are stages of life, as the Dharmasutras and the later dharmashastras describe them. Brahmacharya is the student stage: learning, discipline, living with a teacher. Grihastha is the householder stage: marriage, work, raising children, supporting the community. Vanaprastha is the forest-dweller stage, when the children are grown and a person begins to withdraw from worldly responsibilities. Sannyasa is renunciation, giving up home and possessions entirely for the pursuit of liberation.

To be clear, this was an ideal pattern more than a rule everyone lived. The texts themselves disagree about whether the stages must be taken in order, whether someone can skip straight to renunciation, and who the scheme applied to. In practice, most people spent their lives as students and householders, and many never went near a forest. The scheme is best read as a map of what each season of life is for.

The duties of the grihastha, the householder, are the widest of the four: earning honestly, caring for parents and children, feeding guests, supporting teachers and ascetics, performing the household rites. The householder, one might say, pays for everyone else's spirituality.

## The Vyadha Gita: dharma learned from a butcher

The story is in the Vana Parva of the Mahabharata. A young brahmin named Kaushika was sitting under a tree reciting the Vedas when a crane, perched above, let its droppings fall on him. Kaushika looked up in anger, and the bird fell dead. He felt a little sorry, and a little proud.

He went into the village to beg for food. At one house, the woman of the house asked him to wait while she finished serving her husband, who had just come home tired. Kaushika waited, fuming. When she finally came out, he rebuked her. She answered calmly: I am not a crane, to be burned by your anger. Kaushika was stunned. How could she know? She told him that her attention to her duties had given her a certain clarity, and that if he wanted to understand dharma, he should go to Mithila and find a man named Dharmavyadha.

Dharmavyadha turned out to be a butcher, selling meat in the market. Kaushika was appalled. But the butcher greeted him warmly, said he knew the housewife had sent him, took him home and showed him his aged parents, whom he cared for with great devotion. Then he taught. The teaching is called the Vyadha Gita, the song of the hunter-butcher: do your own work honestly, however humble; serve your parents; speak the truth; do not harm out of anger. The Vyadha Gita story ends with the proud ascetic going home to look after his own parents, whom he had abandoned for the forest.

The story says a butcher taught a brahmin. The story is really about where holiness lives. Kaushika had the powers of the forest and none of its peace. The housewife and the butcher, deep in ordinary duties, had both.

## Is the forest better than the house?

The Mahabharata returns to this question more than once, and it often praises the householder as the stage that supports all the others. Students, forest dwellers and renunciants all depend on the household for food and shelter. Without grihasthas, the other ashramas collapse.

Its favourite example is King Janaka of Mithila, ruler of a kingdom and yet famously unattached. There is a story that when his city caught fire, he remarked that nothing of his own was burning. He lived in the palace as others live in the forest.

Vanaprastha, read this way, is less about leaving and more about loosening. Vanaprastha, literally going to the forest, can be lived as handing over responsibilities, stepping back from control, making room for the next generation. For many families, that stage happens in a son's apartment in New Jersey or a daughter's house in Texas, as [parents visiting from India](/blog/parents-visiting-from-india) often discover.

## What does this stage ask of you?

The ashramas, like [the idea of an ishta devata](/blog/ishta-devata-meaning), belong to a tradition that holds several paths open at once; the Rig Veda's version is [ekam sat, one truth by many paths](/blog/many-paths-one-truth). And they sit inside the larger frame of [the four purusharthas](/blog/four-purusharthas), the aims each stage serves.

Wherever you are in life, student, parent, caregiver or retiree, try asking what this stage asks of you, rather than which stage would look more spiritual. Kaushika wanted the forest. His dharma was at home.
