The Hindu view of grief is not that you should stop feeling it. If anything, the tradition expected you to keep feeling it for a long time. That is why it built thirteen days of mourning, a monthly offering through the first year, and an annual rite that returns for the rest of a family's life. A tradition that wanted grief over quickly would not have built so much scaffolding around it.

## Does the Hindu view of grief say we should not cry?

People sometimes quote the Bhagavad Gita as if it tells you not to grieve. Look at how it actually begins. In the first chapter, Arjuna, the finest archer of his age, collapses in his chariot. His bow slips from his hand, his skin burns, his mouth dries, and he says he cannot go on. Only in the second chapter does Krishna begin to teach: that the self is not born and does not die, that the wise do not grieve for the living or the dead. [Arjuna's despair and why the Gita begins with a breakdown](/blog/arjuna-vishada-yoga-meaning) reads that opening closely.

Notice the order. The teaching comes after the tears, not instead of them. Krishna does not stop Arjuna from falling apart. He waits, and then he speaks. If you are grieving and someone quotes the Gita at you too early, you are allowed to say, "Not yet."

The teaching on the undying self, which the Upanishads develop and which [Advaita, explained simply](/blog/advaita-simply-explained) carries to its conclusion, can become a real comfort. But comfort that arrives before the body has done its grieving often feels like a door shut in your face.

## A mourning period that holds you

Look at the rules of the thirteen days with fresh eyes. Simple food, often cooked by others. No festivals, no celebrations, no obligation to be cheerful. Visitors who come, sit, say little and leave. The house quiet. Sleep when you can.

Read as restrictions, they sound severe. Read as care for a grieving body, they make sense. Grief is exhausting. It disturbs sleep, appetite and concentration. A tradition that removes social demands, feeds you and brings people to sit with you is looking after the body while the mind catches up. At the end of those days, [the thirteenth day ceremony](/blog/thirteenth-day-ceremony-meaning) opens the doors again, gently. If the loss is recent and the practical questions are pressing, [a Hindu funeral in the USA](/blog/hindu-funeral-in-the-usa) takes them one at a time.

## Small things for heavy days

These are small, and they are meant to be.

- **A lamp at the same hour.** Each evening, light one lamp at the same time and sit with it for two minutes. There is more on [the dusk lamp ritual](/blog/the-dusk-lamp-ritual) if you want a simple form.
- **One breath with the name.** Breathe in slowly. As you breathe out, say the person's name, silently or aloud. Once is enough.
- **Food cooked slowly.** Make one simple dish by hand, something they liked. Eat it sitting down, without a screen.
- **Sleep before chanting.** If you are exhausted, sleep first. A tired body cannot hold a practice. Chant tomorrow.

When grief is heavy, when it stops you working, sleeping or eating for a long stretch, talk to a grief counsellor or a doctor. This sits alongside prayer, not against it. The tradition built company into mourning because it knew no one should carry grief alone, and a counsellor is one more person sitting with you.

## Somewhere for attention to go

Here is how I find it useful to think about grief: it is attention with nowhere to go. All the attention you gave someone, the calls, the worry, the small habits of care, is still there, and it has lost its object. The practices of the tradition give it somewhere to go. A lamp, a name, a plate of food, one evening at a time.

You do not have to finish grieving. Nobody does. You only have to give it somewhere to land tonight, and then again tomorrow.
