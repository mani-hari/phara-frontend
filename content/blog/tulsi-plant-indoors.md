A tulsi plant indoors, in a cold climate, can thrive if you give it three things: bright light, steady warmth and less water than you think. Try this. Put it in your sunniest window, keep it away from the cold glass at night, and water only when the top of the soil is dry to your fingertip. A tulsi on a Chicago windowsill in January is a small act of stubbornness. Keep it alive, and it will quietly train you to pay attention to one living thing every single day.

## Caring for a tulsi plant indoors: light, warmth, water

Tulsi, holy basil, is a tropical plant. In India it grows in a courtyard pot in full sun. Most of what goes wrong abroad comes from forgetting that.

- **Light.** A south-facing window is best. In the northern US, winter light is short and weak, so a simple LED grow light for ten to twelve hours a day makes a real difference. Leggy stems reaching for the glass are the plant asking for more.
- **Warmth.** Keep it above about 60°F, ideally warmer. Tulsi sulks below that and can drop leaves overnight. Move it away from the window on very cold nights; glass gets colder than the room.
- **Water.** Water when the top inch of soil is dry, then let the excess drain. A pot with a hole and a saucer is non-negotiable.
- **Pinch the flowers.** When it sends up flower spikes, pinch them off. The plant then keeps putting its energy into leaves. Tradition offers the flowers, called manjari, in puja, so nothing is wasted.

Many people learning how to grow tulsi in the USA start seeds indoors in spring and move the pot outside for summer. That works well. The trick is bringing it back in before the first cold night.

## Why does a tulsi plant die in winter?

Three reasons, usually together. Cold drafts, from a door that opens onto the porch or a window that leaks. Overwatering, because the plant drinks far less in winter and roots sitting in wet soil rot. Short days, which slow the plant down just when you are giving it the same care as in summer. Holy basil winter care is mostly about doing less: less water, no fertiliser, more light, a warmer spot.

Variety matters too. Rama tulsi, with green leaves, is the most common and fairly forgiving. Krishna tulsi, with purple stems and leaves, is beautiful but often slower and fussier. Vana tulsi, the wild form, is vigorous and can handle container life well. If one fails, try another. A plant that dies is not a sign of anything except the weather.

## A two-minute tulsi ritual for busy mornings

The tradition treats tulsi less as a plant and more as a companion in the house. She is associated with Vishnu, and in many homes the tulsi pot is the first stop of the day. You can keep that spirit with a tulsi puja at home daily that takes about two minutes.

Try this. In the morning, before you touch your phone, offer a small cup of water at the base of the plant. Say a short prayer, or simply her name. If you like, walk around the pot once, clockwise; indoors, that may mean turning in place beside it. In the evening, light a small lamp near her. The meaning of that flame is explored in [why Hindus light lamps](/blog/why-do-hindus-light-lamps), and the evening version works beautifully as [a two-minute dusk ritual](/blog/the-dusk-lamp-ritual).

Notice what the ritual does. Watering a plant forces you to look at it. You see a new leaf, a yellowing one, dry soil. The body slows to the plant's pace for a moment. That is a kind of attention most of us rarely give anything before breakfast.

If you are building a fuller routine, the tulsi fits naturally into [puja at home, step by step](/blog/puja-at-home-step-by-step), and it can anchor [a small puja corner in an apartment](/blog/small-puja-corner-apartment) where floor space is tight.

## Put her where you pass her

The single most useful thing you can do is location. Put the tulsi somewhere you walk past every morning: the kitchen window, the spot beside the coffee maker, the sill by the front door. A plant you have to go looking for will be forgotten by February.

Then make one rule for a week. Water her, or check her soil, before you check your phone. See what changes in the first ten minutes of your day.
