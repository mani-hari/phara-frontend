In the Katha Upanishad, a boy named Nachiketa walks to the house of Death and asks the one question adults avoid: what happens after we die? He is not scolded for asking. He is answered. That is the first lesson for anyone explaining death to children, Hindu or otherwise: the question is allowed, and the child deserves a real answer in plain words. The tradition helps with an image a child can hold and with rituals small enough for small hands.

## Explaining death to children: what does the tradition offer?

The Bhagavad Gita, in its second chapter, gives an image that children understand at once. Just as a person takes off worn-out clothes and puts on new ones, Krishna says, the self sets down a worn-out body and takes up another. The body is the clothing. The one who wore it is not destroyed.

This is most of what explaining reincarnation to kids requires, and it works because it is ordinary. Every child has outgrown a shirt. Nobody cries over the shirt; it simply no longer fits. You do not need to add heaven maps or warnings. The image is not frightening, and it leaves room for the child to feel sad, which they will, because the person they loved is no longer in the chair at the dinner table.

Older children who want the fuller idea, karma and rebirth included, can read [reincarnation in Hinduism](/blog/reincarnation-in-hinduism) with you.

## Which stories help a grieving child?

Nachiketa's story is the obvious one: a boy sent to Death's door in a father's temper, who turns down gold and long life because he would rather have an answer. The full telling, and what Yama finally teaches him, belongs to [what moksha means](/blog/what-is-moksha); for a child the point is simpler. The story says a boy outwitted Death. The story is really about a child who was not fobbed off.

Then there is Savitri, told in the Mahabharata. When Yama comes for her husband Satyavan, she follows him, step after step, talking, arguing, refusing to turn back, until Yama is so moved that he returns Satyavan's life. Children love her stubbornness. It is a story about love as much as death, and it lets a child know that missing someone fiercely is not wrong.

And there are the ancestors. On Amavasya, the new moon, many families offer water and sesame to those who have died. You can tell a child that the family still holds its people close, and that is why we remember them by name. There is more on this in [the new moon and the ancestors](/blog/amavasya-and-ancestors).

## Letting children take part

Here is a situation many families in the US know. A grandparent dies in Chennai or Pune, and not everyone can fly. The rites happen on a phone propped against a wall, in a time zone where it is the middle of the night. The child watches the fire, the priest, the uncles with shaved heads, and understands very little.

Give them something to do with their hands. A flower placed before the grandparent's photograph. A lamp lit, perhaps by them, perhaps together. When the family later observes shraddha, the yearly rite for the dead, explain it as feeding memory: we cook what Paati liked, and we say her name. The [annual shraddham](/blog/annual-shraddham-meaning) is, among other things, a family agreeing not to forget.

Use plain words. "Thatha died" is kinder in the long run than "Thatha has gone to sleep," which can make bedtime frightening. "His body stopped working" is true. "We will miss him" is true. "Where did he go?" can be answered with the clothing image, and with "we don't know everything, and that's all right."

## Keeping the name alive

Grief does not end on the thirteenth day, and children revisit it as they grow. Let them. Parents raising children far from grandparents may find the ideas in [raising Hindu kids in America](/blog/raising-hindu-kids-in-america) useful here too.

The story of Nachiketa is really about continuing. He goes to Death's house and comes back with knowledge. That is what we are asking of our children, in a small way. So let the child say the name of the person who died, often, at dinner and in the car. And on that day each year, light a lamp together, and let the child tell you one thing they remember.
