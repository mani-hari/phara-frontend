In many Hindu texts, the holiest thing a student can do is ask an awkward question and refuse to accept the first answer. Argument is not the opposite of devotion; it is one of its forms. The shastrartha debate, which Hindu scholars kept alive for centuries, was a formal contest over the meaning of the sacred texts, with rules for fairness, judges, and a strong expectation that you state your opponent's view honestly before you attack it. The questioning goes back to the Upanishads and runs straight through the Bhagavad Gita.

## How did a shastrartha debate actually work?

Shastrartha means, roughly, the meaning of the shastras, the authoritative texts. A shastrartha was a public debate about that meaning: what a verse says, how a teaching should be read, which school had understood it best. Kings hosted them. Scholars travelled for them. Reputations were won and lost.

The first rule was purvapaksha. Purvapaksha means "the prior view", the opponent's position, and the rule was that you must state it fully and fairly before giving your answer. Philosophical texts are built this way. Page after page sets out the rival argument in its strongest form, then replies. A reader of a great commentary often learns the opposing school's case almost as well as the author's own.

The Nyaya school, which made a science of logic, went further and sorted arguments into kinds. Vada is honest inquiry, two people seeking the truth together. Jalpa is debate to win, where each side uses every trick available. Vitanda is mere fault-finding, attacking the other side without defending any position of your own. The distinction between vada, jalpa and vitanda is useful well beyond philosophy. Most online arguments, you may notice, are the third kind.

## The famous debates

The oldest scene is not a formal debate but it has the same spirit. In the Katha Upanishad, a boy named Nachiketa is sent to the house of Yama, the god of death. Yama offers him three wishes. For the third, Nachiketa asks what happens to a person after death. Yama tries to bribe him out of the question: long life, sons, cattle, gold, beautiful companions, a kingdom. Nachiketa refuses all of it. Keep your horses and your dancers, he says. Tell me the answer. Yama, impressed, finally teaches him.

The most famous shastrartha is told in the Shankara Digvijaya, the traditional life of Adi Shankara. Shankara, a young monk teaching that the self is one with Brahman, sought out Mandana Mishra, a great scholar of ritual who held that the Vedas were chiefly about action. The judge was Mandana's wife, Ubhaya Bharati, herself renowned for learning. She placed a garland around each man's neck and said that the loser's would be the first to wilt. The debate went on for days. At last Mandana's garland faded, and he became Shankara's disciple. The tellings add that Ubhaya Bharati then debated Shankara herself. The Shankara Mandana Mishra debate is remembered less for who won than for how: two scholars, a woman as judge, and a result both accepted.

## Why argue with God?

The Bhagavad Gita is, from beginning to end, a conversation in which a man questions God. Arjuna does not simply obey. He objects, doubts, asks for clarification, and asks again. Krishna answers for eighteen chapters, and at the end he does not command. He says: reflect on all this fully, then do as you choose. The questioning begins in the very first chapter, with [Arjuna's despair](/blog/arjuna-vishada-yoga-meaning).

The tradition also did something unusual with the positions it rejected. It kept them. The views of rival schools, including those it considered wrong, were preserved in the commentaries, so that the next student could test them again. Winning did not mean erasing. The ritual school of Mandana Mishra, for instance, argued at length for why Hindus [perform rituals at all](/blog/why-do-hindus-do-rituals), and its arguments outlived its defeat. The idea of [the ishta devata](/blog/ishta-devata-meaning), too, only makes sense in a tradition comfortable with difference. The same spirit animates [the four ashramas](/blog/householder-and-forest-dweller), where the forest and the household argue over which life is holier.

## The purvapaksha habit

Perhaps the most practical gift of all this is the rule of purvapaksha. It is a discipline, and it is harder than it sounds.

In your next disagreement, at work, at home, with a teenager, try it. Before you answer, state the other person's view well enough that they nod. Not a caricature. Their actual position, in its best form. Then give your reply. You may find the argument changes shape. You may find you were arguing with someone who did not exist. And if you still disagree, you will at least be having a vada, not a vitanda.
