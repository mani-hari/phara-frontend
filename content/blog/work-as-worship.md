My Thatha measured cloth in his Kumbakonam shop as if the customer's wedding depended on it, and in a way it did. I did not have a word for it then. If you want "work is worship" in one line, it is what he did: the task in front of him, done with full care and offered, without clinging to what it would bring. The Bhagavad Gita calls this karma yoga, the discipline of action. My grandfather never called it anything. He just folded the sari properly.

## Work is worship, in the texts

The Gita's third chapter is where most people meet the idea. Krishna tells Arjuna, more or less, that nobody can stop acting even for a moment, so the question is not whether to work but how. Act, he says, but let go of your grip on the fruit. Offer the action instead of hoarding it. The Bhagavad Gita on work is not a counsel of indifference; it asks for full effort and a loose hand at the end. The fuller treatment is in [the Gita's teaching of work without attachment](/blog/gita-on-work-without-attachment).

The line I actually carry, though, comes from further north and further west than Kumbakonam. The twelfth-century Kannada saint Basavanna wrote a vachana, a short free-verse saying, that is remembered in two words: kayakave kailasa. Work itself is Kailasa, Shiva's mountain home. You do not need to climb anywhere. Kayakave kailasa means that heaven is already present in honest labour.

Thatha's shop had its own ethic about this, which I tried to put down in [honest trade and what his shop taught me](/blog/honest-trade-thathas-shop).

## Can a spreadsheet be an offering?

I work in an open-plan office in New Jersey. There is no cloth to measure. There are meetings, a shared calendar, and a spreadsheet I have been fixing since the spring. For a long time I assumed karma yoga was for farmers and potters, not for someone who spends Tuesday afternoon reconciling numbers.

Then I watched a colleague. She is the person who quietly cleans up the shared drive, writes the notes nobody else wants to write, and checks the figures twice. Nobody praises her for it. She does it the way Thatha folded a sari: completely.

Offering does not change the task. The spreadsheet is still the spreadsheet. What changes is who you are while you do it. When the work is offered, you are less likely to cut the corner nobody will see, and less likely to sulk when the credit goes to someone else. This is close to what the tradition calls [seva, service in which you are not the point](/blog/seva-meaning). Some mornings I touch a small figure on my desk before logging in; keeping [a small altar at your desk](/blog/small-altar-at-your-desk) takes less fuss than people think.

## What does work as worship not mean?

Care is needed here, because this old idea can be bent into something unkind.

Work as worship does not mean overwork. It does not mean staying silent when you are treated unfairly, or working weekends because the phrase sounds holy. It does not mean tying your worth to a performance review. Krishna asks Arjuna to act without clinging to the fruit; he does not ask him to accept injustice without a word. Worship, in every temple I know, includes rest. The deity is put to sleep at night. The doors close.

It also does not mean your work defines you. If the job ends, you have not lost your practice; the offering simply moves to the next task, even if that task is looking for work. A column on [the Hindu view of a layoff](/blog/after-a-layoff-hindu-view) takes that up more gently than I can here.

## One small thing to carry into the week

Before your first task on Monday, pause for a breath. Say to yourself, this is my offering. Then do the task as carefully as Thatha folded a sari: corners matched, nothing rushed, nothing hidden. See whether the rest of the day feels any different. It may not. But the one task will be done well, and that is worship enough for a Monday.
