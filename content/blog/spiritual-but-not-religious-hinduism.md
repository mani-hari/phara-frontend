If you call yourself spiritual but not religious, Hinduism has room for you, and always has. Spiritual but not religious Hinduism is not a contradiction: the tradition has held questioners, doubters and quiet seekers for as long as it has held priests. I was reminded of this at a cousin's engagement, over plates of bisibele bath, when my younger cousin said, a little too loudly, that she was "spiritual, not religious". An aunt went quiet. Someone changed the subject to the caterer. I remember thinking that the tradition itself would have been far less worried than the aunt.

## Spiritual but not religious Hinduism: an old idea

The phrase sounds modern, but the stance is very old. The Upanishads, the philosophical texts at the end of the Vedas, are almost entirely made of questions. Students sit near a teacher and ask: what is the self, what is real, what remains when everything else falls away? The answers are not commandments. They are invitations to look.

Go back further. In the Rig Veda there is a hymn of creation called the Nasadiya Sukta. It asks how the universe began, circles the question with great care, and ends like this: the one who looks down on it from the highest heaven, surely he knows, or perhaps he does not know. That line is inside the oldest scripture of the tradition. Doubt is not an intruder in Hinduism. It was invited to the first gathering.

So when my cousin said what she said, she was not leaving anything. She was standing in a long line of people who wanted to understand before they bowed.

## Can you be Hindu without believing in God?

It is a fair question, and many would answer yes. Some of the classical schools of Indian philosophy, such as Samkhya and parts of Mimamsa, did not centre a creator god at all, and they were never thrown out. Hinduism is less a single creed than a family of practices, texts and ways of living.

The tradition also describes several paths, and you do not have to walk all of them. Jnana is the path of knowledge and inquiry. Bhakti is the path of devotion and love. Karma is the path of action done well without clutching the result, which is the heart of [karma explained without the guilt](/blog/karma-explained-simply). Dhyana is the path of meditation. A person who never enters a temple but spends years asking what the self is, or serving others without fuss, is walking one of these roads.

For hindu philosophy without rituals, the path of action and the path of inquiry are natural places to start. So is simple service: many find that [feeding others as a form of worship](/blog/annadhanam-feeding-as-worship) says more than any argument about gods.

## Finding your own entry point

Here is what I have noticed in friends who came back to the tradition after years away. Almost none of them came back through belief. They came back through a practice. One started lighting a lamp at dusk because her grandmother used to, and found the two minutes became the calmest part of her evening; the [dusk lamp ritual](/blog/the-dusk-lamp-ritual) describes exactly that. Another started reading one verse of the Gita on the train each morning. A third began visiting the temple only on her father's death anniversary, which led her, slowly, to questions about ancestors that she had never asked; [pitru dosha explained](/blog/pitru-dosha-explained) is where many start with those.

Practice before belief is not a shortcut. It is how most of the tradition was transmitted: you did the thing with your grandmother, and meaning arrived later, sometimes decades later. And if your practice is more sitting than offering, [meditation and puja](/blog/meditation-vs-puja) are closer cousins than they look. For the idea that ties it together, [dharma in everyday life](/blog/dharma-meaning-in-everyday-life) is a good next read.

## One small thing for the month

Choose one small thing. Five quiet minutes with a lamp in the evening, or one verse from an Upanishad to sit with each morning. Keep it for a month without deciding what it means. You do not have to call it religion. You do not have to call it anything. At the end of the month, notice whether it has become yours.
