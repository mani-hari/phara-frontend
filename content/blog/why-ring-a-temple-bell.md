You ring the bell at the temple entrance because the tradition treats sound as a doorway. One clear note marks the moment you stop arriving and start being present. Why ring a bell at the temple? Ask around and you will find long lists of reasons, some traditional and some dressed up as science. The traditional reasons are worth knowing. But the more useful answer is the one you can test yourself: listen to a single bell until it stops, and notice what happens to your mind.

## Why a bell at the doorway? The traditional reasons

Walk into almost any Hindu temple and there is a bell hanging near the entrance to the sanctum, often several of different sizes. Devotees reach up and ring it once as they arrive. The reasons tradition gives are simple.

The first is announcement. You are letting the deity know you have come, the way you would knock before entering a room. The second is clearing. The sharp, bright note is said to drown out the chatter around you and inside you, the leftover conversation from the car park. The third is auspiciousness. In the old texts on worship, the temple bell's meaning includes inviting good forces and turning away unwelcome ones. Many prayers recited while ringing the bell say exactly this.

The small hand bell used in pooja, the ghanta, carries its own symbolism. Its handle is often topped with a figure: Garuda, the eagle, in Vishnu temples, Nandi, the bull, in Shiva temples, sometimes Hanuman. The figure tells you whose house you are in.

## What does the sound do to your attention?

Here is where the practice gets interesting. A bell is struck once, and then it rings, and then the ring fades, and fades, and fades until you are no longer sure whether you are hearing it or remembering it. If you follow that sound all the way to its end, something happens. For a few seconds, the mind has only one job. It is listening for the ending.

Many people find this is the quickest route into stillness they know. You do not need to stop thinking. You only need to listen so closely that thinking has no room. The moment the sound disappears, there is a small gap of silence, and in that gap you are simply there.

I will not claim more for the bell than that. Some writers describe effects on the brain's hemispheres or on energy centres. You can leave those claims aside. The experience of following a sound to silence is enough, and it is easy to verify for yourself.

This is also why the bell belongs to the same family as the conch, whose side of the story is in [why the conch is blown in worship](/blog/shankha-conch-meaning).

## Why ring a bell during aarti and abhishekam?

During aarti, when the lamp is waved before the deity, and during abhishekam, the ritual bathing of the image, the priest rings the hand bell continuously. The sound becomes a wall around the ritual. Everything outside it, the crying baby, the phone, the traffic, recedes behind the ringing. For those few minutes, attention has a boundary.

The ghanta, the hand bell, works the same way in puja at home. A small brass hand bell costs very little and lasts a lifetime. Ring it as you offer the lamp or the flowers, and let it sound until the offering is complete. Setting up for the first time? [How to do aarti at home](/blog/how-to-do-aarti-at-home) has the steps.

The bell is one of several small acts that frame temple worship. [Walking around the shrine](/blog/pradakshina-meaning) is another, and so is the mark on the forehead. Each one is a way of telling the body that something different is about to happen.

## Ring once, then listen

At your home shrine, before you light the lamp or say anything, ring a small bell once. Just once. Then listen. Stay with the sound as it thins out, and do not begin until it is completely gone. Only then light the lamp. Do this every day for a week. You may find that the bell becomes the real beginning of your practice, and that the silence after it is the part you look forward to. If you do not think of yourself as a believer, that is fine too; the piece on [being Hindu without believing in God](/blog/hindu-without-believing-in-god) makes room for exactly this kind of practice, done for its own sake. And if you want a design for the space in front of your shrine, [kolam and rangoli](/blog/kolam-and-rangoli-meaning) are another way of marking a threshold.
