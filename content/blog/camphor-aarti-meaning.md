Camphor is used in aarti because it burns completely and leaves nothing behind. Watch it once with full attention, from the first spark to the last flicker, and you will see why the tradition chose it to close the worship. That is why camphor is used in aarti: a flame that consumes itself entirely is the clearest picture of an offering that holds nothing back. Everything else about the practice follows from that.

## Why camphor, of all things?

Camphor, karpuram in Tamil and karpura in Sanskrit, is a white, waxy substance that catches fire instantly and burns with a bright, hot flame. It does not need a wick. It does not smoke much when it is pure. And when it is done, there is nothing left on the plate. No ash, no residue, no stub.

The traditional reading follows from that. The camphor is the ego. When it meets the flame of awareness, it burns away completely, without leaving a trace of itself behind. The light it gives off is offered to the deity. In that reading, the self is not set aside for a moment but given up entirely, and what remains is light.

In practice, the camphor aarti, also called karpura harathi, comes at the end of worship. After the lamps, the flowers and the food have been offered, the priest lights a small piece of camphor on a plate and waves it in circles before the deity. Then the plate is brought out to the devotees. Each person passes their palms over the flame, then touches their palms to their eyes. You are receiving the light back. For most devotees, that small gesture of receiving is the part they remember.

## Watching the flame: a practice

Here is something you can try, with or without any belief attached. It takes under a minute.

1. Place a small piece of camphor on a steel or brass plate. A piece the size of a pea is enough.
2. Turn off the lights, or dim them, so the flame is the main source of light.
3. Light the camphor. Sit back.
4. Watch it from the first spark to the very last flicker. Do not look away. Do not reach for your phone.
5. When it goes out, stay in the dark for a few moments.

Notice what the eye does. At first it is drawn to the brightness. Then, as the flame shrinks, the eye leans in, following it down. And at the end, there is a moment when the flame is gone and the eye is still looking for it. Notice what the mind does in that moment. Many people find it goes quiet, briefly, because it was fully occupied by something that has just ended.

This is closely related to the practice of following [the sound of a temple bell](/blog/why-ring-a-temple-bell) until it fades. Both work because they give attention one thing to follow, all the way to its end.

## Is camphor safe to burn at home?

Mostly yes, with some care.

There are two kinds of camphor on shop shelves. Natural camphor, sometimes called bhimseni or edible camphor, comes from the camphor tree. Many of the white tablets sold in packets are synthetic, made from turpentine. Both burn, but many families prefer natural camphor for worship because it burns cleaner and smells gentler.

Whichever you use, keep the room ventilated, especially in a small apartment. Always burn it on a metal plate or a camphor stand, never directly on a wooden shelf or a plastic tray. Keep the flame, and the camphor itself, away from children and pets, since camphor is harmful if swallowed. And watch the smoke detector. Many a pooja in an American kitchen has ended with someone fanning the alarm with a newspaper. With these precautions, most families use it without trouble. This is a safety note, not a health claim.

## Seven evenings, one small flame

End your evening lamp with one small camphor flame. After lighting the lamp and saying whatever prayer you say, place a tiny piece on a plate and light it. Watch it burn down. When it goes out, sit for ten seconds in silence before you move. Let those ten seconds be the prayer.
