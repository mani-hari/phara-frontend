The story says Rama worshipped Shiva at Rameswaram, on the island at the tip of India, before going home. The lingam he worshipped was made of sand, shaped by Sita's hands, because the proper one had not arrived in time. That is the heart of Rameswaram temple history, and it is a strange heart for one of the great shrines of India: the holiest object in the temple is the one that was improvised.

## What is the story behind the Rameswaram temple?

The war in Lanka is over. Ravana is dead, and in many tellings Rama feels the weight of having killed a learned man, however cruel. He wishes to worship Shiva before he returns to Ayodhya. (Rama's own family image of Vishnu would later be given to Vibhishana and come to rest on a river island, which is the story of [the Srirangam temple](/blog/srirangam-temple-guide).) This is very much [Rama as maryada purushottama](/blog/rama-maryada-purushottama), the one who keeps to the limits of right conduct: victorious, and still careful about what the victory cost.

Hanuman is sent north to Kailash to bring a lingam; some tellings say Kashi. He is the fastest being in the epic. Even so, he is late. The auspicious hour approaches, and Sita, sitting on the shore, gathers the wet sand and shapes a lingam with her hands. Rama worships it at the right moment. That sand lingam is the Ramalingam, and it is worshipped in the sanctum to this day.

Then Hanuman arrives, carrying the lingam he flew so far to fetch, and is hurt to find the worship done. Rama does not dismiss him. He installs Hanuman's lingam, the Vishwalingam, beside the other, and asks that it be worshipped first, before his own. The tradition keeps that order.

The story says a lingam arrived late. The story is really about how the right offering is the one made in time, with what is at hand. Sand, not stone. Sita's hands, not a priest's. And then, just as important, the gesture to the one who tried hard and came second.

## The corridors

The Ramanathaswamy temple is known for its prakarams, the pillared corridors that run around the shrine. They are among the longest temple corridors in India, built up over centuries by the Sethupathi rulers of Ramanathapuram and others before them.

People who have walked them describe the same thing: pillar after pillar receding into a dim distance, carved, painted, repeating, until counting stops making sense. A long corridor does something to the mind. It removes the destination for a while. You are simply walking, and the walking becomes the point. Pilgrims often pair Rameswaram with Madurai a few hours inland, whose city-sized temple gets [a guide to the Meenakshi temple](/blog/madurai-meenakshi-temple) of its own.

## Why do pilgrims bathe in so many tirthams?

Before the darshan, the sight of the deity, most pilgrims bathe. First in the sea at the Agni Tirtham, just outside the eastern tower. Then in the wells inside the temple complex, traditionally counted as twenty-two, each with its own name and its own story. An attendant draws water and pours a bucket over your head at each one. You move from well to well, drenched, dripping on the stone floors.

The logic is not hygiene. Each tirtham, a sacred water, is linked with a god, a sage or a virtue, and the pilgrim is washed by each in turn. By the end people say they feel lighter. Perhaps that is the water. Perhaps it is simply that it is hard to hold on to a grudge while someone keeps pouring buckets over you.

Rameswaram is also a place where families perform rites for ancestors by the sea, which is why [the Tila Homam at Rameswaram](/blog/tila-homam-at-rameswaram-meaning) is so closely tied to it.

## Keeping the thread

For centuries Rameswaram was one end of a single pilgrimage. Pilgrims bathed at Rameswaram, carried sand north to the Ganga, and returned with Ganga water to pour on the lingam here. The other end of that thread is described in [why Kashi Vishwanath and Varanasi matter](/blog/kashi-vishwanath-temple). North and south, joined by a pot of water and a handful of sand.

Most of us will not make that journey. If you cannot, a pooja or a Tila Homam at Rameswaram in your family's name keeps the thread unbroken. And the story leaves a simpler gift for anyone, anywhere. If the proper offering has not arrived, use the sand you have. The hour matters more than the material.
