Vidyarambham is the ceremony in which a small child writes their first letters, usually in a plate of rice, on the morning of Vijayadashami. My nephew was not quite three when his turn came. He sat on his grandmother's lap on the floor of her Palakkad house, and she held his index finger in hers and moved it through the grains: the syllable Om, and then the first letter of the alphabet. His finger shook. He looked up at her halfway through, as if to ask whether he was allowed to spoil such a neat plate of rice. She nodded, and he went on. That nod is, to me, the whole ritual. A family is telling a child that learning is sacred, and that he is welcome in it.

## What is vidyarambham?

The word joins vidya, knowledge, and arambham, beginning. In Kerala the ritual is also called ezhuthiniruthu, which means, roughly, "seating a child to write", and ezhuthiniruthu meaning captures the tenderness of it: someone has to sit the child down and sit with them. It is strongest in Kerala and parts of Tamil Nadu and Karnataka, where temples and homes fill with families on the tenth day after the nine nights of Navratri.

The timing is deliberate. The last days of Navratri belong to Saraswati, the Goddess of learning, and books and instruments are placed before her and left untouched during Saraswati puja and [Ayudha Pooja](/blog/ayudha-pooja-meaning). On Vijayadashami, the day of victory, the books are taken back and the first new thing is learned. A child's first letters are the most tender version of that return. If you want to know why the festival builds to this point over nine nights, [why Navratri lasts nine nights](/blog/why-navratri-lasts-nine-nights) tells the story.

## How is the first-letters ritual done?

It is short. A grandparent, teacher, priest or respected elder sits facing east with the child on their lap. The tools are worshipped first: the plate of rice, a slate or palm leaf, a pen or stylus, all placed before Saraswati or Ganesha. The reason is simple enough to explain to a child. We bow to the things that will teach us before we use them.

Then the guiding begins. Writing in rice on Vijayadashami, the elder holds the child's finger and traces a line such as "Om Hari Sri Ganapataye Namah" or the first letter of the family's language. In many families a gold ring is dipped in honey and the same syllable is written gently on the child's tongue, so that speech itself is blessed. The child may cry. Mine laughed at the honey. A sweet is given, the rice is later cooked or offered, and the day is marked.

The Tirukkural, in its chapter on learning, says: learn thoroughly what should be learned, and then live by it. I thought of that couplet watching my nephew, because the ritual is really about the second half. Nobody expects a three-year-old to learn thoroughly. We are asking him to grow into a person who lives by what he learns.

## Vidyarambham far from home

A vidyarambham ceremony at home, far from Kerala, needs very little. A kitchen table works as well as a temple floor: a clean plate, uncooked rice, a lamp, a picture of Saraswati. The person who guides the hand matters more than the place. When grandparents cannot travel, many families set up a laptop at child height so a grandmother in Thrissur can speak the syllables while a parent in Texas guides the finger. The practical side of that, including the time-zone arithmetic, is covered in [joining a puja across time zones](/blog/joining-a-puja-across-time-zones).

Adults take part too. In Kerala, it is common to see grown men and women at the temple on Vijayadashami, beginning music, dance, a new language or a new trade on the same morning. Students preparing for exams often keep this day for their first page of revision, a habit that sits comfortably beside a simple [prayer before exams](/blog/prayer-before-exams). The two goddesses of learning, and how families pray to them, are introduced in [Saraswati and Hayagriva](/blog/saraswati-and-hayagriva).

## One small thing to carry into the week

Whatever your age, pick one thing you have been meaning to learn. On Vijayadashami, or on any morning that feels like a beginning, pour a little rice onto a plate and write its first word with your finger. Then begin, with someone's nod, even if it is only your own.
