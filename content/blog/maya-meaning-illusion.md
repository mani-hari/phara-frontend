Narada, the wandering sage, once asked Vishnu to show him maya. Vishnu agreed, but said he was thirsty first. Would Narada fetch him a glass of water? By the time Narada came back, a whole lifetime had passed. The story is the best short answer to the question of maya meaning in Hinduism, because it shows that maya is not quite what the usual translation, illusion, suggests.

## The story of Narada and the glass of water

It is a Puranic story, told in many versions; in one telling in the Devi Bhagavata Purana, Narada even becomes a woman after bathing in a lake. In the version grandmothers tell, he simply goes to fetch water. He walks to a nearby village. At the first house, a young woman opens the door, and he forgets why he came. He stays, marries her, and they have children. Years pass. He farms, grows old, worries about harvests and his children's marriages. Then a great flood comes. He tries to hold on to his wife and children, and the water takes them one by one. He is washed up alone on a riverbank, weeping.

A voice says: "Narada, where is my water? I have been waiting." He looks up. Vishnu is standing there, exactly as before, and only a few moments have gone by.

## Maya meaning in Hinduism: not 'unreal', but misread

The word maya is often linked to a Sanskrit root meaning to measure, to mark out. Maya is the power that measures the one out into many: the single reality appearing as separate things, separate people, a separate you. In the seventh chapter of the Bhagavad Gita, Krishna calls it "my divine maya, made of the gunas, hard to cross", and adds that those who take refuge in him cross beyond it.

Notice what the story does not say. It does not say Narada's wife and children were fake. It does not say the flood was painless. It says Narada forgot. He was so absorbed in his life that he forgot the one who sent him and the errand he was on. Maya, in this reading, is less a lie than a misreading. The world is there; what we misread is what it is, and who we are within it.

## Is the world an illusion, then?

Here the schools part ways, gently. Advaita Vedanta, which holds that only Brahman is ultimately real, calls the world mithya: neither simply real nor simply unreal. The classic image is a rope in dim light taken for a snake. The fear is real, the rope is real, the snake is not. Two posts pick up the thread: [Advaita explained simply](/blog/advaita-simply-explained), with its pot and its rope, and [atman and Brahman](/blog/atman-and-brahman).

The bhakti traditions read maya more warmly. For devotees of the Goddess, Maya is one of her names; the Devi Mahatmyam calls her Mahamaya, the great enchantress who binds the world and also sets it free, as told in [the Durga Saptashati](/blog/durga-saptashati-for-beginners). For Vaishnavas, the world is lila, divine play, and maya is the part of the play where the players forget it is a play.

The story says Narada was tricked. The story is really about absorption: getting so caught up in the play that you forget who is playing. That is a very ordinary experience. Anyone who has spent a week furious about a meeting, and a month later could not remember what the meeting was about, has had a small taste of Narada's riverbank.

## The glass of water is still in your hand

Maya, then, is not the world. It is the forgetting. Nobody is asking you to stop loving your family or tending your fields; the tradition honours [the household as much as the forest](/blog/householder-and-forest-dweller). Narada's life was not wasted. It taught him more than any lecture could.

The tradition only asks you to remember, now and then, that you were sent for water. Whatever your errand is, the glass is still in your hand, and the one who asked for it is still waiting, unhurried. Whether that remembering leads in time to [moksha](/blog/what-is-moksha) is a question each school answers in its own way.
