A flower is the easiest offering there is, and the tradition still has opinions about which one goes where. The short answer to which flower for which god: bilva leaves and white flowers for Shiva, tulsi and jasmine for Vishnu and Krishna, lotus and red flowers for Lakshmi, durva grass and red hibiscus for Ganesha, and red hibiscus for Durga. Knowing the pairings is less about rules and more about paying attention to what you pick up.

## Which flower for which god?

Here is the common South and North Indian pattern, with the caveat that regions and families vary.

- Shiva: bilva leaves above all, three-lobed and fragrant when crushed; white flowers; and erukku, the humble calotropis that grows in waste ground, which Shiva is said to love precisely because nobody else wants it. Bilva leaves for Shiva are one of the most constant customs across India.
- Vishnu and Krishna: tulsi leaves first, then jasmine and lotus. Tulsi is so central that many homes grow it on purpose, and [growing tulsi indoors](/blog/tulsi-plant-indoors) works even in a cold climate.
- Lakshmi: lotus, red roses and marigold. The best flowers for Lakshmi puja are bright, full and generous, like the goddess herself.
- Ganesha: durva, a slender sacred grass, in bunches of three or five, red hibiscus, and leaves of the shami tree. He is greeted first in almost every pooja, for reasons told in [why Ganesha comes first](/blog/why-ganesha-comes-first).
- Durga and Kali: red hibiscus, the colour of Shakti.
- Murugan: red flowers and, traditionally, kadamba, the round orange blossom of a forest tree.
- Saraswati: white lotus and jasmine.

If you worship a different deity each day of the week, pair this list with [which god is worshipped on which day](/blog/which-god-on-which-day).

## Are there flowers you should not offer?

A few, and each comes with a story.

The best known is the ketaki, a fragrant screwpine flower. In the Shiva Purana, Brahma and Vishnu argue over who is greater, and Shiva appears as an endless column of fire. Vishnu goes down to find its base; Brahma flies up to find its top. Neither succeeds. Brahma, embarrassed, persuades a ketaki flower drifting down from above to lie and say he reached the summit. Shiva sees through it, and the ketaki is barred from his worship forever. The story is about honesty, and the flower pays for someone else's pride.

There is also a common custom of not offering tulsi to Ganesha, which families explain with different stories. And across traditions, some flowers are simply the wrong kind to offer: wilted ones, ones that have fallen to the ground, or ones you have smelled first. The offering should be fresh, whole and untouched by your own enjoyment.

## How do you offer flowers with attention?

Pick or buy fresh, if you can the same morning. Rinse them gently in cool water. Do not smell them first, which feels strange at first and then becomes a small discipline: this one is not for you.

Offer with the right hand, or with both hands cupped together. Place flowers at the feet of the image, not on its head, unless your family custom says otherwise. Offer one at a time, slowly, with a name or a short mantra for each.

In the US, grocery store flowers are perfectly fine. Roses, carnations, chrysanthemums and marigolds all work. A single stem is enough; a small arrangement at [a desk altar at work](/blog/small-altar-at-your-desk) can hold one flower in a glass. Pair them, if you like, with a stick of incense; [why incense belongs in worship](/blog/incense-and-dhoop-meaning) explains what the smoke adds.

After the pooja, do not throw the flowers in the trash. Compost them, or place them at the base of a plant or tree.

## One flower, slowly

Before the next pooja, choose one flower slowly. Look at its colour, its freshness, whether it is fully open. Pick it the way you would pick a gift for someone you love. Then offer it without hurrying past it. Notice how long you usually spend on this, and how long you spend now.
