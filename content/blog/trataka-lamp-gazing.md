Trataka meditation is simple enough to try tonight. Set a lamp at eye level, about an arm's length away. Sit, and let your eyes rest on the flame until they begin to water. Then close them and watch what stays: a glowing afterimage that drifts, fades and returns. That is the practice. It asks nothing of you except that you look at one thing for a few minutes, which, for most of us, turns out to be surprisingly hard.

## What is trataka meditation?

Trataka means steady gazing. It is one of the six cleansing practices, the shatkarmas, described in the Hatha Yoga Pradipika, a classic manual of hatha yoga. The text describes gazing at a small point without blinking until the eyes fill with tears, and counts it among the practices that prepare body and mind for deeper meditation.

The object can be a dot on a wall, a small symbol, or the tip of the nose. But the most common, and the most traditional in homes, is a flame. A lamp is already part of daily worship for many families, so candle gazing meditation, or ghee lamp gazing, sits naturally beside the evening prayer.

The idea is less mysterious than it sounds. The eyes are one of the main channels through which attention is pulled around all day. When you give them one steady thing to rest on, the rest of the mind tends to follow. That is why many people find trataka for concentration works better than trying to concentrate directly.

## How do you do trataka at home?

Here is a simple way to begin.

Choose the lamp. A ghee lamp is traditional; a candle works too. Use a small flame, not a large one.

Prepare the room. It should be dark or dim, and free from draughts, so the flame burns steadily. A flickering flame is tiring to watch.

Place the lamp at eye level, about an arm's length from you. You should not have to tilt your head up or down.

Sit upright, on the floor or in a chair, with the spine easy and the body still.

Now gaze at the flame, especially the tip or the brightest part. Do not strain. Let the eyes be soft and open. The old texts talk about not blinking, but for a beginner, blink when you need to. Comfort matters more than purity of form.

After a minute or so, or when the eyes water, close them. You will see an afterimage of the flame. Hold your attention on it gently. When it fades, you can open the eyes and gaze again, or simply sit.

Start with one to three minutes in total. That is enough. Many people find that the stillness after closing the eyes is the real practice, and the gazing is just the way in.

## Who should skip or adapt it?

Flame gazing is not for everyone, and it is important to be honest about that.

If you have an eye condition, have had recent eye surgery, suffer from migraines, or have epilepsy or any history of seizures, avoid gazing at a flame and ask a doctor before trying any version. For some people, a steady light source can trigger problems.

There is an easy alternative. Gaze at a small black dot on a white card instead. It works on the same principle, with no flame and no flicker.

And for everyone: never push through discomfort. If the eyes sting badly, close them. If you feel dizzy, stop. The practice is supposed to settle you, not strain you.

If you want to extend the practice beyond the eyes, [likhita japa, writing a mantra](/blog/likhita-japa-writing-mantras), uses the hand the same way trataka uses the eyes. [Surya namaskar, the salutation to the Sun](/blog/surya-namaskar-meaning), is its morning counterpart: facing a larger flame. Where trataka sits between the two is clearer once you know [the difference between meditation and puja](/blog/meditation-vs-puja). And if the lamp has made you curious about the other small materials of home worship, try [turmeric in Hindu ritual](/blog/turmeric-in-hindu-ritual).

## The afterimage at bedtime

Three minutes each evening, before bed. Same lamp, same place, same time if you can. Gaze, close, watch the afterimage.

Then pay attention to the next day. Notice whether it is easier to stay with one thing: one conversation, one task, one page. Most people do not notice a dramatic change. They notice a small one, around day four or five, and it tends to stay.
