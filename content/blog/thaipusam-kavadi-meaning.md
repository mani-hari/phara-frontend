In a video my cousin sent from Palani, a man in a saffron veshti, the Tamil wrap-around cloth, climbs the hill steps with a decorated wooden arch across his shoulders, peacock feathers nodding above it, murmuring "vel, vel" with each step. I watched it on the morning train into the city, my own shoulders stiff with a laptop bag. The Thaipusam kavadi meaning is right there in that picture: a kavadi is a burden carried on the shoulders to Murugan as an offering, in memory of Idumban, who carried two hills for his teacher. It is a burden chosen, not a burden endured.

## What is Thaipusam?

Thaipusam falls when the Poosam star is in the sky in the Tamil month of Thai, near the full moon, usually in January or February. The tradition says this was the day Parvati gave Murugan the vel, the leaf-bladed spear he carries, before his battle with the demon Surapadman, a story told in [the meaning of Soorasamharam](/blog/soorasamharam-story-meaning).

It is one of the great festivals of the Tamil world. The hill temple at Palani, one of the [six abodes of Murugan](/blog/six-abodes-of-murugan), fills with pilgrims, many of whom have walked for days. In Malaysia, the climb up the steps of Batu Caves draws enormous crowds. Temples in Singapore, Mauritius, South Africa, and increasingly in North America, hold their own processions. Wherever Tamil families have gone, Thaipusam went with them.

## Thaipusam kavadi meaning

The story of the kavadi belongs to Palani. The sage Agastya, travelling south, asked his disciple Idumban to bring two hills from the north to his new home. Idumban tied them to either end of a pole, lifted it across his shoulders and set off. Near Palani he grew tired and set his load down to rest. When he tried to lift it again, it would not move.

On top of one hill stood a young boy, who refused to get off. Idumban, furious, fought him and lost. The boy was Murugan. Idumban's teacher and his family pleaded for him, and Murugan restored him and made him the guardian of the hill. Then he said that anyone who came to him carrying a burden on their shoulders as Idumban had would be received with special grace.

So the kavadi, the word means a carrying pole, is a small echo of those two hills. Some are simple wooden arches with two pots of milk hanging from either end, the paal kavadi. Some are elaborate frames covered with peacock feathers and flowers. Pilgrims usually fast for days or weeks beforehand, and at the temple the milk is offered to Murugan.

## Why do people carry a kavadi, and what can the rest of us carry?

People carry a kavadi to keep a vow: for a child's recovery, a job, a safe birth, or simply in thanks. For some, it is the fulfilment of a promise made years ago in a hospital corridor. The weight is real. So is the fasting before it. But what I notice, watching those videos, is the faces. They are tired and very calm.

The Bhagavad Gita has Krishna say that if someone offers him a leaf, a flower, a fruit or a little water with devotion, he accepts it. The offering does not have to be large. It has to be offered. I think the kavadi works the same way. The pole across the shoulders is not a punishment. It is a way of saying: this weight is for you.

The rest of us carry things too. A sick parent in another country. A job that is not going well. A child who is struggling. We carry them on the train, into meetings, into bed. Mostly we carry them resentfully, as something done to us. The kavadi suggests another way: the same weight, carried deliberately, with some intention attached. It does not make the load lighter. It changes how you walk. On the days when the weight feels like too much, [small rituals for anxious days](/blog/rituals-for-anxious-days) may help. If you would rather begin with hunger than weight, [the Skanda Sashti fast](/blog/skanda-sashti-fasting-guide) is Murugan's other great discipline.

## One small thing to carry into the week

Name one burden you are carrying this week. Say it out loud, or write it on a slip of paper and keep it in your pocket. Then, each morning, before you pick it up again, offer it: to Murugan, to whatever you pray to, or simply to the day. Carry it deliberately, as Idumban carried his hills. Notice, by Friday, whether your shoulders have changed.
