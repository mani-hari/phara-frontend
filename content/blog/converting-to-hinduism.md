A colleague asked me over coffee in the office kitchen, a little nervously, as if she were asking whether a club had a waiting list: can you convert to Hinduism? The short answer I gave her was yes, and that there is no single door. There is no central authority that keeps a register, no one ceremony every Hindu recognises, and no form to sign. Some groups hold a formal rite. Many people simply begin to practise and, over the years, find they belong.

She looked relieved, and then slightly puzzled. "So how do you know you are in?" That second question is the more interesting one, and I have been thinking about it on the train home ever since.

## Can you convert to Hinduism, formally?

For most of its history, Hinduism was a tradition people were born into rather than joined. There was no missionary structure and no creed to recite. And yet the tradition has always absorbed newcomers. Communities, families and whole regions came into its fold over centuries through practice, through marriage, through a temple built at a crossroads, through a teacher who simply accepted a student.

So when people ask how to become a Hindu, the honest answer is that it depends on whom you ask. There is no Hindu pope, and no council whose decision binds everyone. That can feel unsettling if you want a clear line. It can also feel freeing. The question of [whether Hinduism is a religion at all](/blog/is-hinduism-a-religion) sits underneath this: the word covers a family of traditions more than a single institution.

## What do different groups do?

Some organisations do have a formal rite, and a seeker should know about them.

- **The Arya Samaj** performs a ceremony called shuddhi, meaning purification, in which a newcomer takes part in a fire ritual, recites prayers and receives a Hindu name if they wish.
- **ISKCON**, the Krishna devotional movement, has initiation within its lineage, usually after a period of study, chanting and practice with the community.
- **Some Shaiva organisations** hold a name-giving rite, a namakarana, for adults who have prepared for it and want a formal marker.

Each of these is a door into a particular community, not into Hinduism as a whole. A person initiated in one lineage is welcome in most temples, but no ceremony is required to walk in, stand before the deity and fold your hands. Our local temple in New Jersey sees visitors of every background on a Saturday evening, and nobody at the door asks for papers.

## Is practising enough?

For many people, yes. I know a man in Ohio, raised Lutheran, who began keeping a small lamp on his bookshelf after a trip to South India. Then he learned one prayer. Then he started going to the temple for the big festivals, and eventually for the small ones too. Nobody ever performed a ceremony for him. When I asked if he considered himself Hindu, he shrugged and said, "The priest does. He asks for my star every time."

That shrug says something true about the tradition. Belonging has usually been measured by practice rather than paperwork: the lamp you light, the festivals you keep, the community you show up for, the questions you keep asking. The idea of an [ishta devata, a chosen personal deity](/blog/ishta-devata-meaning), means a newcomer can begin with the form of the divine that moves them and go from there. The broader conviction that [many paths lead to one truth](/blog/many-paths-one-truth) means the tradition has rarely felt the need to draw hard borders.

The Rig Veda has a line that people quote often: "Truth is one; the wise call it by many names." It was not written with converts in mind, but it explains why the question of membership has never been the centre of things.

## Where to begin

If you are drawn to the tradition, the practical steps are small. Visit a temple, ideally with someone who can explain what you are seeing; my notes for a [first visit to a Hindu temple in the US](/blog/first-visit-hindu-temple-usa) may help. Learn one prayer and say it daily. Keep one festival properly this season. Find a study group, a teacher or a Bala Vihar class if you have children.

Many people who describe themselves as [spiritual but not religious](/blog/spiritual-but-not-religious-hinduism) find that Hinduism asks very little of them at the start, and gives a great deal back once they stay.

One small thing to carry into this week: choose one practice and one community, and let the question of labels settle in its own time. My colleague, for the record, now brings her own tea-light to our office Diwali.
