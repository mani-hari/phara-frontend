A forehead can tell a story before its owner speaks. Three horizontal lines of grey ash. A white U with a red line down the middle. A single red dot. Each mark is a sentence about what the wearer has chosen to remember that morning. If you have ever wondered why Hindus apply tilak, the short answer is this: the mark sits at the point between the eyebrows, and wearing it is a daily act of remembering, and a quiet sign of which family of the tradition you come from.

## Why Hindus apply tilak on the forehead

Tilak is the general word for a mark on the forehead, made with ash, sandal paste, clay, turmeric or kumkum. The spot is not random. The space between the eyebrows is associated in yoga with the ajna chakra, the centre of attention and insight, sometimes called the third eye. Placing a mark there is like putting a small bookmark on the place where your attention is supposed to rest.

Grandmothers never explained it in those terms. They put a dot of kumkum on your forehead as you left the house, the way another family might straighten your collar. It meant: you have been blessed, now go. The story sounds like a matter of custom. It is really about beginning the day with an intention you can see in the mirror.

The mark also says where you belong. Walk into a temple in Tamil Nadu and you can often tell, from the foreheads alone, who is a Shaiva devotee of Shiva and who is a Vaishnava devotee of Vishnu. The difference between the tripundra and namam is a difference of lineage, not of rank.

## Vibhuti: the ash that Shiva wears

Vibhuti is sacred ash, usually made from burned cow dung and offered in Shiva temples. Its meaning begins with Shiva himself, who is described in the Puranas as smeared with the ash of the cremation ground. Devotees wear it as the tripundra, three horizontal lines drawn with three fingers across the forehead, sometimes with a red dot in the centre.

There is a story behind the ash, told in the Shiva Purana and retold by the poet Kalidasa. The gods needed Shiva to marry Parvati, and Shiva was lost in meditation. So they sent Kama, the god of desire, to shoot him with a flower arrow. Shiva opened his third eye and burned Kama to ash on the spot.

The story says Shiva destroyed desire. The story is really about impermanence. Everything that burns becomes ash, and ash cannot burn again. A devotee who wears vibhuti is wearing that fact in plain sight: this body, too, will one day be ash. It sounds grim. In practice it is oddly freeing. It is hard to hold a grudge with the end of all things written on your face.

## Namam and kumkum: feet and fire

The Vaishnava mark is the urdhva pundra, the upward line. In the South it is often called namam: two white lines of clay joined at the bottom in a U or a Y shape, with a red or yellow line in the middle. The white lines are commonly read as the feet of Vishnu, and the central line as Lakshmi, who is never far from him. The mark points upward, as if drawing the eye from the earth toward the divine.

Kumkum is turmeric turned red with slaked lime. As a dot on the forehead, it is associated with Shakti, the Goddess as power, and in many regions it is worn by married women in the parting of the hair or between the brows. To follow the colour back to its root, read about [turmeric's place in Hindu ritual](/blog/turmeric-in-hindu-ritual). Kumkum on the forehead meaning differs from region to region and community to community, and many unmarried women and men wear it too. Customs vary, and no single rule covers them all.

## What does the mark ask of you?

If you are visiting a temple for the first time, you will often be offered vibhuti or kumkum by the priest. It is fine to accept and apply a little with your ring finger; the guide to a [first visit to a Hindu temple in the US](/blog/first-visit-hindu-temple-usa) covers the etiquette. The mark belongs to the same family of small acts as [ringing the temple bell](/blog/why-ring-a-temple-bell) and [walking around the shrine](/blog/pradakshina-meaning).

By evening, the mark has faded. Sweat, a hand brushed across the forehead, a long day. That may be the point. It is not a tattoo. It asks to be renewed every morning, which is exactly what attention asks of us too.
