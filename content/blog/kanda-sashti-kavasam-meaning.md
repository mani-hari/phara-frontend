A kavasam is armour. The Kanda Sashti Kavasam, the Tamil hymn heard in so many homes on Tuesday evenings, asks Murugan's vel, his spear, to guard you piece by piece, from the crown of the head to the soles of the feet. The Kanda Sashti Kavasam meaning seems simple enough: protect me. The story says protection. The hymn, I think, is really about attention.

## Who wrote the Kanda Sashti Kavasam?

The hymn was composed by Devaraya Swamigal, a devotee of Murugan who lived in the nineteenth century. The story often told is that he suffered a long illness and prayed at Tiruchendur, the shore temple where Murugan defeated the demon Surapadman, and that the kavasam came to him in the aftermath. Tellings vary on the details, and on where he first sang it. What is agreed is that he did not stop at one. He wrote six kavasams, one for each of the [six abodes of Murugan](/blog/six-abodes-of-murugan), and the one everyone knows is the Tiruchendur kavasam, tied to the six-day festival of Skanda Sashti.

Look at who wrote it. Not an ancient sage with a Sanskrit lineage, but a relatively recent Tamil devotee who knew what it was to be frightened and unwell. That may be why it still sounds like someone talking, not someone reciting. Tamil devotion has other hymns born under pressure; [the Abhirami Anthathi](/blog/abhirami-anthathi-meaning) was sung, the story says, over a fire.

## Kanda Sashti Kavasam meaning, verse by verse in spirit

The hymn opens with a short invocation promising that those who praise Murugan will see their troubles and hard karma fall away. Then it calls on him directly: the young god with six faces, riding the peacock, coming swiftly with the vel in his hand. The rhythm is fast and insistent, a summons rather than a meditation.

Then comes the long heart of the hymn, the part that gives it its name. "Kaakka kaakka", protect, protect. The poet asks the vel to guard the head and the hair, the forehead and the brows, the eyes, the ears, the nose, the cheeks, the lips, the tongue, the teeth. Then the neck, the chest, the shoulders, the arms, the fingers, the heart, the belly, the back, the joints one after another, down to the feet. Nothing is left out.

After the body comes the world outside it. The hymn asks for protection from fear, from harmful spirits as the tradition pictures them, from poison, from wild animals, from illness and from enemies. It closes by returning to praise and surrender. Murugan's own story, and why Sashti matters so much, is in [the Skanda Sashti fasting guide](/blog/skanda-sashti-fasting-guide).

## Why name every part of the body?

Here is what the hymn is really doing. A frightened person does not feel fear in a particular place. It is everywhere at once, a fog. The kavasam does not let you stay in the fog. It walks you through the body, one named part at a time, and asks you to place each part under protection.

Anyone who has done a body scan in a relaxation class will recognise the method. Each named place receives attention, and then prayer. The fear that was vague becomes something you can address, one piece at a time. By the time you reach the feet, you have touched your whole self with care. That is not nothing. For many people it is exactly what an anxious evening needs, and it belongs with the other small [rituals for anxious days](/blog/rituals-for-anxious-days).

The story says the vel guards the body. The hymn is really teaching you to guard it yourself, with your own attention, and to let Murugan hold what you cannot.

## How to begin

Listen once through slowly with a translation beside you. The Tamil is rapid, and the first time you will lose the thread. That is fine. Follow the English, and let the sound carry you. By the third or fourth listening you will start to hear which part of the body the poet has reached.

Many families recite the kavasam during Skanda Sashti, and on Tuesdays, which are Murugan's day; [Tuesday worship of Murugan and Durga](/blog/tuesday-worship-murugan-durga) explains the custom. If you are drawn to Palani, where Murugan stands as the renouncer with his staff, [the Palani Murugan temple](/blog/palani-murugan-temple) tells that story.

But any quiet evening will do. Sit, press play, and follow the poet down from your crown to your feet. You may find the armour was attention all along.
