Most poojas begin with the breath, not the flowers. Before the lamp is lit and the first name is said, the tradition asks you to sit and breathe. Pranayama before puja can be as short as three breaths, and it works best that way. Try it tomorrow: three slow breaths before you strike the match, and then notice how the next five minutes feel. Most people find they are actually present for the prayer, instead of arriving halfway through it.

## Why does worship start with pranayama?

Pranayama means the regulation of prana, the breath and the life-force it carries. In sandhyavandanam, the daily prayer at the junctions of the day, and in many home poojas, a short pranayama comes right after achamanam, the sipping of water from the palm, and before the sankalpam, the spoken statement of intention.

The order is not accidental. You first cleanse, then settle, then declare what you are about to do. A settled breath is treated as the doorway into worship. Try saying a sankalpam while you are still breathing like someone who has just run up the stairs. It does not land. The words come out, but you are somewhere else.

The body knows this before the mind does. When the breath is fast and high in the chest, attention scatters. When it slows and drops, attention gathers. The tradition put a few breaths at the entrance so that the person who walks in is the one who is actually going to pray.

## Pranayama before puja: three breaths that settle

Here is a simple pranayama for beginners. Three breaths. Each one is different.

Sit upright on the floor or a chair, spine easy, hands in the lap. Let the eyes close or rest on the lamp.

1. The first breath is plain and slow. Breathe in through the nose, unhurried. Then breathe out, also through the nose, and let the out-breath be longer than the in-breath. Longer out-breaths tend to calm the body.
2. The second breath is one round of nadi shodhana, alternate-nostril breathing. With the right hand, close the right nostril with the thumb and breathe in through the left. Close the left nostril with the ring finger, release the thumb, and breathe out through the right. Breathe in through the right, close it, and breathe out through the left. That is one round.
3. The third breath is held gently. Breathe in slowly, pause for a comfortable moment at the top, without force, and then let the breath out slowly.

That is all. Then open your eyes and light the lamp. Breathing before prayer like this fits inside a minute, and it changes the quality of everything after it. The same breath carries well into a round of japa; [why 108 is sacred](/blog/why-108-is-sacred) explains the count.

## What if breathing exercises make you uncomfortable?

Some people feel dizzy or anxious when they focus on the breath. That is common and nothing to be ashamed of.

Never strain. Never hold the breath forcefully. If the held breath in step three feels tight, skip it. If you are pregnant, have high blood pressure, a heart condition, or any breathing difficulty, skip retention entirely and ask your doctor before trying anything more than plain slow breathing.

And know that plain slow breathing is enough. Three unhurried breaths, in and out through the nose, do most of the work. The tradition built elaborate forms of pranayama, but it also knew that most householders had a few minutes before work and a child asking for breakfast. The simple version was always allowed.

If silence helps, [a short vow of silence](/blog/mouna-vratam-silence) pairs well with this. And if you want to see how breath and attention show up even at the dinner table, [eating with your hands](/blog/eating-with-your-hands) is an unexpected companion. The difference between [meditation and puja](/blog/meditation-vs-puja) is smaller than people think, and the breath is where they meet.

## Three breaths, then the lamp

Three breaths, then the lamp. Every day, for seven days. Do not add anything else. Do not extend it. The practice is small on purpose.

At the end of the week, try one day without the breaths, going straight to the lamp. Notice whether the pooja feels different. Most people find the answer is obvious, and they do not skip the breaths again.
