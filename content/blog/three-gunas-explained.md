The three gunas are the three qualities the tradition sees in every mind and body: tamas, heaviness; rajas, restlessness; and sattva, clarity. You can see all three in a single day. There is the heaviness before your first coffee, when the body wants to stay exactly where it is. There is the rush after lunch, the inbox, the calls, the feeling of being pulled in six directions. And there is, if you are lucky, a clear hour late in the evening when the house goes quiet and you can actually think. The tradition named these states so you could learn to notice them, and noticing them is most of the work.

## What are the three gunas?

Guna means quality, or strand, like the strands twisted together into a rope. The idea comes from Sankhya, one of the oldest schools of Indian philosophy, and it is laid out plainly in chapter 14 of the Bhagavad Gita, Gunatraya Vibhaga Yoga, the chapter on the division of the three qualities.

Sattva, rajas and tamas are the three. Sattva is clarity and balance: the mind that sees what is in front of it. Rajas is activity and restlessness: drive, ambition, agitation, the engine running hot. Tamas is inertia and heaviness: dullness, sleep, the wish to not move at all.

The Gita's point is that every mind and every body is a mix of all three, all the time. Nobody is purely sattvic. The proportions shift over a day and over a lifetime.

## The three gunas in your day

Watch yourself for a single day and you will see the strands change places.

Morning, for most people, starts in tamas. The body is heavy, the mind foggy. Then rajas takes over, often too much of it: the commute, the meetings, the constant switching. Pockets of sattva appear in unexpected places, a walk without the phone, the first minutes after a shower, sitting in front of the lamp at dusk.

Many things tilt the balance. Sleep does, perhaps most of all. A short night makes the next day swing between tamas and frantic rajas. Screens are almost pure rajas: stimulating, fragmenting, hard to put down. Company matters too. Some people leave you clearer and some leave you wired.

Food is the part people ask about most. Chapter 17 of the Gita describes foods by guna, and that lens runs through temple kitchens and grandmothers' rules. Treat it as a traditional way of paying attention, not a diet plan. [The sattvic diet](/blog/sattvic-diet-explained) carries that lens into the kitchen.

## Is tamas bad and sattva good?

This is where many people get the gunas wrong. They turn them into a moral ladder, with tamas at the bottom and sattva at the top, and then feel guilty every time they are tired.

Each guna has its job. Tamas is what lets you sleep. Without it, you would lie awake all night with a racing mind. Rajas is what gets things built: houses, careers, dinners for twelve. Sattva is what lets you see clearly what to build and why. A life of pure sattva would be serene and would not get the laundry done.

The Gita goes further. Its ideal is not the person stuck in sattva but the gunatita, one who has gone beyond all three. Krishna says that even sattva binds, through attachment to happiness and knowledge. The aim is to see the qualities moving through you without being run by any of them. That is not a demand for perfection. It is an invitation to watch. If you worry that this sounds like fate, the question of [astrology and free will](/blog/astrology-and-free-will) raises a similar knot, and the answer is similar: tendencies are not verdicts.

The same idea sits underneath [Advaita explained simply](/blog/advaita-simply-explained): the witness who watches the weather is not the weather.

## Name the guna, three times a day

Three times a day, morning, mid-afternoon and evening, stop for ten seconds. Ask yourself one question: which guna is running the show right now?

Do not try to change it. That is the important part. If it is tamas at three in the afternoon, just say "tamas" and carry on. If it is rajas at eleven at night, say "rajas". You are not grading yourself. You are building the habit of seeing.

By the end of the week, most people notice patterns they never saw before. Rajas after certain meetings. Tamas after certain meals. Sattva in odd, small places. Once you can see the pattern, you can start to work with it, one choice at a time.
