The annaprashan ceremony takes about ten minutes and one spoonful of sweet rice. Everything else, the silk, the guests, the feast, the photographer, is optional. Annaprashan, literally "the eating of grain", is the samskara that marks a baby's first taste of solid food, and families across India still keep it, from Kerala to Bengal. Abroad, it can be as simple as a lamp, a prayer and a small bowl of payasam on your kitchen table, and it can still be one of the most tender mornings of the first year.

## When is the annaprashan ceremony done?

Commonly around the sixth month, when many babies begin to show interest in food. Some traditions go by the child's sex: even months, such as the sixth or eighth, for boys, and odd months, such as the fifth or seventh, for girls. Others simply wait until the baby is ready.

The day itself is usually chosen with the family priest or a panchangam, the traditional almanac, looking for an auspicious day and time. There is more on how this is chosen in [what a muhurtham is](/blog/what-is-a-muhurtham). If you are abroad and there is no family priest nearby, a temple priest can help, or an elder in India can check the panchangam for you.

Annaprashan is one of the classic samskaras, the rites of passage that mark a life; there is a full list in [the 16 samskaras explained](/blog/sixteen-samskaras-explained).

## The ceremony itself

The ceremony itself is very small.

1. A short prayer to Ganesha, so that the beginning goes smoothly.
2. A lamp lit before the family deity.
3. A little sweet rice, usually payasam or kheer, placed in a silver bowl and offered to the deity first.
4. The baby is fed a tiny taste from a silver spoon or a ring dipped in the payasam.

Who feeds the baby varies. In many families the father gives the first taste. In Bengal, where the ceremony is called mukhe bhaat, "rice in the mouth", the maternal uncle traditionally does it, often with an elaborate spread of dishes placed before the baby. In Kerala it is called choroonu, and many families travel to Guruvayur to have it done in the presence of Krishna; [the Guruvayur temple guide](/blog/guruvayur-temple-guide) explains why so many first meals happen there.

If you want to make the payasam yourself, the recipe for [paal payasam as naivedyam](/blog/paal-payasam-naivedyam) is a good place to start. For the baby, only a tiny amount of the soft rice, and nothing that has not already been cleared for your child.

## Doing it abroad

Many families now hold the annaprashan at home or at a temple in the US. Both work.

At home, you need very little: a clean space, a lamp, a picture or small idol of Ganesha and your family deity, one dish, and the baby in something comfortable. A few elders can join by video, and grandparents in India often want to be the ones to say the first blessing. Time the call for their morning if you can.

At a temple, the priest will usually perform a short puja and guide the family. Some families combine it with [the baby's first temple visit](/blog/babys-first-temple-visit), which makes a lovely morning.

Many families also follow a custom where a tray of objects is placed before the baby after the first bite: a book, a pen, a coin, some jewellery, a little soil or rice. Whatever the baby reaches for is said, playfully, to hint at their future. Keep it playful. It is a game for the grandparents, not a prophecy.

One practical note. Before the ceremony, check with your paediatrician about first foods and anything your baby should avoid. The ritual is flexible; the baby's needs come first.

## Try this

Whatever the scale, from a quiet kitchen to a full hall, add one moment. Just before the first bite, pause. Ask everyone in the room, and everyone on the screen, to say the child's name once, together.

Notice what happens. The noise stops. The baby looks up. For a second, everyone's attention is on this small person and their first taste of the world. That is the whole ceremony, really. The rest is lunch.
