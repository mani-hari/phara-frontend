Two brothers are told to race around the world. Kartikeya, the warrior, leaps on his peacock and is gone over the horizon. Ganesha, round-bellied and riding a mouse, looks at the road, then quietly walks three times around his parents, Shiva and Parvati. "You are my world," he says. He wins. That, in short, is why Ganesha is worshipped first: in the Puranic story, Shiva granted him the honour of being invoked before every other deity. The story says the winner was clever. The story is really about what "the world" means.

## Why is Ganesha worshipped first?

The race story appears in several Puranic tellings, and in South India it is often told with a mango. The sage Narada brings a single fruit of wisdom to Kailasa, Shiva's mountain home, and the brothers race for it. Kartikeya circles the earth; Ganesha circles his parents; the mango goes to Ganesha. Kartikeya, sulking, walks off to the hill of Palani, which is how that temple story begins.

Whichever version your grandmother told, the ending is the same. Shiva decrees that no ritual, no journey, no new venture will begin without first calling on Ganesha. So in every homam, the Vedic fire ritual, in every wedding and every puja, the first name spoken is his.

Notice what the story rewards. Not speed, not strength, not effort. It rewards seeing clearly. Kartikeya takes the instruction literally and goes looking for the world out there. Ganesha asks what the world actually is for him, and finds it standing in front of him. That is a strange and lovely thing to put at the start of every beginning: before you run, check what you are running towards.

## The guardian at the threshold

There is an older Ganesha story, from the Shiva Purana, that explains the elephant head. Parvati shapes a boy from turmeric paste and sets him at her door while she bathes. Shiva comes home, is refused entry by a child he does not know, and in anger strikes off his head. When Parvati's grief threatens to undo the world, Shiva restores the boy with the head of the first creature found, an elephant, and makes him lord of all his followers.

Ganesha lost that fight. Read it again and it is about doorways. Ganesha is the one who stands at the threshold and decides who passes. That is why he is called Vighneshvara, the lord of obstacles, and why the meaning of "remover of obstacles" cuts both ways. A doorkeeper can open the door or keep it shut. Praying to him is not asking for a life without obstacles. It is asking the doorkeeper to let you through, and perhaps to stop you when you are about to walk into the wrong room.

Every beginning is a doorway. A new job, a new house, a new school year, a marriage. Ganesha stands at all of them.

## Why Ganesha is worshipped first in a homam, a wedding or a new home

In practice, the Ganapathy prayer works as a pause. Before the fire is lit in a homam, before the wedding vows, before a family steps into a new home, someone stops and says his name. In the [griha pravesh puja steps](/blog/griha-pravesh-puja-steps), it is the very first act at the door. In a US apartment, as this guide to [a housewarming puja in the USA](/blog/blessing-a-new-home-abroad) describes, it may be the only formal prayer of the day, and that is enough.

The same pause opens daily worship. Even a [five-minute daily puja](/blog/five-minute-daily-puja) usually begins with a line to Ganesha before anything else. Students say it before exams; this [calm prayer before exams](/blog/prayer-before-exams) starts in much the same place. Families say it when they name a child, when they sign a lease, when they start a long drive.

The broken tusk belongs here too. Tradition says Ganesha snapped off his own tusk to keep writing when his pen failed, as the sage Vyasa dictated the Mahabharata without stopping. The scribe who sacrifices something to finish the work is a fitting guardian of beginnings.

## The ten-second pause

Before your next beginning, try this. Stand at the door, literal or not. Close your eyes for ten seconds. Say Ganesha's name, or simply say to yourself what you hope for from what you are about to start. Then step through. If you want something steadier than a pause, many families keep [Sankatahara Chaturthi, the monthly Ganesha fast](/blog/sankatahara-chaturthi-fast), on the fourth night after each full moon.

That pause is the oldest part of the ritual. The rest was built around it.
