Here is how to chant a mantra if you have never done it: pick one short traditional mantra, sit upright, breathe naturally, and repeat it for five to ten minutes at the same time every day. That is all. You do not need a guru or a cave. You need a mat, a mantra and seven mornings. Below is the plan, day by day, and what to do with the one problem everyone has.

## How to chant a mantra: the basics

Posture first, because the body is the instrument. Sit on the floor on a folded blanket or cushion, or on a chair with both feet flat. Keep the spine upright but not rigid, as if a thread were lifting the crown of your head. Rest the hands on the thighs. Close the eyes or let them rest on a lamp.

Breath next. Do not control it. Let it be natural for a minute before you start. Once you begin, the mantra will lengthen the out-breath by itself.

Voice. Chant at a pace where every syllable is clear. Slow is better than fast. Pitch does not matter; comfort does.

Choosing a mantra. For chanting a mantra for beginners at home, choose something short and widely taught. Om Namah Shivaya, "I bow to Shiva", is traditionally open to anyone and easy to pronounce; its meaning is unpacked in [the Om Namah Shivaya guide](/blog/om-namah-shivaya-meaning). Learn the pronunciation from a reliable recording or an elder. How to chant a mantra correctly? Get the sounds right once, carefully, and then stop worrying about perfection. Tradition values precise sound, and it equally values steady, attentive repetition.

## The seven-day plan

Same time each day, ideally morning, before the phone.

Day one: aloud. Chant clearly for five minutes. Notice the vibration in your chest and throat.

Day two: aloud again, but slower. Let each repetition finish completely before starting the next.

Day three: whisper. The sound becomes breathier and more intimate. Notice how attention changes when the voice drops.

Day four: silent. Repeat the mantra only in the mind. This is harder. The mind wanders more. Keep going.

Day five: with a mala. How to use a mala for chanting: hold the string of 108 beads in your right hand, draped over the middle finger, and move one bead toward you with the thumb for each repetition. Many traditions avoid using the index finger. When you reach the larger guru bead, turn the mala around rather than crossing over it. Choose aloud, whisper or silent, whichever suited you best.

Day six: with a lamp, and longer. Light a small lamp before you begin and chant for ten minutes. Let your eyes rest on the flame between repetitions.

Day seven: rest and notice. Chant for five minutes in whatever way felt most natural, then sit silently for five more. Do not chant. Just observe what remains.

## What if my mind keeps wandering?

It will. On day one you will be planning lunch by the third repetition. On day four you may forget the mantra entirely and find yourself replaying a conversation from last week.

This is not failure. It is the practice. The whole point of a mantra is to give the mind a place to return to, and every time you notice you have drifted and come back, you are doing exactly what the practice asks. A thousand returns in a session is not a bad session. It is a thousand repetitions of attention. [What a mantra actually is](/blog/what-is-a-mantra) explains why this return matters so much.

If the wandering is intense, chant aloud and a little faster for a minute; the voice pulls the mind back. If you are sleepy, open your eyes and sit straighter.

## After day seven

Take a notebook and write three lines. What changed in your mornings? Which mode, aloud, whisper or silent, felt most like yours? What did you notice about your mind by day seven that you did not notice on day one?

Then decide whether to continue for a month. If you do, keep it simple: same mantra, same place, same time. Some people fold chanting into a [five-minute daily puja](/blog/five-minute-daily-puja); others save a longer session for a night like [the Shivaratri vigil at home](/blog/shivaratri-vigil-at-home).
