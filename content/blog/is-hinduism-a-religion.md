Is Hinduism a religion or way of life? The honest answer is both, and the question itself shows how awkwardly the word religion fits a tradition that never had one founder, one book or one headquarters. Ask a grandmother and she will not answer at all. She will tell you to wash your feet before entering the prayer room, and to take some sundal to the neighbours. Ask the question again and you realise that was the answer.

## Is Hinduism a religion or way of life?

On one side, Hinduism looks very much like a religion. It has scriptures, temples, priests, rites of passage, a vast family of gods and goddesses, and ideas about the soul, rebirth and liberation. Millions of people pray, fast and go on pilgrimage. By any ordinary definition, that is religion.

On the other side, much of what Hindus actually do has little to do with belief. It is food: what is cooked on which day, what is offered first, who eats before whom. It is festivals: the lamps of Deepavali, the kolam of Pongal, the dolls of Navratri. It is family custom, handed down without explanation. It is ethics: how to treat a guest, a teacher, an elder. A person can do all of these things faithfully without ever deciding what they think about God.

Most Hindus do not experience these as two separate things. The lamp at dusk is a prayer and a household habit at the same time. The meal offered first to the deity is worship and dinner. The distinction between religion and way of life, which seems so clear in the question, blurs the moment you step into a Hindu kitchen.

## Where does the word Hindu come from?

The word itself is not originally a religious one. The ancient Persians called the great river of the northwest the Hindu, their pronunciation of the Sanskrit Sindhu, the river we now call the Indus. The people living beyond it were the Hindus, the river people. The Greeks borrowed the word and gave us India. For a very long time, Hindu was a geographical name, a description of where people lived rather than what they believed.

Only much later did Hindu come to mean a religion, gathered up under one name by outsiders and then gradually accepted by insiders. Many Hindus prefer an older self-description: sanatana dharma. Sanatana means eternal, without beginning. Dharma is harder to translate; it means order, duty, the right way of things, the law that holds the world together. Sanatana dharma, put simply, means the eternal order. It describes a way the world works, not a creed you sign. If you want to follow that word into ordinary days, start with [dharma in everyday life](/blog/dharma-meaning-in-everyday-life).

## Why no single founder or book?

Does Hinduism have a founder? No. There is no moment when it began, no prophet who announced it, no council that fixed its doctrine. It grew, the way a language grows, over thousands of years and across a subcontinent.

Its texts are less a single book than a library. The Vedas, the oldest hymns. The Upanishads, which ask what the self is and what is ultimately real; the piece on [atman and Brahman](/blog/atman-and-brahman) begins there. The epics, the Ramayana and Mahabharata, with the Bhagavad Gita inside the second. The Puranas, full of stories of gods. The Tamil hymns of the Alvars and Nayanmars, sung in temples to this day. Different families, different regions and different schools lean on different shelves of that library.

That is why Hindus can disagree so widely and still recognise each other. A Vaishnava in Srirangam, a Shakta in Kolkata and a family in New Jersey that keeps only Deepavali and a Friday lamp all share what one might call a family resemblance: overlapping habits, stories and ideas, with no single one required. The piece on [whether Hindus believe in one God](/blog/do-hindus-believe-in-one-god) shows how the same pattern works for the divine itself.

## What should you say when someone asks?

If a friend or colleague asks you whether Hinduism is a religion, you do not need a lecture. Try answering with what you actually do. The lamp you light. The meal you cook on a festival day. The prayer you say before an exam. The way your family greets an elder. Describe that honestly, and let the question stay a little open. It is the best definition available, and it is truer than any textbook.
