Most of us cook while doing three other things: a podcast in one ear, a phone propped against the spice tin, a child asking about homework. Cooking as offering asks for the opposite. One pot, full attention, and a meal made for someone other than yourself first. The tradition calls the result naivedyam, food offered to the deity before anyone eats, and the practice is simpler than it sounds. Try it for one dish and see what happens to the cook.

## What does cooking as offering mean?

In the ninth chapter of the Bhagavad Gita, Krishna says: whatever you do, whatever you eat, whatever you offer, whatever you give, do it as an offering to me. It is one of the most practical lines in the whole text, because it does not ask you to go anywhere or buy anything. It asks you to change the direction of an ordinary act.

The kitchen in a Hindu home has always carried this idea. Annapurna, the goddess of food and nourishment, is said to live there. In many houses the stove is the second shrine, and older women would not enter it without bathing. Temple kitchens, where [prasadam is made and then shared](/blog/why-prasadam-is-shared), are run with the same seriousness as the sanctum.

The shift is subtle but real. When you cook for yourself, you are the customer. When you cook for the divine, you become the one who serves, and then, when the food comes back to you as prasadam, you are fed. You are no longer eating your own dinner. You are receiving something.

## How to prepare naivedyam: the kitchen practices

Families differ, but most share a few habits when they prepare naivedyam.

1. Bathe first, or at least wash hands, feet and face, and wear clean clothes.
2. Start with a clean stove and clean vessels. Many families keep separate vessels only for offerings.
3. Leave out onion and garlic, a custom many households follow for food meant for the deity. The reasons, and the folk tale told about them, are in [why onion and garlic are avoided](/blog/why-no-onion-garlic).
4. Keep the recipe simple: rice, a little dal, ghee, jaggery, fruit. Pongal, payasam and curd rice are classic choices.
5. Hum, chant or stay quiet instead of scrolling. A name repeated softly works well.
6. Pay attention to your own mood. The tradition says the cook's state of mind goes into the food. Whether or not you believe that literally, you know it is true of any meal made in anger.

That is it. None of these steps is about cooking skill. All of them are about attention.

## Why not taste the food before offering it to God?

This is the custom that surprises people most. In many homes, the cook does not taste the naivedyam at all before it is offered. Not a spoonful of the payasam to check the sugar, not a grain of rice to check the salt.

The traditional reason is that the first taste belongs to the deity. You would not take a bite from a plate before handing it to an honoured guest. But look at what the practice does to the cook. If you cannot taste, you have to use every other sense. You smell the ghee to know when the cashews are golden. You watch the colour of the jaggery syrup. You listen to the sound of the mustard seeds. You rely on experience and on trust. Many cooks say this rule is what made them good cooks.

Not tasting food before offering to God is, in the end, a small practice of restraint. It trains patience, and it trains attention.

After the offering, a small portion is placed before the lamp, a few moments pass, and then the family eats. The food is the same food. But it is now prasadam, and most people find they eat it differently.

## Try it with one dish

Choose one dish this week. Something simple: [sakkarai pongal](/blog/sakkarai-pongal-for-pooja), a pot of curd rice, a banana sliced into milk. Wash first. Put the phone in another room. Cook it without tasting and without a screen. When it is done, offer a small portion at your lamp, sit for a minute, and then eat. Notice how it tastes afterwards, and notice how you feel about the people you serve it to. The prayer that goes with the meal is the [Brahmarpanam before meals](/blog/brahmarpanam-prayer-before-meals), a single verse from the Gita. And on a quiet day such as a [Friday Lakshmi pooja](/blog/friday-lakshmi-pooja), this practice fits right in.
