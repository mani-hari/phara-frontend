In Chennai my mother still puts a small ball of rice on the wall before lunch, and waits. When a crow takes it, she eats. When none comes, she eats anyway, but a little more slowly. If you have ever asked why Hindus feed crows before the family eats, here is the short answer: the crow is traditionally seen as a messenger of the ancestors, and feeding it is part of a daily offering to all living creatures, made before we feed ourselves.

## Why feed the crows before the family eats?

The crow has a curious place in the tradition. In many South Indian homes it is seen as a messenger of the pitrus, the ancestors, who are said to come in that form to receive what the family offers. That is why crows are fed with special care on days of remembrance: new moon days, the annual shraddham, the fortnight of Mahalaya. [The new moon and the ancestors](/blog/amavasya-and-ancestors) explains the thinking, and [the annual shraddham](/blog/annual-shraddham-meaning) describes the rite itself. The belief linking crows and ancestors is old and widely held, though families explain it in different ways.

The crow is also the vahana, the vehicle, of Shani, the planet associated with time and discipline. On Saturdays some families make a point of feeding crows for that reason.

But the everyday practice is simpler than any of this. It is called kaka bali, the crow's portion. The name means just that: a small share of the day's cooking, usually plain rice with a little ghee or dal, set aside and placed outside before the family sits down. My grandmother in Thanjavur did it. My mother does it. She calls it the first plate.

## Part of a daily offering

Feeding crows belongs to a larger idea. The Dharma Shastras, the old texts on household duty, describe five daily offerings for a householder, the pancha maha yajna. One is to the gods, one to the sages through study, one to the ancestors, one to human guests, and one to all other creatures. That last is the bhuta yajna: feeding animals and birds before feeding yourself.

Feeding birds, as the tradition describes it, is not charity in the ordinary sense. It is a way of remembering that your meal is part of a much larger web of eating and being eaten. Birds, dogs and cows are the traditional guests. The cow gets the first roti (the reasons are in [why Hindus honour the cow](/blog/go-puja-cow-worship-meaning)). The crow gets the ball of rice. The dog, in some homes, gets the last roti of the night.

Many families say [the brahmarpanam prayer before meals](/blog/brahmarpanam-prayer-before-meals), a Sanskrit verse that treats the whole act of eating as an offering. Kaka bali is that prayer done with the hands. It sits within the wider idea of [dana, the Hindu view of giving](/blog/dana-in-hinduism).

## What if the crows do not come here?

When we first moved to New Jersey, I assumed there would be no crows. There are plenty. They are larger than the Chennai crows, and more suspicious. There are also starlings, sparrows, blue jays and the occasional cardinal, which my son is convinced is the most beautiful bird in the world.

So the practice travels. A small dish of plain cooked rice, or grain, on the patio or balcony railing. No salt, no spices, nothing fried, nothing sweet. A little water nearby in summer. Most birds are happy with it.

A few cautions. Check local rules about feeding wildlife; some towns and apartment complexes discourage it, especially where it attracts rodents or geese. Put out only a small amount, and clear away what is not eaten by evening. If feeding outside is not allowed, a bird feeder with seed is a gentle alternative, or you can set aside the portion and add it to your compost with a moment of thanks.

And some days, like my mother's, no crow comes. That is fine too. The offering was made. You eat a little more slowly.

## One small thing for this week

Before you eat, put aside one spoonful of your meal for a creature outside. Just one. Take it out, set it down, and come back to the table. It takes less time than checking your phone, and it changes how the first bite tastes. If it becomes a habit, you may find yourself doing it on birthdays and festivals too; a column on [giving on your birthday](/blog/giving-on-birthdays) grew out of the same instinct.
