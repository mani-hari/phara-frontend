Record your grandmother chanting this week. The tune she learned as a child, with its small regional turns and the places where she takes a breath, is not in any app. It lives in one voice. Learning slokas from grandparents is one of the most direct ways a tradition passes on, and a phone recording, made carefully, lets that teaching continue long after the lesson. You do not need equipment. You need a quiet room, a willing elder, and the discipline to actually practise.

## What should you ask them to chant?

Start with what they already say every day, because that is what they know most deeply.

- **The morning prayers.** The Suprabhatam, sung to wake the deity. Many grandparents know it by heart without ever having read it.
- **The long stotras.** The [Vishnu Sahasranamam](/blog/vishnu-sahasranamam-for-beginners), the thousand names of Vishnu, or the [Lalitha Sahasranamam](/blog/lalitha-sahasranamam-meaning) for the Goddess. These take a while, so ask for them in parts.
- **The Tamil hymns.** The [Kanda Sashti Kavasam](/blog/kanda-sashti-kavasam-meaning), the armour-prayer to Murugan, or Abhirami Anthathi. The tunes vary enormously between families.
- **The lullabies.** Nobody writes these down. They disappear first.
- **The one they turn to when worried.** Ask them directly: "What do you chant when you can't sleep?" The answer is often surprising, and it is the one you will want later.

## How to record it well

A few small things make the difference between a recording you treasure and one you cannot bear to listen to.

1. **A quiet room.** Turn off the fan and the television. Close the window if there is traffic.
2. **Morning, when the voice is fresh.** Most elders chant best after bath and before breakfast, which is often when they chant anyway.
3. **The phone a hand's width away**, on a table, not held. Held phones pick up every movement.
4. **One prayer per file**, named with their name and the prayer: "Paati, Vishnu Sahasranamam, part one." In twenty years you will be glad of the labels.
5. **Then ask the questions.** What does it mean? Who taught you? When did you chant it most? Record the answers too. The story around a prayer is often as precious as the prayer.

If your grandparents live in India, this can all happen on a video call, though the sound is better if someone on their end records it on a phone in the room and sends it to you. There is more on keeping that connection alive in [grandparents on video calls](/blog/grandparents-on-video-calls).

## Learning slokas from grandparents: how do you actually practise?

Here is where most recordings end up unheard in a folder. Collecting is easy. Practising takes a plan.

Try this. One line a day. Only one.

- In the morning, play the recording of that line three times. Listen to the tune as well as the words.
- Chant along with it three times.
- Then chant it once without the recording.
- The next day, add the next line, and begin by chanting the previous one from memory.

That is slow, and slow is the point. A line a day for a year is several full stotras. More importantly, your body learns the rhythm, the breath, the places to pause, the way your grandmother learned it. The breath matters more than people think; if you are new to chanting, the basics in [chanting for beginners](/blog/chanting-for-beginners) will help.

Every week or so, call your grandparent and chant to them. Let them correct you. They will, often with enormous satisfaction. Some of the best conversations happen in those corrections: "No, no, that syllable is longer. My father used to say it like this."

## Try this for a week

Pick one prayer your elder knows well. Record it this week, in parts if it is long. Then learn one line a day for seven days, chanting along with their voice, then without it.

On the seventh day, call them and chant those seven lines back to them. Notice what happens in their face. That moment is the tradition passing on, and you will both remember it.
