At my Thatha's cloth shop in Kumbakonam, there was always one bolt of cotton set aside that was never sold. It was for whoever came asking, and nobody wrote down who took it. That bolt taught me more about dana in Hinduism than anything I read later. Dana is giving as a duty and a practice, not a favour: you give because it is right, you give with respect, and you do not keep an account of it.

## What is dana in Hinduism?

Dana simply means giving, or a gift. But in the tradition it is not an optional kindness for good days. It is one of the householder's regular duties, as ordinary as cooking or earning.

The clearest instruction comes from the Taittiriya Upanishad, in the teacher's farewell to students leaving the school. Give with faith, it says; do not give without faith. Give with grace. Give with modesty. Give with awe. Give with understanding. I find that list more demanding than any amount. It says the manner of giving matters as much as the gift.

The Hindu view of charity is also quite practical. Give according to your means, not beyond them. Give to those who need it, not only to those who will notice. And give without making the receiver feel small. Thatha's bolt of cotton was kept at the back of the shop, not the front, so that nobody had to ask in front of other customers. [Honest trade in Thatha's shop](/blog/honest-trade-thathas-shop) says more about how he thought.

## What are the three kinds of giving in the Gita?

The seventeenth chapter of the Bhagavad Gita divides almost everything into three qualities, and giving is no exception. Here are its three kinds of giving.

Sattvic giving is given because it is right to give, at the right place and time, to a worthy person, expecting nothing in return. Sattvic dana is clean: no strings, no hope of a favour, no plaque.

Rajasic giving is given reluctantly, or in the hope of a return, or with an eye on the result: the donation made to be seen, or to earn something back.

Tamasic giving is given at the wrong time and place, to the wrong person, without respect, or with contempt.

What I like about this is that the gift itself can be the same in all three. The same bag of rice. The difference is entirely in the giver. The Gita is not grading the gift. It is grading the heart behind it.

## Anna dana, vidya dana, abhaya dana

The tradition names many kinds of giving, and three are often put first. Anna dana is the gift of food. Vidya dana is the gift of knowledge. Abhaya dana is the gift of safety, freedom from fear, perhaps the rarest of the three.

Food is placed first, and the reason is simple. Nobody can learn when hungry, and nobody can feel safe when hungry either. Temples feed thousands a day for that reason; [annadhanam, feeding as worship](/blog/annadhanam-feeding-as-worship) is the longer story. The same instinct appears in smaller forms: my mother's ball of rice on the wall, her daily habit of [feeding crows and birds](/blog/feeding-crows-and-birds), and the handful of grain set aside for the cow, which has its own long story in [why Hindus honour the cow](/blog/go-puja-cow-worship-meaning).

What does anna dana look like in a New Jersey suburb? A tray of food dropped at a shelter. A standing order at the temple kitchen. A meal for a neighbour just home from the hospital. My son and I did a food bank shift one Saturday, and he talks about it more than any gift he received that month. My column on [giving on your birthday](/blog/giving-on-birthdays) grew out of that. And giving sits close to [seva, service without keeping score](/blog/seva-meaning).

## One small thing for this week

Give something without telling anyone. Not your colleagues, not your friends, not even your spouse. It need not be large. Then notice whether you still want to tell someone. Most of us do. That wanting is worth watching. Thatha never told anyone about the cotton, and I only knew because I spent summer holidays sitting in his shop.
