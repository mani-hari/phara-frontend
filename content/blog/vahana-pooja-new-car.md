The first time I saw a row of new cars garlanded in a New Jersey temple parking lot, lemons wedged under every tyre, I laughed. Then I brought ours the next weekend. A vahana pooja for a new car is short and simple: a turmeric and kumkum mark, a garland, a lamp and camphor shown to the car, a coconut broken, four lemons crushed under the tyres, and a prayer to Ganesha before the first drive. Vahana means vehicle, the same word used for the animal each deity rides, and the ritual treats your car as something that will carry you, and your family, for years.

## Vahana pooja for a new car: the simple steps

Here is the sequence most families follow, at a temple or at home.

1. Wash the car. Tradition begins with cleanliness, and a clean car photographs better for the parents anyway.
2. Draw a small mark of turmeric and kumkum on the bonnet, and another on the steering wheel. Some families add an Om or a small Ganesha mark in kumkum; others draw a simple line.
3. Hang a flower garland across the front grille or the bonnet.
4. Light a lamp or a piece of camphor on a plate and show it to the car in a slow circle, the same gesture you would make before a deity. Camphor burns away and leaves no ash, and [why camphor is used in aarti](/blog/camphor-aarti-meaning) says what families read into that.
5. Break a coconut in front of the car, as families do at most beginnings; the habit is older than cars, and [why Hindus break a coconut](/blog/why-break-a-coconut) traces it.
6. Place four lemons, one under each tyre, and drive forward slowly so the car crushes them.
7. Before the first real drive, say a short prayer to Ganesha, remover of obstacles.

A new car puja at home can be exactly this, done in your driveway by the family. No priest is required, though many people like one.

## Why lemons under the tyres?

Everyone asks. The answer depends on whom you ask.

Families usually explain it as warding off drishti, the evil eye, the idea that envy and admiring glances can stick to a shiny new thing; [what drishti means](/blog/drishti-evil-eye-meaning) goes further into it. The lemon absorbs it and is crushed. Others see it as a small sacrifice at the start of a journey, a token offered to the road. Many simply see it as marking a beginning: the first thing the car does is roll forward over something, and then it is ready.

The tradition varies, and I have stopped needing the explanation. My father still asks on our video calls whether we did the lemons. We did. He is satisfied, and so, oddly, am I.

## Can you do car pooja at a US temple?

Yes. Car pooja at a temple is common in the US: many Hindu temples offer it on weekends, right in the parking lot. You drive in, a priest comes out with a plate, does a short archana, breaks the coconut, places the lemons, and you drive over them while other families wait their turn. The atmosphere is cheerful, part blessing and part car show. Call your local temple ahead to ask what days they do it; some have a queue after a long weekend.

If the nearest temple is far, the driveway works just as well. Gather the family, give the children a job, the garland, the lemons, and let everyone watch the car roll forward.

The car then gets its yearly return during Navratri, on Ayudha Pooja, when tools, books and vehicles are honoured together. Families wash and garland their cars again that day, as I described in [the meaning of Ayudha Pooja](/blog/ayudha-pooja-meaning). It is a yearly reminder that the car is not just a purchase but something that serves you.

## Before the first long drive

If you want a simple home aarti for the car, the step-by-step guide to [how to do aarti at home](/blog/how-to-do-aarti-at-home) works exactly the same outdoors.

And here is one small thing to carry. Before the first long drive, sit in the car for one quiet minute with the engine off. Hands on the wheel. Say a sentence of thanks for safe roads, and for the people you will carry. Then start the engine. You may find yourself doing it before every long drive after that.
