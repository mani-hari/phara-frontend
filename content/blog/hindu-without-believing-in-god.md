Can you be Hindu without believing in God? Yes, and this is not a modern loophole invented for sceptical grandchildren. The Rig Veda, the oldest text of the tradition, contains a hymn about the beginning of the world that ends by wondering whether even the highest being knows how it all began. A tradition that put that doubt into its oldest scripture has always had room for people who are not sure. The question is less "are you allowed?" and more "what does belonging look like when belief is uncertain?"

## Can you be Hindu without believing in God?

Most people who call themselves Hindu did not arrive there by signing a creed. They were born into a family that lit a lamp at dusk, fasted on certain days, celebrated Deepavali and Pongal, touched the feet of elders, and told stories about Ganesha and Krishna at bedtime. Belonging came through family, practice, festivals and values long before anyone asked them what they believed.

That is why the question sounds strange from inside. Hinduism never ran on a single creed. There is no statement of faith that everyone must recite, no council that decided the doctrine, no authority that can declare someone outside. The piece on [whether Hinduism is a religion or a way of life](/blog/is-hinduism-a-religion) looks at why. A person can keep the lamp, the meal, the festival and the ethics of the tradition while being honestly unsure about God, and many do.

Atheism in Hinduism is not a contradiction. It is one of the oldest seats at the table.

## The Nasadiya Sukta's honest doubt

The Nasadiya Sukta is the creation hymn found in the tenth book of the Rig Veda. It is short, and it does something remarkable. Instead of telling a confident story of how the world was made, it asks questions.

It begins: there was neither existence nor non-existence then. There was no air, no sky beyond. What covered it? Where? In whose protection? It goes on, carefully, describing darkness hidden in darkness, and a first stirring of desire. And then it ends with lines that have startled readers ever since. Who really knows? Who can declare it? The gods came later than the creation, so who knows where it arose? Perhaps the one who oversees it from the highest heaven knows. Or perhaps he does not know.

The story says the world began from something unknown. The story is really about honesty. For many readers, the point of the Nasadiya Sukta is that the deepest questions deserve to be held open, not closed with a confident answer. It is not an atheist hymn. But it is a hymn that makes room for not knowing.

## Schools that did without a creator

Classical Indian philosophy had several schools that did not need a creator God, and they were counted within the tradition, not outside it.

Classical Samkhya, one of the oldest schools, describes reality as two principles: purusha, pure consciousness, and prakriti, matter and nature. The world unfolds from prakriti on its own. In its classical form, Samkhya philosophy has no God who created it; the question simply does not arise. Yoga, its sister school, later added a special kind of purusha called Ishvara, but Samkhya itself did without one. The three gunas, which many Hindus use to describe food and temperament, come from Samkhya; there is a piece on [the three gunas](/blog/three-gunas-explained).

Purva Mimamsa, the school devoted to Vedic ritual, argued that the Vedas are eternal and authorless, and that ritual works by its own power. Many of its thinkers explicitly rejected the idea of a creator God. Ritual without a creator: an odd combination to modern eyes, and a perfectly respectable one in classical India.

And then there were the Charvakas, the materialists, who denied God, the soul, rebirth and the authority of the Vedas altogether. Their own texts are almost entirely lost. We know their views because other schools argued with them at length, quoting them in order to refute them. That, in itself, says something: the tradition preserved its sharpest critics by taking them seriously.

## What can you do while the question stays open?

If belief is uncertain for you, you do not need to settle it before you practise. Try practising one thing for its own sake. Light a lamp at dusk and sit with it for two minutes. Keep one festival fully. Learn one verse and what it means. Watch what it does to you, as the piece on [whether rituals are superstition](/blog/are-rituals-superstition) suggests, and let the question of God stay open. The tradition has room for that. It always has. And if you are wondering where the idea of lives and returns fits into all this, [reincarnation in Hinduism](/blog/reincarnation-in-hinduism) takes it up.
