A colleague asked me once, carefully, whether she was "allowed" to come to the temple with us for Deepavali. I told her the only rule was to take her shoes off, and she laughed with relief. So, can non-Hindus visit Hindu temples? In the US, almost always, and warmly. Your friend does not have to pray, believe or know anything. They only have to come in, and most of what makes a first visit easy is what you tell them before you arrive.

## Can non-Hindus visit Hindu temples?

At almost every temple in America, yes. Temples here were built by immigrant communities who remember what it was like to be the newcomer, and many of them host school groups, interfaith evenings and curious neighbours as a matter of course. A friend at the door is not unusual. It is welcome.

Know, though, that some temples in India do limit entry for non-Hindus, either to the whole temple or to the inner sanctum. The rules vary from place to place, and they are old. If you are travelling with a friend in India, check before you go, so that nobody is surprised at the gate.

## What to tell your friend before you go

A short briefing in the car saves a lot of awkwardness. I usually say something like this:

- **Shoes come off** at the entrance. Socks are fine.
- **Modest clothes**: shoulders and knees covered, nothing that makes sitting on the floor difficult.
- **Walk clockwise** around shrines if you walk around them at all.
- **There will be bells, fire and chanting.** Sometimes loud, sometimes all at once.
- **Nobody expects you to pray.** You can simply watch.

That last one matters most. My colleague told me later that she had been worried she would have to do something and get it wrong. Once she knew she could stand at the back and look, she relaxed enough to enjoy it.

If your friend is someone from work, the broader piece on [explaining Hindu festivals to coworkers](/blog/explaining-hindu-festivals-to-coworkers) has some good ways into the conversation.

## Inside, together

Resist the urge to explain everything. A temple in New Jersey may have twenty shrines, and a tour of all of them is exhausting for both of you. Pick one. I usually choose Ganesha, because the story of the elephant head is a good one and children in the room are often already looking at him. Tell that one story well and let the rest be atmosphere.

When the priest brings the aarti flame around, you can show your friend what you do, passing your palms over the flame and touching your eyes, and then let them choose. Some will do it. Some will smile and step back. Both are fine. The same with prasadam: offer it, explain that it is food shared after being offered to God, and do not push. If they ask afterwards what the flame was for, [what the camphor aarti means](/blog/camphor-aarti-meaning) has a short answer.

The canteen is the easiest part. Everyone understands a dosa.

Sooner or later, a thoughtful friend will ask the question: which one is the real God? Answer honestly and briefly. I say that most Hindus see one divine reality appearing in many forms, and that families often have a favourite form they feel closest to. If they want more, point them to the pieces on [whether Hindus believe in one God](/blog/do-hindus-believe-in-one-god) and on [why American temples house so many deities](/blog/why-us-temples-have-many-deities). You do not need to have a theology lecture ready. You only need to be kind.

## One small thing to carry into the week

Invite a friend to a quiet visit first, not a crowded festival. A weekday evening, half an hour, the canteen after. A temple is easier to love when you can hear it, when the bells are distinct and the priest's chanting is not competing with a thousand conversations. My colleague came back with us for Deepavali the following autumn, and she was the one who remembered to take her shoes off first.
