The story says a wish-granting cow rose out of the ocean when gods and demons churned it. The story is really about the animal that already gave a village most of what it needed. That, in short, is the go puja meaning. Go is the Sanskrit word for cow, and go puja is the ritual honouring of a cow and her calf, with a bath, a garland, turmeric and kumkum, a meal of fruit, grass and jaggery, and a slow walk around them. It is gratitude, made visible.

## What does go puja mean?

The ritual itself is gentle. The cow and calf are bathed and their horns sometimes painted. A garland goes around the neck. Turmeric and kumkum are applied to the forehead, and sometimes to the hooves. The family offers bananas, fresh grass, jaggery, a handful of cooked rice. They walk around the animal clockwise, the way they would walk around a shrine, [the clockwise walk of pradakshina](/blog/pradakshina-meaning). Then they bow.

It is commonly done on Govatsa Dwadashi, the day before Dhanteras in the North and West; on Mattu Pongal in Tamil Nadu, the day after Pongal, when cattle are washed, painted and paraded; and on Gopashtami in the month of Kartika. Customs vary by region, and many temples keep a cow and perform a short go puja every morning. The day for the cattle is part of [why Pongal is celebrated](/blog/why-is-pongal-celebrated) at all.

## Kamadhenu and Krishna

The Puranas give the cow several stories, and each says something different.

In the churning of the ocean, the samudra manthan told in the Vishnu Purana and the Bhagavata Purana, many treasures rise from the waters before the nectar. One of them is Kamadhenu, the cow of plenty, who can give whatever is asked of her. The gods take her. The full list, and what each treasure might mean, belongs to [the churning of the ocean](/blog/samudra-manthan-meaning). The Kamadhenu story places the cow among the ocean's greatest gifts, alongside the moon and the goddess Lakshmi herself.

In the epics, the sage Vasishtha keeps a wish-granting cow in his forest hermitage; the Mahabharata calls her Nandini, Kamadhenu's daughter. When the king Vishvamitra tries to take her by force, he fails, and the failure sends him on the long road from king to sage. The story says a king wanted a cow. The story is really about what cannot be taken, only received.

And then there is Krishna, Gopala, protector of cows. He grows up among cowherds in Vraja. When Indra sends a flood to punish the village, Krishna lifts Govardhana hill on one finger and shelters the people and the herds underneath it for seven days; [the story of Krishna lifting Govardhana](/blog/krishna-govardhana-story) tells it properly. The cows are not a detail in that story. They are half of who he is protecting.

## Why does the cow stand for giving?

Ask a grandmother, and she will list it on her fingers. Milk. Curd. Ghee for the lamp and the homam. Buttermilk in summer. Dung for fuel, and dried and mixed with water for the floor of the house and the courtyard. Bullocks for the plough and the cart. In an old village, the cow was close to a household's whole economy, and she asked for grass and water in return.

That is why the tradition made her an image of the gift that keeps on giving without asking. The question why Hindus worship cows is often answered with theology, but the older answer is gratitude. You do not worship what you merely use. You honour what sustains you, and you show it.

Grandmothers kept this in one small habit: the first roti of the day went to the cow. Before the family ate, before the guests, the first piece was set aside. The same instinct appears in the ball of rice left for crows, which a separate column on [feeding crows and birds](/blog/feeding-crows-and-birds) describes, and in the broader tradition of [dana, the Hindu idea of giving](/blog/dana-in-hinduism).

## Go puja without a cow

Most of us do not live near a cow. Gau puja at home, for an apartment in a city, might seem impossible. But the ritual was never really about the species. It was about noticing a creature that gives, and giving back.

So if there is no cow near you, let go puja become feeding any animal with care. Set aside a handful of grain for the birds, or the first roti for a neighbour's dog, or a bowl of water on a hot afternoon. Choose a creature that will not thank you. That was always the point.
