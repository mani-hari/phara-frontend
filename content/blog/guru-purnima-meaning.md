Guru Purnima is the full moon set aside to thank the people who moved you from not knowing to knowing. That is the simplest Guru Purnima meaning, and it holds whether or not you have a guru. You may not have one. You certainly have teachers: the person who taught you to swim, the manager who told you the hard thing, the grandmother who showed you how to light the lamp. The tradition puts a teacher at the centre, Vyasa, and a silent one in the background, Dakshinamurthy.

## What does Guru Purnima mean in the tradition?

Guru Purnima falls on the full moon of Ashadha, usually June or July. It is also called Vyasa Purnima, because the tradition honours Vyasa that day. Vyasa, the story says, arranged the one great Veda into four so that ordinary people could hold it, and composed the Mahabharata, including the Bhagavad Gita. He is the teacher who made the teaching portable.

In South Indian temples the day also turns attention to Dakshinamurthy, Shiva as the teacher, seated under a banyan tree, facing south. Around him sit elderly sages, and he is shown as a young man. He teaches in silence, and the old men's doubts dissolve. It is an odd and beautiful image: the teacher says nothing, and the students understand.

Many monastic orders begin their rainy-season retreat around this full moon, and disciples traditionally visit their teachers with fruit, flowers and a bow.

## Do you need a guru to keep Guru Purnima?

No. A popular explanation, found in the Advaya Taraka Upanishad, splits the word into gu, darkness, and ru, that which removes it. A guru is whatever removes a darkness. By that measure, you have had many.

Think of the person who taught you a skill that still feeds you. The friend who said something true at the right moment. The book that rearranged how you think. The coach who made you run one more lap. All of them removed a darkness of some kind.

Buddhists remember the same full moon as the day the Buddha gave his first teaching at Sarnath to five companions. Two traditions share the full moon and the idea behind it: someone who had seen something turned to others and shared it.

## A practice for the full moon

Try this on the evening of Guru Purnima. It takes about twenty minutes.

1. **List three people.** Write down three people who taught you something that stayed. Be specific about what: not "a relative", but "the aunt who taught me to make rasam without measuring".
2. **Contact one.** Write or call one of them. If they are no longer living, write the letter anyway and keep it by the lamp.
3. **Sit with a lamp.** Light a lamp, sit for ten minutes, and let the gratitude be specific. Picture the moment the teaching happened. Where were you? What did they say?

Specific gratitude lands differently from general gratitude. "Thank you for everything" slides off. "Thank you for the day you made me redo that report" stays. The Tirukkural gives a whole chapter to this, and [what the Tirukkural says about gratitude](/blog/tirukkural-on-gratitude) makes a good reading for the evening. If you keep a monthly full-moon practice, [pournami pooja at home](/blog/pournami-pooja-at-home) fits alongside this one easily.

## Seven names in seven days

The sacred days of the year each carry a flavour: this full moon for teachers, [Kartik Purnima for lamps](/blog/kartik-purnima-significance), [Ratha Saptami for the sun](/blog/ratha-saptami-significance), and others for gods and harvests. Guru Purnima is the one that turns you outward toward particular people.

For a week after it, each day notice one thing someone taught you that you still use. Tying a knot, tempering mustard seeds, apologising properly, reading a map. Say their name in your head.

By the end of the week, you will have seven names and a strange, warm feeling that you were never really self-made. That feeling is the festival. As the month of [Margazhi](/blog/margazhi-month-meaning) does for dawn, this full moon does for memory: it asks you to wake up to what was already given.
