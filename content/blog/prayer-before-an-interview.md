The two minutes before an interview decide more than you think, and you can use them. Sit down. Breathe out longer than you breathe in. Say one mantra. That is a prayer before an interview in its whole form, and it fits in a lobby, a parked car or the moment before you click "join" on a video call. You are not asking for the job. You are asking for a clear voice.

## A prayer before an interview in two minutes

Here is the sequence. Try it once now, so it is familiar when you need it.

**Minute one: five slow breaths.** Breathe in for a count of four. Breathe out for a count of six or eight. Keep your eyes soft, on the floor or on one fixed point. Five breaths take roughly a minute.

**Minute two: the mantra and the sankalpam.** Say one of these, silently or under your breath:

- "Vakratunda mahakaya suryakoti samaprabha, nirvighnam kuru me deva sarva karyeshu sarvada", once. O Lord with the curved trunk and mighty body, radiant as a million suns, remove the obstacles from all my work, always. The verse is unpacked word by word in [the Vakratunda Mahakaya meaning](/blog/vakratunda-mahakaya-meaning).
- Or "Om Gam Ganapataye Namah", three times.

Then make a sankalpam, a one-line statement of intention. "I will speak truthfully and listen well." Say it once. One honest line is enough, as [what a sankalpam is](/blog/what-is-sankalpam) explains.

That is all. Stand up and walk in.

## Why Ganesha and Saraswati?

Ganesha is the keeper of thresholds, worshipped before every beginning. An interview is a threshold in the plainest sense: you are standing at a door, and on the other side is a room you might work in. Many people find it natural to greet the doorkeeper first.

Saraswati is speech and clarity. Her name is often linked to flowing, and she governs words, learning and expression. If Ganesha clears the road, Saraswati steadies the voice on it. Families preparing a student for exams often turn to her as well, sometimes with [a Saraswati homam for students](/blog/saraswati-homam-for-students).

Notice what you are asking for. Not a result. A clear voice, a steady mind, the ability to say what you actually know. The result belongs to the people on the other side of the table, and you cannot control them. You can control whether you arrive in your own body.

## What does it do to the body?

In plain terms, three things happen.

**A longer exhale slows a racing heart.** When the out-breath is longer than the in-breath, many people find the heartbeat settles and the shoulders drop. It is one of the few levers the body gives you directly.

**A repeated sound gives the wandering mind a place to rest.** Before an interview, the mind runs through every question it fears. A mantra is a short, familiar sound that the mind can return to each time it wanders. It does not need to understand the Sanskrit to be steadied by the rhythm.

**A clear intention stops you rehearsing fear.** The sankalpam gives the mind a sentence to hold that is not "what if I freeze". You walk in thinking "I will speak truthfully", which is something you can actually do.

None of this is magic, and none of it replaces preparation. You still need to know your work. But many people find that the two minutes change how the first two minutes of the interview go, and those tend to shape the rest.

## Before every meeting, for a week

Do the two-minute practice before every meeting for a week, not only interviews. The stand-up. The call with your manager. The awkward conversation with a colleague.

It will feel slightly silly on Monday. By Friday it will be automatic. And that is the point: by the time an interview really matters, the practice is already yours, and your body knows what to do when you sit down and breathe out. If you want a place to keep it, a [small altar at your desk](/blog/small-altar-at-your-desk) can hold a lamp and a picture of Ganesha within reach.
