My aunt in Chennai knows everyone's birth star by heart, the way other people remember phone numbers. The rest of us need three facts. If you are wondering how to find your nakshatra, the answer is simple: you need your date, time and place of birth, because a nakshatra, or birth star, is just the segment of the sky where the Moon stood at the moment you were born. With those three facts, a family horoscope, a panchangam or a good calculator will tell you in a minute.

I learned this the slightly embarrassing way. At a temple in New Jersey, the priest asked for my son's star before an archana, and I had to text my mother at two in the morning her time. She answered within a minute, of course. Now I keep a list.

## How to find your nakshatra: what do you need?

Three things: the date, the time and the place of birth.

The date and time are obvious. The place matters because it fixes the time zone, and the Moon's position has to be calculated for a precise moment. The Moon moves quickly. It passes through all twenty-seven nakshatras in about a month, spending roughly a day in each. Each nakshatra covers thirteen degrees and twenty minutes of the sky, and the Moon crosses that stretch in something close to a day.

So for most birthdays, one star covers most of the day. The time only becomes critical when the Moon changes stars on your birth date, which happens more often than you would expect.

## Three quick ways to check

**The family horoscope.** If your parents had a jathakam, a traditional horoscope, written when you were born, your star is almost certainly on the first page, usually alongside your rasi, the Moon sign. In many Tamil families it sits in a drawer with the passports.

**A panchangam for your birth date.** The panchangam, the Hindu almanac, lists the nakshatra for every day along with the times it begins and ends. If you know how to read one, you can find your star directly. [How to read a panchangam](/blog/how-to-read-a-panchangam) walks through the columns.

**A calculator.** Online janma nakshatra calculators work well if you choose one that uses the sidereal zodiac with the Lahiri ayanamsa, the setting most Indian astrologers follow. The key detail for anyone born in the US: enter your birth time in its own local time zone and your actual city. Do not convert it to Indian time yourself; the calculator does that.

## What if the star changes on your birth day?

This is where most confusion comes from. Say the panchangam shows Rohini until mid-afternoon and Mrigashira after that. A child born in the morning is Rohini; a child born in the evening is [Mrigashira nakshatra](/blog/mrigashira-nakshatra). Only the time can decide.

If the birth time is unknown, write down both possibilities and ask an astrologer, or an elder who remembers the birth. Families sometimes rely on a remembered detail, like whether it was before or after lunch. Your rasi is often clear even when the star is not, since each rasi holds more than two stars. [How rasi and nakshatra fit together](/blog/rasi-vs-nakshatra) explains the relationship more fully.

For children born here, the star is read from local time and place, not from where the grandparents live. My son was born in New Jersey in the evening; his star follows the New Jersey clock. The piece on [finding the birth star for a US-born baby](/blog/birth-star-for-us-born-baby) goes through this carefully, including what to do when relatives in India calculate it differently.

## Keep it with your puja things

Once you know your star, you will find it used everywhere: for an archana at the temple, for choosing a name, and for the star birthday, when many families perform a small pooja on the day the Moon returns to your birth nakshatra. Some mark it with an [Ayushya homam on the star birthday](/blog/ayushya-homam-star-birthday).

One small thing to carry into this week: write down each family member's star on a card and keep it with your lamp and camphor. It is the first thing a priest asks, and it saves a two-in-the-morning text to Chennai.
