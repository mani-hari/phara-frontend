Cleaning brass is one of the few chores that tradition treats as part of the pooja itself. If you want to know how to clean brass pooja items, the old answer still works best: tamarind or lemon with salt, a little patience, a thorough rinse and a completely dry cloth. Try it slowly on a Friday morning, one lamp at a time, and it stops feeling like a chore at all.

## How to clean brass pooja items with tamarind or lemon

Brass darkens because the copper in it reacts with air and moisture, and oil and smoke add a sticky layer on top. Mild acid lifts the tarnish; salt gives a gentle scrub. That is the whole science, and grandmothers knew it without the vocabulary.

The tamarind method:

1. Soak a small ball of tamarind, about the size of a walnut, in a cup of warm water for ten minutes.
2. Squeeze the softened pulp in your hand and pick up a pinch of coarse salt with it.
3. Rub the pulp and salt over the brass in small circles. You will see the dull brown lift almost immediately.
4. Rinse well in warm water.
5. Dry completely with a soft cloth, including inside the grooves.

Tamarind brass cleaning is gentle and smells faintly sour and pleasant. The lemon method is almost the same: cut a lemon in half, dip the cut side in salt, and rub. For heavy tarnish on an old lamp, make a paste of lemon juice and baking soda, spread it on, leave it for a few minutes, then rub and rinse.

Always rinse well. Acid left on brass will tarnish it again faster, and salt left in the crevices leaves white marks. Always dry completely.

## How do you get oil and soot off a brass diya?

The lamp is the hardest item on any altar, because oil soaks into the metal and burnt wick welds itself to the rim. How you light it and what you burn both decide how much scrubbing waits for you later: see [how to light a diya](/blog/how-to-light-a-diya) and [choosing a ghee lamp or an oil lamp](/blog/ghee-lamp-or-oil-lamp).

To clean oil stains from a brass diya, start with very hot water and a squeeze of dish soap. Let the lamp soak for fifteen or twenty minutes. Scrape the old wick residue gently with a wooden stick or a toothpick; metal tools will scratch. For stubborn sticky oil, a paste of flour, salt and a little white vinegar clings well and lifts the film. Use a soft toothbrush for carved lamps, especially the little leaves and peacocks on a traditional kuthuvilakku, the tall standing oil lamp of Tamil homes. In villages, wood ash or stove ash from the kitchen hearth was the classic scrub, gritty and mildly alkaline, and it still works if you have a fireplace.

## What works in a US kitchen?

You do not need special products. Most American kitchens already hold what you need: lemons and salt, baking soda, white vinegar. In a pinch, ketchup works, because its acid does the same job as tamarind; leave a thin layer on for a few minutes, then rinse. Indian grocery stores sell brass cleaning powder, usually a mix of mild acid and fine abrasive, which is convenient for a large collection.

A few cautions. Avoid steel wool and harsh chemical cleaners, which scratch or strip the surface. Some modern pooja items are lacquered or only plated with brass; test a small hidden spot first, and if the finish looks like it is coming off, stop and just wipe with a damp cloth. Microfiber cloths are excellent for the final polish. Silver is a different metal and should be cleaned separately, with baking soda and warm water, never with tamarind.

Clean brass sets off the rest of the altar. A freshly polished kalasha, for instance, changes the feel of a pooja entirely; the piece on [the meaning of the kalasam](/blog/kalasam-meaning) explains why that pot matters so much. And Friday, traditionally the day of Lakshmi, is when many households polish; follow the cleaning with a lamp and you have most of a [Friday Lakshmi pooja](/blog/friday-lakshmi-pooja).

If a piece is cracked beyond cleaning, there is a gentle way to retire it; a column on [what to do with old idols and puja photos](/blog/old-idols-and-photos) covers that.

## Make it the first offering

Choose one day a week for brass. Clean only what you used that week, not the whole cupboard. Do it slowly, with warm water and full attention on the metal in your hands. Let the polishing be the first offering of the day, before the lamp is even lit.
