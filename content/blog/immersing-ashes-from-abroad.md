Many families keep the ashes at home for weeks or months before they can make the journey, and that waiting is allowed. Asthi visarjan, the immersion of the remains in water, has no deadline that love cannot meet. If you are immersing ashes in India from abroad, the practical steps are few: a sealed urn, the right papers, a phone call to the airline, and a decision about which river. The harder part is the decision to go, and nobody can hurry that for you.

## Carrying the ashes home: the journey

Most families carry the ashes in hand luggage rather than checked bags, in a sealed urn that security scanners can see into; wood, plastic and some composite urns usually work, while heavy metal ones may cause delays. Keep the cremation certificate and the death certificate in a folder, with copies. Call the airline ahead and ask about their policy. Guidance changes, and what a cousin heard on his last trip may no longer apply. Check current airline, security and customs guidance before you fly, both for leaving the US and for arriving in India. The same care applies to anything else you carry; [flying with puja items](/blog/flying-with-puja-items) has the details.

A friend in Ohio carried his mother's ashes in a small wooden box on his lap for the whole flight to Chennai. A flight attendant noticed, asked quietly, and kept bringing him tea for the rest of the journey without being asked. People are kinder about this than we expect.

## Where do families immerse ashes in India?

The traditional places are well known: the Ganga at Kashi or Haridwar, the sea at Rameswaram, the Triveni Sangam at Prayagraj where three rivers meet. Rameswaram in particular draws Tamil families, often with other rites the same day, such as [the tila homam at Rameswaram](/blog/tila-homam-at-rameswaram-meaning).

But many families choose the river nearest their home town: the Kaveri near Srirangam or Kumbakonam, the Vaigai, the Tamirabarani, the Godavari. When my aunt talks about it, she does not mention the famous ghats first. She mentions the river the family bathed in on festival mornings. The river matters more than the fame of the place. A river the person swam in as a child, or walked beside on the way to the temple, is a river that knew them.

## What happens at the water?

A priest at the riverbank usually conducts a short puja first: a sankalpam, the statement of intention, naming the one who died, then a few mantras and offerings. The ashes are released into the water, often with milk and flowers poured alongside. The karta, the family member performing the rites, and close relatives take a dip afterwards, which marks the end of that part of the rite.

Some families add a tila homam, a fire offering of black sesame for the ancestors, or a tarpanam, an offering of water and sesame, the same day. A simpler form of [tarpanam at home](/blog/tarpanam-at-home) can continue in the years after, wherever you live.

The immersion also connects forward. Each year the family keeps [the annual shraddham](/blog/annual-shraddham-meaning) on the lunar day of death, whether or not the ashes have yet reached the river. The Gita says that for the one who is born, death is certain, and for the one who dies, birth is certain. My father says it more simply: the river is where the journey is handed on.

## If the journey is years away

Sometimes travel is not possible for a long time: a visa, health, small children, work. Some families immerse the ashes in the ocean or a river in the US, following local rules, with a priest or just the family. Others wait. Both are done, and both are respectful.

If you wait, keep the urn in a clean, quiet place, a shelf near the puja corner rather than a cupboard, and light a lamp beside it on the days that matter. The tradition waits with you. What was done at the funeral home, described in [a Hindu funeral in the USA](/blog/hindu-funeral-in-the-usa), is complete in itself. The immersion finishes the journey when you are able to make it.
