Lakshmi is called Chanchala, the restless one, because she does not stay put. Why Lakshmi is restless is explained by the stories themselves: she leaves when she is taken for granted, and comes back when she is welcomed. Fortune, in this picture, is not a possession locked in a box. It is a guest with a mind of her own, drawn to houses that are clean, generous and attentive, and quietly gone from the ones that assume she will always be there.

## Why Lakshmi is restless: the day she left Indra

The Vishnu Purana tells how she once left heaven itself.

The sage Durvasa, famous for his temper, was walking one day with a garland of fragrant flowers. He met Indra, king of the gods, riding his great white elephant Airavata, and offered him the garland as a gift. Indra, careless in his glory, took it and tossed it onto the elephant's head. The elephant, bothered by the scent, pulled it off with its trunk and trampled it into the ground.

Durvasa's anger was immediate. You treat a gift with contempt because you think your fortune is secure, he said. Let that fortune leave you. And it did. Lakshmi withdrew from Indra's heaven. The gods lost their strength, their splendour, their confidence. The asuras pressed in. The world grew dim.

Lakshmi disappeared into the ocean of milk, and it took the great churning, the samudra manthan, to bring her back. She rose from the waters seated on a lotus, and chose Vishnu. Plenty else came up from the deep, and [the samudra manthan](/blog/samudra-manthan-meaning) lists it all.

The story says the gods lost everything because of a garland. The story is really about attention. Indra did not commit a great crime. He simply stopped noticing what he had been given.

## Who is Alakshmi?

Folk tradition gives Lakshmi an elder sister: Alakshmi, sometimes called Jyeshtha, the elder. In some tellings she too rose from the churned ocean, before Lakshmi. Where Lakshmi is drawn to order, generosity and light, Alakshmi follows quarrels, clutter and neglect. She settles where people bicker, where dishes pile up, where lamps go unlit and guests go unwelcomed.

This is why, in many homes, the house is swept and washed before Lakshmi is invited, and lamps are lit at dusk. The customs around Lakshmi and Alakshmi are practical as much as devotional: you make room for one by clearing out the other. Nobody needs to believe in a literal sister goddess to see the sense of it. Some households are simply easier to be fortunate in.

## Why should wealth move at all?

The name Chanchala can sound like a complaint, as if fortune were fickle. Read differently, it is a description of how wealth works.

Money that never moves stops being useful. A coin buried in a pot helps nobody, including its owner. Wealth does its good by circulating: paying the farmer, the weaver, the priest, the neighbour's son who needs school fees. That is why giving is built into so many Lakshmi festivals. At Diwali, families give sweets, clothes and gifts; traders settle their accounts and open new ones. What this looks like on an ordinary working day is the subject of [Hinduism and business ethics](/blog/hinduism-business-ethics).

Then there is the lotus. Lakshmi sits on a flower that grows from muddy water and rises above it, untouched. Fortune can come from ordinary, messy places, through work and trade and effort, and still be clean. It is also a reminder that she does not live in the mud. She rises.

The tradition does not ask anyone to scorn wealth. It asks them to hold it loosely, the way you hold water. Grip too hard, and it slips away. A story of honest dealing, [Thatha's shop in Kumbakonam](/blog/honest-trade-thathas-shop), shows what that looked like at an ordinary cloth counter.

## Making room

Durvasa's curse fell on a king who thought he no longer needed to pay attention. The cure was a whole cosmos churning. Most of us will need less than that.

This week, move one thing along. A donation you have been meaning to make. A favour you owe and have not returned. A drawer you have not opened in a year, cleared, and its contents given away. The old idea is that fortune enters where there is room. Whether you read that as a goddess choosing a clean house, or as the simple truth that a life stuck in place attracts nothing new, the practice is the same. Make the room, light the lamp, and leave the door open.

None of this is a promise that a clean drawer brings money. It is closer to the old teaching that what we do shapes what comes to us, which there is more on in [karma, explained simply](/blog/karma-explained-simply).
