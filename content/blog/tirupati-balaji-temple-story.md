The god of Tirupati is said to be in debt. In the Tirupati Balaji story, Venkateswara borrowed a great fortune from Kubera, the treasurer of the gods, to pay for his wedding, and every coin that goes into the temple hundi, the offering vessel, is a devotee helping him pay it back. I heard this as a boy in Chennai and thought it was a joke. I now think it is one of the wisest stories we have about money and God.

## What is the Tirupati Balaji story?

The version most families know comes from the Venkatachala Mahatmyam and related Puranic tellings. The sages were holding a great sacrifice and could not decide which god should receive its fruits. They sent the sage Bhrigu to test the three. He found Vishnu reclining with his eyes closed, took this for indifference, and kicked him in the chest. Vishnu did not get angry. He rose, held Bhrigu's foot and asked whether it had been hurt.

Lakshmi was not so forgiving. Vishnu's chest is her home, and she left Vaikuntha, Vishnu's heaven, in hurt and came down to earth. Vishnu followed her. He wandered until he came to the seven hills of Tirumala and settled there as Srinivasa. In time he met Padmavati, the daughter of a local king, and they were to be married. But a god in search of his wife has no money of his own, so he went to Kubera and borrowed enough for a royal wedding, promising to repay it with interest until the end of this age.

Two other stories help with this one: [Kubera and the meaning of wealth](/blog/kubera-story-meaning), and [Lakshmi, the restless goddess](/blog/lakshmi-restless-goddess). The goddess of fortune is the one who walks away. The god has to borrow to find her again.

## Why do devotees give their hair?

At Tirupati, men, women and children have their heads shaved as an offering, often in fulfilment of a vow. Tonsure is the offering of vanity itself. Hair is the one part of the body we fuss over every morning, and giving it up is a small, visible humility. It grows back. That is part of the lesson.

The popular story goes further. Once, while Venkateswara lay hidden in the hills, a blow to his head left a bald patch. A Gandharva princess called Neela Devi saw it, cut a lock of her own hair, and placed it on the wound. He promised that those who came to him would offer their hair, and that it would be hers. It is why the hill is sometimes called Neeladri.

Many families offer a baby's first hair at a temple for the same reason, and [the mundan, a child's first haircut](/blog/mundan-first-haircut), explains that ceremony. Parents describe the odd lightness of carrying the child home afterwards. A head made light. A vow kept. Other pilgrimages ask for the vow to be lived longer; [the Sabarimala Ayyappa vratham](/blog/sabarimala-ayyappa-vratham) asks for forty-one days of it before the climb.

## The hundi and what giving means

The hundi at Tirupati is famous for how much is dropped into it. What is more interesting to me is who drops it in. A bank manager from Edison, back for a cousin's wedding, and a farmer from a village in Andhra who has walked up the hill, stand in the same queue and put something in the same vessel. Neither is paying a fee. Both are helping a friend with a debt.

That is the difference the story makes. If God needed nothing, giving would be a transaction: I pay, you bless. Because the story makes Venkateswara a borrower, giving becomes partnership. You are not buying anything. You are standing with someone who took a risk for love.

## For families in the US

Most of us cannot climb the seven hills each year. The local Venkateswara temple, and there are good ones in several American cities, keeps the same daily worship; if you are going for the first time, [a first visit to a Hindu temple in the USA](/blog/first-visit-hindu-temple-usa) will help. A vow kept in the heart for the next India trip counts. An offering made in a parent's name counts. Many families also keep [Vaikunta Ekadashi](/blog/vaikunta-ekadashi-significance) as their Tirupati day from afar.

One small thing to carry into this week: keep a small jar at home. The next time good news arrives, a clear report, a job offer, a child's first word, put a coin in it for the Lord. When the jar is full, give it where it is needed. More often than you expect, you will remember whose debt you are helping to pay.
