When my father had his knee operation in Tamil Nadu, I was in New Jersey watching the clock in two time zones, and the prayer I said most was only one line long. I had grown up hearing long stotras. That morning I could not have finished one. If you are looking for a Hindu prayer before surgery, for yourself or someone you love, it helps to know that short is allowed. The tradition has always had one-line prayers for exactly the hours when longer ones will not come.

## A Hindu prayer before surgery that fits a waiting room

Here are three I have seen families use. Pick one. You do not need all three.

**The Dhanvantari mantra.** Dhanvantari is the physician of the gods, who in the Puranas rises from the churning of the ocean carrying the pot of amrita, the nectar of life. His short mantra is "Om Namo Bhagavate Vasudevaya Dhanvantaraye", I bow to the Lord Vasudeva in the form of Dhanvantari. [The Dhanvantari mantra's meaning](/blog/dhanvantari-mantra-meaning) gives the longer form too.

**Om Namah Shivaya.** Five syllables, known to almost every Hindu household, easy to say silently under a mask or in a hospital corridor.

**The Mahamrityunjaya mantra.** Longer, but many families already know it. It asks Shiva to free us from death the way a ripe cucumber is freed from its vine.

Say it silently, as many times as the wait needs. Do not count if counting makes you anxious. In a waiting room, the prayer does not have to be performed. It only has to be said.

If you are the one going into surgery, the same line works in the pre-operative room, while the drip goes in, while you are asked to count backwards. Many people find that a familiar sound gives the mind one steady thing to hold.

## Praying from far away

Most of the families I know here have faced this: a parent's surgery in India, and a flight that cannot get you there in time, or a job that cannot spare you for three weeks. Distance changes the logistics. It does not change the prayer.

Three things helped us.

- **A lamp at the hour the surgery begins.** India is nine and a half or ten and a half hours ahead of New Jersey, depending on the season. My father's surgery began at seven in the morning there, which was late the evening before for me. I lit a lamp on the kitchen counter and sat with it.
- **An archana in the patient's name.** An archana is a short offering of names and flowers at a temple, done in the person's name and birth star. We asked the temple near my parents' home to do one the day before. Some families arrange a larger homam, usually [the Mrityunjaya homam](/blog/mrityunjaya-homam-meaning), and a few, wanting courage for the whole family, choose [the Chandi homam](/blog/chandi-homam-meaning).
- **The family call.** My sister set up a call from the hospital corridor. Nobody said very much. We were simply together, on a phone, for the first hour.

For the weeks around a hospital stay, [praying for a sick parent from abroad](/blog/praying-for-a-sick-parent) goes further.

## Is it enough to pray?

This question comes up at every hospital I have sat in, usually from someone who feels guilty that they cannot do more.

Prayer walks beside the surgeons and nurses, never in front of them. My mother, who arranged the archana, also asked the surgeon every question on her list and followed every instruction afterwards. She saw no conflict. Neither do I.

Tirumoolar, the Tamil saint, wrote in the Tirumantiram that the body is a temple. If that is so, the surgical team are doing temple work too. Thanking them is part of the prayer. When my father came home, my mother sent sweets to the ward, and that, as much as the lamp, was how she closed the circle. [Simple thanksgiving rituals after recovery](/blog/gratitude-after-recovery) has more ways to close it.

Some people also wrestle with the old question of why the illness came at all. The answer in [whether illness is karma](/blog/illness-and-karma) is more careful than the one relatives sometimes offer in the corridor.

## One small thing to carry

If you know a surgery is coming, choose your one-line prayer now. Write it on a card, or in the notes on your phone, or simply say it once a day until the day arrives.

When the morning comes, you will not be searching for words in the corridor. You will already have them, and they will already be worn smooth, the way a prayer should be.
