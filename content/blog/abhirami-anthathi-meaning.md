The story says a priest named the wrong day, a king sentenced him to death for it, and the Goddess threw her earring into the sky to make the priest right. It is one of the most loved stories in Tamil devotion, and it is the origin of the Abhirami Anthathi, a hundred verses to the Goddess Abhirami. The Abhirami Anthathi meaning is usually explained as praise and surrender. The story around it is really about devotion so complete it forgets the calendar.

## Who was Abhirami Bhattar?

He was a priest at the temple of Thirukadaiyur, near the coast of the Kaveri delta, where the Goddess is worshipped as Abhirami, the one of surpassing beauty, beside Shiva as Amritaghateswarar. Thirukadaiyur is also the temple where families traditionally celebrate a sixtieth or eightieth birthday; [the shashtiabdapoorthi, the sixtieth birthday rite](/blog/shashtiabdapoorthi-60th-birthday), explains why so many families make the journey for it.

The priest, known to us as Abhirami Bhattar, was so absorbed in the Goddess that people thought him strange. He sat before her for hours. He talked to her. He did not always notice who was speaking to him. Some in the town called him mad.

One day a Maratha king of Thanjavur, visiting the temple, asked him an ordinary question: what was the tithi, the lunar day? Bhattar was gazing at the Goddess's face at that moment. He answered: the full moon. It was, in fact, the new-moon day, the darkest night of the month.

## How did the moon rise?

The king was not amused. A priest who could not tell the full moon from the new moon was either a fool or a liar, and he declared that if the full moon did not rise that night, Bhattar would die. In the telling most people know, a pyre was built and a platform hung above it by a hundred ropes.

Bhattar sat on the platform and began to sing to Abhirami. As he finished each verse, one rope was cut. The fire burned below. He kept singing. Tellings vary on the exact moment, but somewhere in the long hymn the Goddess appeared, took off one of her earrings, and flung it into the dark sky, where it shone like a full moon. The king fell at Bhattar's feet. Bhattar finished the hundred verses anyway.

The new-moon night itself carries its own meaning in the tradition; [amavasya and the ancestors](/blog/amavasya-and-ancestors) explains why it is kept for them.

## Abhirami Anthathi meaning and form

An anthathi is a Tamil poetic form in which the last word of each verse becomes the first word of the next. Antham means end and aadhi means beginning. The end of one is the beginning of the other. The last verse of the Abhirami Anthathi even loops back to the first, so the chain closes into a circle, like a garland.

The hundred verses move between praise, surrender and wonder. The poet describes the Goddess's beauty, confesses his own weakness, asks her to stay with him, and marvels that she would care for someone like him. It is intimate, sometimes playful, and deeply loved in Tamil homes, where it is often sung on Fridays and full moons. [Women in Hinduism and the Goddess](/blog/women-in-hinduism-goddess) looks at the wider tradition of honouring her, and [the Soundarya Lahari explained](/blog/soundarya-lahari-explained) shows how a Sanskrit poet described the same Goddess.

## The other moon

Bhattar was not wrong about the moon. He was looking at a different one. When the king asked, the face in front of him was shining, and he reported what he saw. The story does not make him a fool who was rescued. It makes him a witness to something the king could not see, until the Goddess lent the king her earring so that he could.

Most of us are the king in this story, not the priest. We check calendars. We want the facts right. The story does not mock that. It only suggests that somewhere, occasionally, there is a light worth losing track of the date for.

If you want to begin with the hymn, read one verse a day with a translation. Notice how each verse hands its last word to the next. By the end of a week you will feel the chain pulling you forward, and you will understand why an anthathi is easy to remember and hard to stop.
