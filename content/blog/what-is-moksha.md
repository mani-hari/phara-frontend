What is moksha? It is usually translated as liberation and pictured as an exit: the soul finally stepping out of the world and closing the door behind it. The Katha Upanishad tells it differently. It tells it through a boy who walks into the house of Death, waits three days for Death to come home, and then asks the one question Death would rather not answer.

## What is moksha, in plain words?

Moksha means release, freedom. In the first place, freedom from samsara, the round of birth, death and rebirth that Hindu thought describes; there is more on that in [reincarnation in Hinduism](/blog/reincarnation-in-hinduism). More deeply, freedom from the sense of being a small, separate, anxious self, the one who is always bargaining with life.

It is the fourth of the four purusharthas, the aims of a human life. The first three are dharma, right conduct; artha, wealth and security; and kama, pleasure and love. The tradition does not ask you to despise these, and [the four purusharthas](/blog/four-purusharthas) makes that point well. Moksha sits beside and beyond them, the aim that makes sense of the others.

## Nachiketa at Yama's door

The story opens with a father, Vajashravas, performing a sacrifice in which he is meant to give away all he owns. He gives away old cows that no longer give milk. His young son Nachiketa sees the hollowness of it and asks, "To whom will you give me?" He asks three times. The father snaps: "To Death I give you."

The boy takes him at his word and goes to the house of Yama, the lord of death. Yama is away. Nachiketa waits three days without food. When Yama returns, ashamed to have kept a guest waiting, he offers three boons. For the first, the boy asks that his father be calm and welcome him home. For the second, he asks to learn a sacred fire ritual. For the third, he asks: when a person dies, some say he still exists and some say he does not. What is the truth?

Yama tries to wriggle out of it. Ask for something else, he says. Take sons and grandsons who will live a hundred years, cattle and gold and horses, a long life, music and chariots. Nachiketa refuses them all. These things last only until tomorrow, he says. Keep your horses and your songs.

So Yama teaches him. The self is not born and does not die. It is not killed when the body is killed. It is smaller than the smallest and greater than the greatest, hidden in the heart.

The story says a boy won an argument with Death. The story is really about choice. Yama names it himself: there is shreyas, the lasting good, and preyas, the merely pleasant. Both approach every person. The wise choose the first. Moksha begins not at death, but at that fork, each time you choose.

## Can you be free while still living?

Many Hindu teachers say yes. The idea is jivanmukti, liberation while still in the body. In Advaita Vedanta, the school that teaches that the self and ultimate reality are one, a jivanmukta has seen through the small self and goes on living, eating and speaking, but without being gripped. The life of Ramana Maharshi at Tiruvannamalai is often given as an example. The idea behind it is unpacked in [atman and Brahman](/blog/atman-and-brahman).

Other schools describe it differently. In Vishishtadvaita, the school of Ramanuja, the freed soul remains distinct and lives in loving service of Vishnu. In Dvaita, the school of Madhva, the difference between soul and God is permanent. In the bhakti traditions, moksha is often less a merging than a nearness: the devotee who would rather taste sugar than become it. The Gita holds several of these paths side by side, which is one reason [the Bhagavad Gita for beginners](/blog/bhagavad-gita-for-beginners) is a good next step.

Is moksha the same as the Buddhist nirvana? They share a family resemblance, freedom from the cycle, but the Buddha did not teach an eternal self, and the Upanishads do.

## A way of standing in the world

Moksha may be less a door out of the world than a way of standing in it without being gripped. Nachiketa did not stay in the house of Death. He went home to his father. The difference was that the horses and the songs no longer had a hold on him.
