Ganesha broke off his own tusk because his stylus snapped in the middle of a verse, and he had promised Vyasa that the writing would never stop. That is the Ganesha broken tusk story most people grew up with. It is told as a clever explanation for an odd detail in the image. It is really about something harder: what you are willing to give up so that the work is not interrupted.

## The Ganesha broken tusk story in the Mahabharata

Vyasa, the sage, had the whole of the Mahabharata in his head, a poem so vast that no one could hold it without help. He needed a scribe. Brahma suggested Ganesha.

Ganesha agreed, but with a condition. Once Vyasa began dictating, he must not pause, not for a moment. If he stopped, Ganesha would put down his pen and leave. Vyasa, who knew something about negotiation, added a condition of his own: Ganesha must not write a single verse without first understanding its meaning. Then Vyasa began, and whenever he needed a breath, he composed a verse so knotted that even Ganesha had to stop and think.

Somewhere in that long dictation, Ganesha's stylus broke. He had promised not to stop. So he snapped off one of his tusks, dipped it, and kept writing.

A word of honesty about the source. The dictation episode appears in many printed versions of the Adi Parva, the first book of the Mahabharata, and it is how millions of people know the story. But the scholars who assembled the Critical Edition of the epic, comparing hundreds of manuscripts, set it aside as a later addition. That does not make it less true as a story. It means the tradition kept adding the parts it felt were missing.

## Parashurama, the moon and other tellings

The scribe story is not the only one. In a telling usually traced to the Brahmanda Purana, with a similar one in the Brahma Vaivarta Purana, Parashurama, the warrior-sage and a devotee of Shiva, arrives at Kailasa and demands to see Shiva. Ganesha, guarding the door, refuses. They fight. Parashurama throws the axe that Shiva himself had given him, and Ganesha, out of respect for his father's weapon, does not block it. The axe takes his tusk.

A third telling, beloved of children, has Ganesha riding home late on his mouse after a feast of modakas. The mouse stumbles at a snake, Ganesha tumbles, his belly bursts, and the moon laughs. Ganesha breaks off his tusk and hurls it at the moon, cursing it to wane. This is why, in some regions, people avoid looking at the moon on Ganesh Chaturthi.

Why keep so many stories for one tooth? There are more still; a Tamil telling has him hurl the tusk at the demon Gajamukhasura. Because each region has its favourite, and each says something different. Respect for a father's gift. Anger at mockery. Commitment to a promise. The tradition did not choose. It let the tusk carry all of them. If you want the story of the mouse he was riding that night, there is more in [what Ganesha's mouse means](/blog/ganesha-mouse-vahana-meaning).

## Why is Ekadanta, the one-tusked, worshipped for wholeness?

Here is the paradox. Ganesha is the remover of obstacles, prayed to before any beginning, the god you ask for everything to go smoothly. And he is broken. One of his names is Ekadanta, the one with a single tusk, and the Mudgala Purana counts Ekadanta among his principal forms.

You might expect the god of smooth beginnings to be flawless. The tradition went the other way. In most images, the broken tusk is held in one of his hands, in one of his right hands, like a pen, or sometimes like a weapon. He does not hide it. He carries it.

There is a wry wisdom in that. Anyone who has finished anything real knows it cost something. A manuscript, a degree, a house, a marriage that lasted: none of them came through intact. The one-tusked god is worshipped for wholeness precisely because he knows what wholeness costs. It is not the absence of damage. It is keeping going. ## When the tool fails halfway

The next time a tool fails you halfway through something that matters, a laptop that dies before a deadline, a voice that goes hoarse before a speech, a plan that breaks in the middle, ask what you could offer instead of stopping. Something of your own, perhaps. Time, pride, a piece of the neat picture you had of how it would go.

Keep the work moving, and let the symmetry go. The god who wrote the longest poem in the world did it with a broken tooth, and nobody has ever held that against him.
