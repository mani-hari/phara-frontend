Friends arrived an hour early one Saturday. I was still in my weekend clothes with the vacuum cleaner in my hand, and my face said everything before I did. Since then I think of a line from the Thirukkural on hospitality every time the doorbell rings. The anicham, a flower so delicate it wilts when you smell it, is tougher than a guest; a guest wilts at a single cold look.

## Virunthombal (Hospitality): what the chapter says

The chapter is called Virunthombal, caring for guests, and it sits in the first book of the Tirukkural, the book of virtue, among the chapters on household life. That placement is the argument. Kural 81 says the whole purpose of keeping a home and building up its wealth is to receive guests and do them good. The house, in Valluvar's view, is not a fortress. It is a place designed to be walked into.

Other couplets fill in the picture. Kural 82 says eating alone while a guest waits outside is wrong even if what you are eating is the nectar of immortality. Kural 84 says the goddess of fortune gladly lives in the home of the one who welcomes guests with a smiling face. And the chapter closes with the image that caught me, kural 90:

*Moppak kuzhaiyum anichcham mugandhirindhu*\
*nokkak kuzhaiyum virundhu.*

The anicham fades when smelt; a guest fades when the host's face turns away.

Notice what matters most. Not the menu. Not the size of the house. The face.

## Thirukkural on hospitality in a New Jersey home

Hospitality in a New Jersey suburb comes with its own logistics. Visitors call ahead, mostly. Dietary lists arrive by text: one vegan, one gluten-free, one child who will only eat plain rice. And every so often my parents' friends from Chennai, visiting their own children in the US, come to stay with us for a week, and the house rearranges itself around them.

For a long time my wife and I got this backwards. We worried about the food first. Was there enough, was it good enough, had we made the right sambar? We would open the door already anxious, and the anxiety was on our faces. Guests notice. They start apologising for coming.

The Saturday of the early friends taught me the lesson. I stood at the door with the vacuum, embarrassed, and my wife stepped past me and said, "You came early! Wonderful, come help me cut vegetables." They laughed, came in, and it was one of the best evenings we had had in a long time. The food was late. Nobody cared. Since then we try to lead with the face, not the food.

Thatha, who ran a cloth shop in Kumbakonam, used to say the same thing about customers, and I have written about how hospitality ran through [honest trade in Thatha's shop](/blog/honest-trade-thathas-shop).

## Why is the guest treated like God?

Many of us grew up hearing *atithi devo bhava*, treat the guest as God. The phrase comes from the Taittiriya Upanishad, from the parting instructions a teacher gives to students leaving his home: let your mother be a god to you, your father, your teacher, your guest. It is a religious framing. The guest is a visitor from the divine.

Valluvar's version is quieter and, in a way, more demanding. He never says the guest is God. He says the guest is the reason the house exists. That makes the guest less a blessing to be earned and more a test of generosity, the moment when all our talk of [dharma in everyday life](/blog/dharma-meaning-in-everyday-life) either shows up at the door or does not.

A guest is not a burden to be managed. A guest is a chance to find out who you are when someone needs something from you. The chapter that follows in my mind is the one on love; the [Tirukkural on love](/blog/tirukkural-on-love) calls it the breath of life. And welcoming people well takes steady, unglamorous work, which is exactly what the [Tirukkural on effort](/blog/tirukkural-on-effort) is about.

## Smile before you check the fridge

One small thing for this week: the next time the doorbell rings unexpectedly, smile before you check the fridge. Let your face do the welcoming first. The house can be messy, the food can be simple, the conversation can happen in the kitchen while you both chop onions. The anicham flower will survive. So will the guest, if the first thing they see is a face glad to see them.
