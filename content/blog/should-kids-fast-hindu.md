Most Hindu traditions do not ask children to fast at all. So the honest answer to "should children fast?" is: mostly no, and not in the way adults do. What you can offer them instead is the attention a fast is meant to create. A child can give up one small thing, notice their hunger before a meal, or say a thank-you over their plate. That teaches the heart of fasting without asking a growing body to go without the food it needs.

## Should children fast at all?

The traditions are clear on this, and generous. The customary exemptions from fasting cover children, elders, the unwell, pregnant and nursing women, and anyone whose work or health makes it unwise. The dharma texts that set out fasting rules also set out who need not keep them. Fasting was always meant for bodies that can hold it.

This matters because children often want to fast. They see a parent skipping lunch on Ekadashi, the eleventh lunar day kept by many Vishnu devotees, or keeping [the nine nights of Navratri](/blog/navratri-fasting-rules), and they want to join in. That wish is lovely. It is a child wanting to belong. The task is to honour the wish without handing them an adult's discipline too early. The adults in the house can find their own rules in [Ekadashi fasting rules](/blog/ekadashi-fasting-rules).

Families differ on when to begin. Children also ask about fasting after a death in the family, when adults keep a day of mourning; [explaining death to kids](/blog/explaining-death-to-kids-hindu) covers that gently. Some let teenagers try a partial fast; some wait until adulthood. If your child has any health condition, talk to your paediatrician before any change to their meals.

## A child-sized fast

Here is what a fast can look like for a child. The shape is the same as an adult's, only smaller.

- **Give up one thing.** Sweets, a screen, a favourite snack, for the day. The giving up is the practice, not the hunger.
- **A satvik lunch.** On Ekadashi, pack a simpler school lunch: no onion or garlic, perhaps fruit, curd rice or upma. The child eats fully; the food is just lighter.
- **An evening that fits.** A Navratri evening often falls after soccer practice. Let the child eat a proper dinner, then join the lamp and the song. They are part of the festival without being hungry for it.

A growing body keeps its mealtimes. That is the one rule that does not bend.

## Try this for a week

This is a small experiment for the whole family, not just the child.

For one week, before one meal each day, pause together for a quiet moment. Ten seconds is enough. Everyone looks at the food. Then someone says a thank-you: to whoever cooked, to the farmer, to God, whatever fits your household. Many families use the Gita verse known as the Brahmarpanam, and [the Brahmarpanam prayer before meals](/blog/brahmarpanam-prayer-before-meals) gives the words and their meaning.

Then, once during the week, try this: when a child says "I'm starving", do not rush. Ask: where do you feel it? Is it in your stomach, or are you bored? Notice the hunger together, without drama, and then eat. You are teaching them to watch a sensation instead of being swept along by it. That is exactly what an adult fast is for. You are just skipping the part where they go without.

Children who learn this early often find that an adult fast, when it comes, makes sense. They already know what it is for. More ideas for giving children their own part in festivals are in [festival rituals for kids](/blog/festival-rituals-for-kids) and [raising Hindu kids in America](/blog/raising-hindu-kids-in-america).

## Let them choose the offering

The strongest version of this is the one the child chooses. Ask them what they would like to offer on a festival day. They may say "no dessert". They may say "I'll help cook the prasadam". They may say "I'll not argue with my sister". Any of these is a real offering.

A fast they choose teaches more than one they are made to keep. It teaches that devotion is something you give, not something taken from you. And when they are older and ready, the choice will already be theirs.
