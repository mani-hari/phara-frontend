The first Ganesha we brought home in New Jersey came from a folding table in a temple parking lot, wrapped in newspaper, and sat on my son's lap in the back seat all the way to Edison. He was small then, and he held that clay figure as if it might wake up. Ganesh Chaturthi in the USA often starts like that: a small clay image, bought on the nearest weekend, carried home carefully, and kept for as many days as the calendar and the office allow. The festival asks for very little that cannot be done in a suburban living room, and it gives back a great deal.

## How do families keep Ganesh Chaturthi in the USA?

Ganesh Chaturthi falls on the fourth day of the bright half of Bhadrapada, usually August or September; check the current year's panchangam, because it moves with the moon. The practice itself is simple: bring a clay Ganesha home, worship him for a set number of days, and then let him go in water.

How many days depends on family custom and, in America, on the work week. Some families keep him for one and a half days, some for three, five or the full ten ending on Ananta Chaturdashi. My grandmother in Thanjavur would have found our arithmetic funny: a Tuesday festival, a Saturday gathering, a Sunday goodbye. But the arithmetic is honest, and Ganesha, of all gods, understands obstacles. His festival opens the season for the same reason he is invoked at the start of every puja, the subject of [why Ganesha comes first](/blog/why-ganesha-comes-first).

For the image, look for natural, unbaked clay. Temples, Indian grocers and community groups usually have them in the weeks before.

## The home puja, on a Tuesday if need be

If a priest can come, he will do the pranapratishta, the invocation that invites the deity into the image. If not, a simple invocation in your own words is enough: light the lamp, welcome him, and ask him to stay for the days you have planned.

Then the offerings. Twenty-one blades of durva grass, the slender grass Ganesha is said to love, tied in small bunches. Kozhukattai, as Tamil homes call the steamed sweet dumplings, or modak; my wife makes them the night before, and her recipe is close to [the modak for Ganesh Chaturthi](/blog/modak-for-ganesh-chaturthi) on this site. A lamp in the morning and another in the evening, and a few minutes of chanting, even if it is only his name.

One custom my son loves: we do not look at the moon that night. The story goes that Ganesha, full of sweets, tumbled off his mouse, and the moon laughed. Ganesha cursed him, and so anyone who looks at the moon on Chaturthi risks a false accusation. The Puranas say even Krishna was accused of stealing the Syamantaka jewel after glancing up. My son now shields his eyes dramatically on the driveway. I suspect the lesson he takes is less about the moon than about laughing at someone who falls.

## Community: temples, pandals and the immersion question

Most temples here hold their processions on the nearest weekend, and some community halls set up pandals, decorated pavilions with a large Ganesha, music and food for everyone. Go at least once. For children growing up here, seeing hundreds of people chanting together teaches something a home puja cannot. A friend from Kerala keeps [Onam and its homecoming king](/blog/onam-mahabali-meaning) in the same season, and we go to his too.

Then the goodbye. In much of the US, local rules keep idols out of lakes and rivers, and rightly so. Many families now dissolve the clay in a bucket of water at home and return it to the garden, and [an eco friendly Ganesh visarjan](/blog/eco-friendly-ganesh-visarjan) sets out the steps. And when a colleague asks why there is clay in your flower bed, [explaining Hindu festivals to coworkers](/blog/explaining-hindu-festivals-to-coworkers) may help you find the one sentence you need.

## One small thing to carry into the week

Avvaiyar, the Tamil poet-saint whose [plain-spoken sayings](/blog/avvaiyar-wisdom-sayings) children still learn, sang her Vinayagar Agaval to Ganesha, asking him to lead her inward. You do not need the whole poem. This week, keep him in mind at one doorway, the front door, every time you leave. One second of remembering before the keys and the commute. He is, after all, the god of thresholds, and that is the one we cross every day.
