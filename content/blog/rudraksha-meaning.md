A rudraksha is the dried seed of a tree, and the tradition calls it Shiva's tears. Wear one for a week and it becomes something else too: a small weight against the chest that keeps bringing you back to yourself. The rudraksha meaning lives in both of those facts, the story and the object. You do not need to believe the story literally to find the object useful. Many people find that a bead worn every day becomes a kind of quiet reminder that the body is carrying.

## Rudraksha meaning: the eye of Rudra

The word joins two Sanskrit roots. Rudra is an old name for Shiva, the fierce and the compassionate one. Aksha means eye. So rudraksha is the eye of Rudra, or, as it is often translated, Rudra's tears.

The story traditionally told in the Shiva Purana goes like this. Shiva sat in meditation for a very long time, for the welfare of all beings. When at last he opened his eyes, tears fell from them to the earth, and where they fell, rudraksha trees grew. The seeds of those trees carry the memory of his compassion.

The tree itself is real and very ordinary. It belongs to the Elaeocarpus family and grows in the foothills of the Himalayas, in Nepal, and across parts of Southeast Asia, especially Indonesia. The fruit is blue when ripe. When the flesh is removed, what remains is a hard, deeply grooved seed. That seed is the rudraksha.

## What do the mukhis mean?

Look closely at a bead and you will see natural lines running from top to bottom, dividing it into segments. Each segment is called a mukhi, a face. Most beads have between one and fourteen or more, and tradition gives each number its own meaning and associated deity.

The five mukhi rudraksha is by far the most common. It is traditionally linked with Shiva's five faces, which in some texts represent the five elements and five activities of the divine. It is the bead most often used in a mala and the one most people start with.

Rarer beads, such as one-mukhi or very high counts, are prized, and that is exactly why caution matters. Fakes are common: beads carved to add lines, or seeds from other trees passed off as rudraksha. You will also find sellers who promise that a particular bead will cure illness, bring wealth or fix your horoscope. Treat such claims with scepticism. The tradition values the bead as an aid to remembrance, not as a machine for outcomes.

## How should you wear a rudraksha?

Most people wear a single bead or a small cluster on a thread or a silk cord around the neck, so it rests near the heart. Others wear a full mala of 108 beads plus one, the extra bead, called the meru or guru bead, marking the start and end of a round. Why that number? [Why 108 is sacred](/blog/why-108-is-sacred) gathers the explanations.

How to wear rudraksha beyond that depends on whom you ask. Some teachers say to remove it while sleeping, bathing or visiting a funeral; others say never to take it off. Some recommend washing it occasionally and oiling it lightly to keep it from drying out. Customs vary, and no single rule covers every lineage. If you have a teacher, follow them. If not, keep it clean and treat it with respect.

A rudraksha mala for japa, the repetition of a mantra, is one of the oldest uses of these beads. You move one bead per repetition, often chanting om namah shivaya. For the method, see [how to use a japa mala](/blog/how-to-use-a-japa-mala); for the words, [the meaning of Om Namah Shivaya](/blog/om-namah-shivaya-meaning).

## One bead, one breath

Wear one bead. Just one, on a simple cord. For seven days, each time you notice it against your skin, take one slow, conscious breath. You will notice it often at first, when you bend down, when you change clothes, when it swings forward as you lean over a desk. By the end of the week you may notice it less, and the breath may start to come on its own. That is the point. The bead is not the practice. The noticing is. If you want to take the practice into a temple, the note on [what to wear to a Hindu temple](/blog/what-to-wear-hindu-temple) covers the rest of the outfit.
