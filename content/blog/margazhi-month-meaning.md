Margazhi month meaning, in a sentence: it is the Tamil month of the pre-dawn, roughly mid-December to mid-January, set aside for early-morning prayer, devotional song and the largest kolams of the year, rather than for weddings and new ventures. In Margazhi my mother drew her biggest kolam in the dark, a flashlight in one hand and a bowl of rice flour in the other, humming the Tiruppavai under her breath. By six the whole street smelled of ghee and pepper pongal. I did not know then that the Bhagavad Gita calls this the best of months. I only knew it was the one month the house woke before I did.

## Margazhi month meaning: the dawn of the gods

In the tenth chapter of the Bhagavad Gita, Krishna lists the finest thing in each category of the world and says of himself: "Among months, I am Margashirsha," the northern name for Margazhi. It is a curious choice. Not the month of harvest, not the month of the great festivals, but the cold, dark month just before the sun turns north.

The tradition offers an explanation. A whole human year, it says, is a single day for the gods. Their daytime begins when the sun starts its northward journey in Thai, the month that follows, and Margazhi is the hour just before their sunrise. What the brahma muhurta is to a single day, Margazhi is to the year. If you are curious about that daily version, there is a gentle guide to [the brahma muhurta and waking before dawn](/blog/brahma-muhurta-waking-early).

## Why is Margazhi kept for prayer and not weddings?

Because it is a waiting month, a month of preparation rather than celebration. In the Tamil devotional tradition, the young poet-saint Andal sang the thirty verses of the Tiruppavai as a Margazhi vow, waking her friends in the dark to bathe and go to worship; the poem opens with the name of the month itself. The Shaiva saint Manikkavasagar's Tiruvempavai follows the same pattern of girls calling one another awake. In many South Indian temples one verse is sung each morning of the month.

The month also holds [Vaikunta Ekadashi](/blog/vaikunta-ekadashi-significance), when the northern gate of Vishnu temples is opened at dawn and devotees keep one of the most important fasts of the year; this guide to [Ekadashi fasting rules](/blog/ekadashi-fasting-rules) explains how people keep it. With so much attention turned to prayer, families traditionally hold weddings, housewarmings and big purchases until Thai. There is a Tamil saying that when Thai is born, a way is born. Margazhi is the dark before that way appears. Then comes the harvest, and [the reasons Pongal is celebrated](/blog/why-is-pongal-celebrated) make more sense once you have lived through the waiting.

## Keeping Margazhi from anywhere

I live far from that street now, in a city where the December mornings are grey and my building has a rule against chalk on the corridor floor. So I keep a smaller Margazhi. One morning a week, I wake before the alarm, light a lamp, and read a single pasuram, one of Andal's verses, over breakfast. On Sundays I draw a small kolam with rice flour on a steel tray and set it by the door. My neighbour's daughter asked what it was, and now she wants to draw the next one. The meaning behind the patterns is lovely; this piece on [kolam and rangoli](/blog/kolam-and-rangoli-meaning) tells it well.

In Chennai this is also the music season, when halls fill every evening with Carnatic concerts. You can bring a little of that home too. Play a recording of the Tiruppavai while you cook. If sitting still with a lamp feels unfamiliar, the difference between [meditation and puja](/blog/meditation-vs-puja) is smaller than you might think, and Margazhi mornings are a good place to discover it.

## One small thing for the thirty days

For the thirty days of Margazhi, wake fifteen minutes earlier than usual. Give that time to one thing only: one verse, one lamp or one kolam. Do not add anything else. Check the current year's panchangam for when the month begins where you live, then begin. By the time Thai arrives, you may find you are awake for it.
