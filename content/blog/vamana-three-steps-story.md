A boy with an umbrella and a water pot walks into a king's sacrifice and asks for only as much land as he can cover in three steps. The king laughs, kindly, and says yes. By the end of the Vamana avatar story the boy has measured the whole universe, and the question has changed. It is no longer what the king lost. It is what he gave.

## The Vamana avatar story in the Bhagavata Purana

The eighth book of the Bhagavata Purana tells it most fully. Mahabali, king of the asuras, was the great-grandson of Hiranyakashipu and the grandson of Prahlada, the boy who had once calmed Narasimha; there is more on that family in [the Narasimha story](/blog/narasimha-avatar-meaning). Bali was not a tyrant like his great-grandfather. He was generous, just and loved by his people. He was also very powerful. Through his sacrifices and his virtue he had come to rule all three worlds, and the gods had been pushed out of heaven.

Aditi, the mother of the gods, prayed to Vishnu, and Vishnu was born as her son, a small brahmin boy named Vamana. He came to Bali's great sacrifice, where the king was giving gifts to anyone who asked.

Bali welcomed him warmly and offered him anything: villages, cattle, gold, a kingdom. The boy asked for three steps of land, measured by his own small feet. Bali's teacher, Shukracharya, saw at once who the boy was and warned the king. Do not give it, he said. This is Vishnu, and you will lose everything. Bali considered the warning and answered that he had never gone back on a gift, and would not start now. He poured water over the boy's hand to seal the promise, as the old custom required.

## What does Trivikrama mean?

The moment the water touched his hand, Vamana began to grow. With his first step he covered the earth. With his second he covered the heavens. There was nowhere left for a third.

This giant form is called Trivikrama, the one of three strides, and the idea is much older than the Purana. The Rig Veda, the oldest of the Vedas, praises Vishnu as the god who crosses the worlds in three strides, earth, sky and the space beyond, and whose third step is so high that no bird can reach it. The trivikrama meaning, then, is the small form that turns out to be the largest, and the three steps of Vishnu that hold everything that exists. The boy with the umbrella was the universe all along.

## Was Bali a villain or a hero?

Vamana turned to Bali and asked where he should place his third step. Bali, who had nothing left, bowed his head and said: place it here.

Vishnu placed his foot on the king's head and pressed him down, not to death but to Sutala, one of the lower worlds, which Bali was given to rule. In many tellings Vishnu is so moved by Bali's generosity that he agrees to stand guard at Bali's door himself. The god who took everything becomes the servant of the man who gave it.

Read carefully, the story of Mahabali and Vamana is hard to fit into a tidy moral. The gods get heaven back. But the one who comes out of the story with the most dignity is the asura king who kept his word knowing exactly what it would cost him.

Kerala remembers him with enormous love. The tradition there says Mahabali asked for one favour, to visit his people once a year, and Onam is the festival of his return, in the month of Chingam, late in the summer. Houses are decorated with flower carpets to welcome a king. [Onam and the meaning of Mahabali](/blog/onam-mahabali-meaning) follows the king home. Regional tellings differ in tone: some make Bali proud and in need of humbling, others make him nearly a saint. Both are old. The [ten avatars](/blog/dashavatara-meaning) include Vamana in fifth place, the first to take a fully human shape.

## The size of the yes

Next time you are asked for something that sounds small, a few minutes, a small favour, a little help, notice the size of the yes you give. The story suggests generosity is not measured by the size of the gift but by what it lets go of. Bali gave the one thing he had left, which was himself. The thread runs back to [the old idea of dana, giving](/blog/dana-in-hinduism). For now, one small yes this week, given without counting, is enough.
