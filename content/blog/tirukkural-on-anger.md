One morning on the Turnpike, stopped dead in three lanes of brake lights, I caught myself with knuckles white on the wheel, rehearsing a speech to a driver who had cut in front of me and was already long gone. He was having a fine morning. I was the one on fire. The Thirukkural on anger says exactly this, and most of us learn it the hard way: the fire you nurse burns you first.

## Vegulamai (Not Being Angry): what the chapter covers

Thiruvalluvar, the Tamil poet-sage whose Tirukkural is a book of short two-line couplets, gives anger a chapter of its own. It is called Vegulamai, which simply means not being angry, and it sits in the first book, Aram, the book of virtue. If you have never met the poet, begin with [who Thiruvalluvar was](/blog/who-was-thiruvalluvar); Tamil homes quote him like an uncle.

The chapter opens with a sharp distinction. Kural 301 reads:

*Selvidaththuk kaappaan sinamkaappaan allidaththuk*\
*kaakkinen kaavaakkaal en.*

Roughly: the one who holds back anger where it can actually do harm is the one who truly restrains it; where it can do no harm, what does holding it back even mean? Valluvar is not interested in the calm of a man who has nobody to shout at. He is interested in the moment you have power and choose not to use it.

A few couplets later comes the image I remembered in traffic. Kural 306 calls anger "sinamennum serndhaaraik kolli", the killer of whoever comes near it, and says it burns even *inamennum emappunai*, the raft of kinship that keeps a family afloat. Anger does not stay with its owner. It spreads to the people standing closest.

## Thirukkural on anger at a red light

Here is what embarrassed me that morning. The driver who cut in could not hear a word I said. My anger could not reach him. So it waited, and it found the people who could not answer back.

By the time I got to the office I had an email from a junior colleague asking a question I thought she should already know. I typed a reply that was two sentences too long. That evening my son left his shoes in the hallway, again, and got a lecture sized for a much bigger crime. Nobody on the Turnpike paid for my temper. The people who paid were the ones I had power over.

That is exactly what kural 301 singles out. Anger aimed at someone above you is dangerous to you, so you swallow it. Anger aimed at a junior, a waiter, a child, costs you nothing in the moment, and so it slips out. Valluvar says that is where restraint is actually measured. It is a rule about ethics more than temperament, which is why it sits comfortably beside the idea of [dharma as a way of living ordinary days](/blog/dharma-meaning-in-everyday-life). It is also the natural companion to the chapter on speech; the [Tirukkural on kind words](/blog/tirukkural-on-sweet-speech) is about what we say once we have decided not to shout.

## Is all anger wrong in the Kural?

I do not think Valluvar expects us to become stones. He knew a flash of anger arrives before we have agreed to it. What he warns against is the grudge, the anger we feed and carry around. Kural 303 says, in effect, forget anger toward anyone at all, because every harm follows from it. The word is forget, not suppress.

And then there is kural 304, which I find the most honest line in the chapter:

*Nagaiyum uvagaiyum kollum sinaththin*\
*pagaiyum ulavo pira.*

Is there any enemy greater than anger, which kills both laughter and joy? That is the real cost. Not a sin recorded somewhere, but a dinner table where nobody laughs because Appa is still angry about the traffic. Even the old stories of wealth know this; the tale of [Kubera, the god who guards treasure](/blog/kubera-story-meaning), is partly about what a clenched heart does to plenty.

When I finally understood the chapter, it felt less like a scolding and more like a kindness. It is also, oddly, a lesson in gratitude, because the people we snap at are so often the people who have carried us; there is more on that in the [Tirukkural on gratitude](/blog/tirukkural-on-gratitude).

## Three breaths before you speak

One small thing for this week: the next time anger rises, in traffic or in a meeting, wait three breaths before you speak or type. Just three. Then ask who would have paid for the words you were about to say. Very often the answer is someone who did nothing, someone smaller than you, someone who would have carried it home. Let the breath take the heat. The driver who cut you off will never know. The people at your dinner table will.
