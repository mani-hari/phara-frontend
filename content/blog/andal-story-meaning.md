A girl tried on the garland meant for God, looked at her reflection, and put it back. Her father was horrified. The Lord, the story says, wanted no other garland. The Andal story comes from Srivilliputhur in Tamil Nadu: a foundling named Kodhai, raised by a temple priest, who wore the flowers before offering them and became the only woman among the twelve Alvars, the poet-saints of Vishnu. Her songs, the Tiruppavai among them, are still sung every winter dawn.

## The Andal story from Srivilliputhur

Periyalvar, also called Vishnuchittar, was a devotee who spent his days making garlands for the temple of Vishnu at Srivilliputhur. One morning, the story goes, he found a baby girl lying under a tulsi plant in his garden. He took her in and named her Kodhai.

She grew up among flowers and hymns. Her father told her stories of Krishna, and she fell in love with him, not as an idea but as a person she intended to marry. Every day Periyalvar strung a garland for the Lord, and every day, when he was not looking, Kodhai put it on first. She stood before a mirror, or in some tellings a well, and asked herself whether she was beautiful enough to be the Lord's bride. Then she put the garland back in its basket.

One day her father found a long strand of hair in the garland. He understood what had been happening and was appalled. A garland worn by a person was unfit for God. He scolded her, threw the flowers away and made a fresh one.

## What does soodi kodutha sudar kodi mean?

That night, the Lord appeared to Periyalvar in a dream. Why had he not brought the usual garland, he asked. He wanted the one Kodhai had worn. Nothing else would do.

From then on Kodhai was called Andal, she who rules, even the Lord. She also got a Tamil title that devotees still use: soodi kodutha sudar kodi. It means the shining creeper who gave what she had worn. A creeper, because she wound herself around the Lord the way a vine climbs a pillar. Shining, because her love was bright enough for everyone to see.

The story says a garland worn by a girl became purer than a fresh one. The story is really about which offering counts. Periyalvar followed the rules, and the rules were right in their own terms: you do not offer God your leftovers. But Andal's garland was not a leftover. It carried her. What the Lord wanted, the dream insists, was not a correct object but a person inside the offering.

The garland story is re-enacted to this day. At Srivilliputhur her garland is sent to the great temple at Tirupati for a festival, and to other shrines of the Lord on special days.

## Why does Andal still matter?

Andal wrote two works. The Tiruppavai is thirty verses in which a group of girls wake one another before dawn in the month of Margazhi and go together to wake Krishna; the verses are unpacked in [the meaning of the Tiruppavai](/blog/tiruppavai-meaning), and the month itself in [why Margazhi is special](/blog/margazhi-month-meaning). The Nachiyar Tirumozhi is more intimate, a young woman's longing and her dream of the wedding, verses that still surprise first-time readers with their frankness.

According to Srivilliputhur's temple tradition, she was eventually taken to Srirangam in bridal procession, where she entered the shrine of Ranganatha and merged with the Lord. Her temple at Srivilliputhur, with its tall tower, remains one of the best loved in Tamil country, and the month of [Aadi](/blog/aadi-month-significance) marks her birth star, Pooram, with festivals.

She matters for another reason too. Andal did not wait to be permitted. She did not wait for a priest to approve her devotion, or for her father to arrange her marriage to God. She decided, wore the garland and wrote the songs. Parvati made a similar choice on her mountain, as the story of [Swayamvara Parvathi](/blog/swayamvara-parvathi-story) tells.

## Offering what is yours

Most of us approach worship like Periyalvar. We try to do it properly: the right flowers, the right sequence, the right words, even if the words are in a language we do not quite follow. There is nothing wrong with that. Care is itself a form of love.

But the Andal story leaves room for something else. Offer something today that is unmistakably yours. A song you actually know, even if it is not a hymn. A flower you actually chose from the garden, not the one the list requires. A few words in your own language. The correct thing done at a distance is good. The story suggests the Lord may be waiting for the thing you have held first.
