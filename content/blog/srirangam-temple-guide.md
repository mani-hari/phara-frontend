At Srirangam temple, Vishnu is not standing and not seated. He is lying down, eyes half-closed, on the coils of the serpent Adisesha, on an island between the Kaveri and Kollidam rivers. A whole temple-town has grown up around a god who is resting. The Alvars, the Tamil poet-saints of Vishnu, sang of this place so often that tradition honours it as the first of the one hundred and eight Divya Desams, the sacred abodes of Vishnu.

## Why does Ranganatha at Srirangam temple face south?

The story begins with Rama. After the war in Lanka and his coronation in Ayodhya, Rama gave gifts to those who had stood by him. To Vibhishana, Ravana's brother who had crossed over to Rama's side, he gave the image of Ranganatha, Vishnu reclining, which had been worshipped in his own family for generations. Vibhishana was to carry it home to Lanka.

On the way south he stopped by the Kaveri to perform his evening prayers and set the image down on the island. When he came back, it would not move. He pulled, he pleaded, he wept. Vishnu stayed. But, the story says, he agreed to turn his gaze to the south, toward Lanka, so that Vibhishana would never be out of his sight. Ranganatha lies with his head to the west and his face turned south to this day.

The story says an idol grew too heavy to lift. The story is really about devotion that cannot be carried off. You can take a god home as a gift, and find that he has chosen where to rest. You can also find that he keeps looking at you anyway.

## Seven walls and a town inside them

Srirangam is built as seven concentric prakarams, walled enclosures, one inside the other. The innermost holds the sanctum. The outer ones hold streets, houses, shops, a market, people's kitchens and bicycles. You can live inside the temple here, and many families do. It is a different idea of scale from [the Big Temple at Thanjavur](/blog/thanjavur-big-temple), not far away, where a single tower does the work of seven walls.

It is a good image for how the tradition thinks about the sacred. There is no hard line where the holy begins. It thickens as you walk inward, through gateway after gateway, until you are standing before the reclining god, and then it thins again as you walk back out into the street where someone is selling flowers. The temple is a city, and the city is a temple.

## Songs and a teacher

The Alvars' hymns are gathered in the Nalayira Divya Prabandham, the four thousand sacred verses, and Srirangam appears in them more than almost any other shrine. The most famous voice is Andal's. She was the young woman who wove garlands for Vishnu, wore them herself first, and sang the Tiruppavai in longing for him. Tradition says that at the end of her life she was brought to Srirangam as a bride and merged into Ranganatha. Her story is told in [Andal and what her devotion means](/blog/andal-story-meaning), and her songs in [the meaning of the Tiruppavai](/blog/tiruppavai-meaning).

The temple also belongs to a teacher. Ramanuja, the philosopher who taught that the world and souls are real and live within God as a body lives with its self, spent his later years at Srirangam and shaped how the temple worships, down to the order of its daily rituals. Poetry and philosophy, longing and system, sit in the same enclosure here. The tradition never thought they needed separate buildings.

## Rest is not absence

The reclining god offers another way of looking. We tend to picture the divine as busy: fighting, blessing, dancing. Ranganatha is resting, and nothing is wrong. The world is held. Rest is not absence.

The great festival here is [Vaikunta Ekadashi](/blog/vaikunta-ekadashi-significance), when the northern gate, the door to Vishnu's heaven, is opened and thousands walk through it. A very different Vishnu, a small child standing with his four emblems, is worshipped across the hills in Kerala at [the Guruvayur temple](/blog/guruvayur-temple-guide).

From afar, an archana, the chanting of a deity's names, to Ranganatha with a sankalpam in your name keeps a line open to this island. At home, a lamp lit on Vaikunta Ekadashi or on any Saturday evening does the same. You join the long line of people who have not asked for anything in particular. They have simply come to look at him while he rests.
