In our Edison kitchen, Navratri announces itself with a bowl of chickpeas soaking on the counter. By evening they are sundal, steel boxes are packed, and my son is carrying them to three doorsteps. If you are looking for a sundal recipe for pooja, the answer is humbler than the festival around it: cooked legumes, a quick tempering, fresh coconut, and no onion or garlic. It is offered to the Goddess first and then given away, which is the whole point.

## Sundal recipe for pooja: the basic method

**Ingredients (serves four, with some to give away)**

- 200 g dried chickpeas (1 cup), soaked overnight
- 1 tablespoon coconut oil
- 1 teaspoon mustard seeds
- 1 teaspoon urad dal
- 2 dried red chillies, broken
- A sprig of curry leaves
- A pinch of asafoetida
- Salt to taste
- 4 tablespoons freshly grated coconut (about 25 g)

**Method**

1. Drain the soaked chickpeas and cook them in fresh water with a little salt until just tender. They should hold their shape; mushy chickpeas make a sad sundal.
2. Drain well and let the steam escape for a few minutes.
3. Heat the coconut oil. Add mustard seeds; when they pop, add urad dal and let it turn golden.
4. Add the red chillies, curry leaves and asafoetida, stir for a few seconds, then tip in the chickpeas.
5. Toss with salt over low heat for two minutes.
6. Turn off the heat and fold in the grated coconut.

That is it. No onion, no garlic, nothing that would make it everyday food rather than naivedyam, the portion cooked to be offered before anyone eats.

## Why a different sundal every Navratri night?

Many families rotate the legume across the nine nights: chickpeas one evening, green gram the next, then black-eyed peas, peanuts, rajma, field beans, and so on. My mother in Chennai still names each night by its sundal. Ask her what happened on the fourth day of some long-ago Navratri and she will say, "That was the peanut day," and then remember the rest.

Part of the reason is practical. Golu, the display of dolls on stepped shelves, brings visitors every evening, and every visitor leaves with a small packet of sundal and a betel leaf. Nine nights of chickpeas would test anyone's patience. Part of it is devotional: each night's Goddess gets something fresh. The story behind the sequence is in [why Navratri lasts nine nights](/blog/why-navratri-lasts-nine-nights).

Outside Navratri, Fridays belong to Amman, the Mother Goddess of village and household, and to Lakshmi in many Tamil homes, and a small bowl of sundal at the Friday lamp is a common habit. Other days have their own customs; the [Monday fast for Shiva](/blog/somvar-vrat-monday-fast) is one that many of our friends keep.

## How do you manage sundal on a weeknight in the US?

Navratri rarely cares about office calendars. Here is how we get through it:

- **Instant Pot for the chickpeas.** Soaked chickpeas cook in about fifteen minutes on pressure. Start them before the morning train.
- **Frozen grated coconut.** Every Indian store has it. Thaw a portion in the fridge overnight.
- **Cook early, temper late.** The chickpeas wait in the fridge; the tempering takes five minutes after work.
- **First bowl to the lamp.** Before a single box is packed, a small bowl goes before the Goddess. My son has learned not to reach for the pot until that bowl is placed.

The Tirukkural, in its chapter on hospitality, says that Lakshmi herself lives gladly in the home of the one who welcomes guests with a cheerful face. I think of that line when the doorbell rings at eight-thirty and someone has come for golu.

On busier nights, a jar of [temple-style puliyodarai paste](/blog/puliyodarai-temple-tamarind-rice) or a quick bowl of [daddojanam, curd rice made as prasadam](/blog/daddojanam-curd-rice-prasadam), fills out the plate.

## One small thing to carry into the week

This Navratri, make one sundal, offer it, and then take a small box to a neighbour you have never cooked for. Do not explain too much. Say it is a festival, that it was offered first, and that it is theirs. [Why prasadam is shared](/blog/why-prasadam-is-shared) says it better than I can: the food was never only ours to begin with.
