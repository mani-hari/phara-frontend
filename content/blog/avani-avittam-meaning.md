Every year in Chennai, the morning after the rains, lines of men sat on the steps of the temple tank in new white dhotis, changing their threads together. I was a boy, bored and hungry, waiting for my father to finish. I did not understand it then. I think I understand it now, watching my son start a new school year in New Jersey with a backpack that still smells of the store. Avani Avittam is, underneath everything, a first day of school. It is the annual renewal of a student's vow, and the thread is only the part you can see.

## What is Avani Avittam?

Avani Avittam is the Tamil name for upakarma, the ceremony in which those who wear the sacred thread renew their commitment to Vedic study. It falls around the full moon of the Tamil month of Avani, usually in August, when the moon is near the Avittam star, which gives the day its name. Families of different Vedic lineages keep it on slightly different days: Yajur Veda families on the full moon itself, Rig Veda families by their own star, Sama Veda families some weeks later. My aunt in Chennai still phones around to confirm which day is whose; check the current year's panchangam for yours.

It sits in the middle of the monsoon festivals, just after [the Tamil month of Aadi](/blog/aadi-month-significance) closes and in the same season many homes keep [Naga Panchami](/blog/naga-panchami-meaning). In the old rhythm of life, the rains kept people at home, and a season spent at home was a good season to begin study again.

## Why change the sacred thread?

The yagnopavitam, called poonal in Tamil, is a loop of cotton threads worn over the left shoulder, first given at the upanayanam, the thread ceremony. It is a vow worn on the body. On Avani Avittam the old thread is set aside and a new one put on with mantras, usually in a group led by a priest.

Before that comes the part I find the most human of all. The men recite the Kamo'karshit japa, a short formula that says, in effect, desire did it, anger did it, not my better self. It is a plea for pardon for the year's lapses. No one sits at the tank pretending to have kept every vow. They admit they have not, and begin again. The next morning, many repeat the Gayatri mantra a thousand and eight times, the Gayatri japam (for the words themselves, see [what the Gayatri mantra means](/blog/gayatri-mantra-meaning)).

I no longer wear the thread every day. My father does, and on video calls I sometimes see it at the neck of his vest. Watching him, I have come to think the renewal matters more than the perfection.

## Hayagriva and the return of knowledge

The same full moon is kept as Hayagriva Jayanti, for the horse-headed form in which, the Puranas say, Vishnu brought the stolen Vedas back from the bottom of the ocean. Many South Indian families worship Hayagriva, along with Saraswati, as the giver of learning; [Saraswati and Hayagriva](/blog/saraswati-and-hayagriva) tells more of that pairing.

Put the two together and the day becomes larger than a ritual for one community. It is a festival of learning: knowledge lost, knowledge recovered, and a vow to keep studying.

## One small thing for this week

The Taittiriya Upanishad, in its famous farewell to the graduating student, says simply: do not neglect your study. Svadhyayan ma pramadah. It does not say finish your study. It says keep at it.

My son's new timetable is on our fridge. Next to it I have put a note of my own: the Tamil poems I stopped reading in college, the women poets I always meant to return to, [Avvaiyar's sayings](/blog/avvaiyar-wisdom-sayings) among them. Fifteen minutes a night.

This week, pick up one thing you stopped learning, a language, an instrument, a text, a skill, and give it fifteen minutes. You do not need a thread for that. You only need to begin again, which is what the men at the tank were doing all along.
