A colleague once asked me, very kindly, whether Holi was the Hindu Christmas. I opened my mouth and nothing useful came out. I had lived with these festivals my whole life and never practised a simple answer. Explaining Hindu festivals to coworkers turns out to be easy once you have one sentence ready for each: what it celebrates, how families mark it, and whether a greeting is welcome. Most colleagues are not asking for theology. They want to know how to wish you well, and whether they can join in.

## One-line answers for the big festivals

These are the lines I now keep ready. They are not complete, and they are not meant to be.

- **Diwali:** "The festival of lights, in autumn. We light oil lamps, pray for a good year and share sweets. It is about light winning over darkness."
- **Navratri:** "Nine nights honouring the Goddess, in autumn. Some of us fast, some dance garba in big circles, and it ends with Dussehra, good over evil." If they want to know why nine, [why Navratri lasts nine nights](/blog/why-navratri-lasts-nine-nights) has the story.
- **Holi:** "The spring festival of colours. We throw coloured powder, eat too much and forgive old quarrels. The night before, a bonfire recalls a story about a boy who could not be burned." The boy is Prahlada; there is more in [the meaning of Holi, Prahlad and Holika](/blog/holi-meaning-prahlad-holika).
- **Pongal or Makar Sankranti:** "A harvest festival in the middle of winter. We boil new rice in milk until it overflows, as a sign of plenty."
- **Ganesh Chaturthi:** "The birthday of Ganesha, the elephant-headed god of beginnings, in late summer. Families bring home a clay idol and immerse it in water a few days later."
- **Maha Shivaratri:** "The great night of Shiva, in late winter. Many people fast and stay awake through the night in prayer."

Notice that none of these mention a date. Hindu festivals follow the moon, so they move around the Western calendar. If a colleague asks why, [how the Hindu festival calendar works in the USA](/blog/hindu-festival-calendar-usa) is the answer I send them.

## What do coworkers actually want to know?

After many years of these conversations, I have found the questions are almost always the same four.

**Is it a holiday? Will you be out?** Answer plainly, and tell them early. Because the dates move, give your team a few weeks' notice, the way you would for any major holiday.

**What do you eat?** This is the friendliest question and deserves a real answer. Sweets, mostly, and often a special meal. If you bring some in, label the ingredients.

**Can I join?** Usually, yes. Community garba nights, temple Diwali events and Holi gatherings in parks are open to everyone. Say so. It is often what they were hoping to hear.

**Do I say Happy Diwali?** Yes. Happy Diwali, Happy Holi, Happy Navratri, all welcome. Nobody minds a mispronunciation made with warmth. There is a longer piece on sharing one festival in particular, [Diwali at the office](/blog/diwali-at-the-office).

## Explaining Hindu festivals to coworkers without over-explaining

Two habits get in the way, and I had both.

The first is apologising. "It is a bit complicated, there are lots of gods, it is hard to explain." You would not apologise for Thanksgiving. Answer the question that was asked, then stop.

The second is treating every question as a test. A colleague who asks about Holi is not asking you to defend Hinduism, or to know every Purana by heart. They are being friendly. You are allowed to say, "I am not sure; my grandmother would know." That is a perfectly good answer, and it often leads to a better conversation than a lecture would.

The Rig Veda has a line I think of in these moments: truth is one, the wise call it by many names. It was not written for office kitchens, but it works there. People understand festivals of light, harvest and new beginnings from their own traditions. Start from what you share.

## One small thing to carry into the week

Open your team's shared calendar and add two Hindu festivals for the coming season, with one sentence each. Nothing more. It takes five minutes, and it quietly changes the office. Next time, someone else may ask you about it before you mention it.

And if children are part of your household, the same one-sentence habit works at home too; I have found [small festival rituals for kids](/blog/festival-rituals-for-kids) teach more than any explanation.
