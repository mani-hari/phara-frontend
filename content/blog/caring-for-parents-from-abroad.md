The calls from Chennai that frighten me are the ones at an odd hour. Since my father's heart surgery, I have learned that steadiness cannot be built on the frightening calls. It has to be built on the ordinary ones. Caring for aging parents in India from abroad is mostly this: a small number of reliable habits, a clear sense of what is yours to do and what is not, and a practice that keeps you connected on the days when there is nothing to fix.

## Caring for aging parents in India from abroad: what is actually yours to do?

When my father was in hospital, I wanted to do everything and could do almost nothing. It took me a while to work out that the useful things were smaller and steadier than I thought.

- **A weekly call at a fixed hour.** Not only when something is wrong. Sunday morning for me, Sunday evening for them. My mother now has the tea ready before the phone rings.
- **Know their doctors' names.** And their medicines, roughly. When something happens, you want to ask the right questions, not the first ones.
- **One sibling on the ground.** My brother in Chennai goes to the appointments. I do not second-guess him from New Jersey. That took some learning.
- **A shared list.** Medicines, doctors, insurance papers, the neighbour with the spare key. We keep ours in a shared document that my brother, my sister and I can all see.

What is not yours to do is manage their lives from twelve thousand kilometres away. Parents are adults. They have run a household longer than we have been alive.

## A practice, not a panic

Worry needs somewhere to go, or it goes into sleepless nights and snapped conversations. I found it helps to give it a shape.

My mother lights the lamp at six in the evening in Chennai. That is early morning for me. On most days now, I light a small lamp on the kitchen counter at the same moment, before anyone else is awake. We do not talk about it. I simply know that her flame and mine are lit together.

Once a month, on each parent's birth star day, I say a short prayer for them. If you do not know your parents' nakshatras, the piece on [how to find your nakshatra](/blog/how-to-find-your-nakshatra) will help, and there is more on longer prayers in [praying for a sick parent](/blog/praying-for-a-sick-parent).

The Tirukkural has a whole chapter on gratitude, Seynnandri Arithal, knowing the help one has received. Kural 102 reads:

*Kaalaththinaal seydha nandri siridheninum*\
*gnaalaththin maanap peridhu.*

Help given at the right time, however small, is larger than the world. I think of it when I send a small thing at the right moment, a call on the morning of a scan, a lamp on a hospital day. I have written more on [the Tirukkural on gratitude](/blog/tirukkural-on-gratitude).

## When guilt arrives

It will. Usually at a festival, or when a cousin who lives near them posts photographs from a family lunch.

Distance is not neglect. Most of us left for reasons our parents supported, and often encouraged. What matters is not how often you visit but how well: a visit where you actually sit with them, accompany them to the temple, fix the leaking tap and listen to the same stories again.

Ask your parents what help they want instead of deciding for them. My father did not want a nurse. He wanted someone to sort out his phone. My mother wanted me to call her sister. Neither was on my list.

For the days when it feels heavy, I wrote a companion column on [spiritual strength for caregivers](/blog/caregiver-spiritual-strength).

## One small thing to carry into the week

Put your parents' birth stars into your phone calendar, repeating each month. On those days, light a lamp for them wherever you are, and send a one-line message: "Lit a lamp for you today." That is all. You will be surprised how often the reply is a photograph of their lamp, lit at the same time.
