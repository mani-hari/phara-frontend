An evening lamp ritual takes two minutes: light a lamp at dusk, say a short verse, and pause with whoever is home. That is all it asks. My grandmother lit hers at dusk no matter what was happening. The television went off mid-serial, the front door and the back door were opened, and for two minutes the whole house in Thanjavur seemed to breathe out. We children grumbled about the serial. I would give a great deal now for those two minutes back.

## The evening lamp ritual: sandhya deepam

The lamp lit at dusk has a name: sandhya deepam, the lamp of the sandhya, the junction between day and night. In many South Indian homes, it is lit near the entrance or at the altar as the sun goes down. Doors and windows are opened, traditionally to welcome Lakshmi, the goddess of prosperity, who is said to visit at this hour and to pass by houses that are dark and shut.

My grandmother was not superstitious about much, but she was firm about this. The lamp was lit, the hands were washed, and the children were made to stand in front of it and fold their hands. Then she recited, always the same verse, shubham karoti kalyanam: may this light bring what is good and auspicious, health and plenty, and may it destroy the enemy that is harmful thinking. I learned it by heart long before I understood that last line. I think about it more now than I did then. Harmful thinking is the only enemy most of us meet on an ordinary evening.

## Why does dusk matter?

Dusk is the hinge of the day. The light is going, the body is shifting gears, the mind starts counting the day's unfinished things. In a village, that was the hour the cattle came home and the lamps came out. In my life, it is the hour the last meeting ends and the commute begins, or, on work-from-home days, the hour I realise I have not left the desk since lunch.

Without a boundary, the workday simply leaks into the evening. Emails at the dinner table, a laptop open on the sofa, one more message at ten. The old ritual put a lamp exactly at that hinge. It is a way of saying: the day is done, the home begins. I did not see that as a child. I see it very clearly now, when I light the lamp on my own kitchen windowsill in a suburb far from Thanjavur, and my son, who is eleven, rolls his eyes and then comes to stand beside me anyway.

## The two-minute ritual

Here is the version I keep, which is my grandmother's with the edges worn down.

Light one lamp at sunset, or as soon as you get home. Brass or clay, ghee or sesame oil; [ghee lamp or oil lamp](/blog/ghee-lamp-or-oil-lamp) explains the difference if you want to choose. Open a door or a window, even a crack. Say one short verse: shubham karoti, or the deepa jyothi mantra, or the Gayatri, which was always meant for the sandhya hours; see [the Gayatri mantra's meaning](/blog/gayatri-mantra-meaning). If you know none of these, say thank you for the day. Then stand or sit for a moment with whoever is home. Nobody has to say anything.

The best time to light the lamp in the evening is around sunset, but consistency matters more than precision. A lamp lit at the same time every day becomes a boundary; a lamp lit perfectly once a month is only a nice gesture. If you want to fold it into a slightly longer evening prayer at home, the [five-minute daily puja](/blog/five-minute-daily-puja) is a good shape. And once a year, around the full moon of the Tamil month of Karthigai, the single lamp becomes dozens; [celebrating Karthigai Deepam at home](/blog/karthigai-deepam-at-home) shows how. For the meaning behind it all, read [why Hindus light lamps](/blog/why-do-hindus-light-lamps).

## One small thing for this week

This week, set a reminder on your phone for sunset. When it goes off, light a lamp before you open your laptop again. Just once a day, for seven days. Notice what the house does in those two minutes. Mine still breathes out, the way my grandmother's did, even with the television long gone and the serial replaced by a phone.
