As a child, the story goes, Hanuman saw the rising sun and leapt for it, thinking it was a ripe fruit. Indra struck him down with the thunderbolt, and his jaw, *hanu*, was scarred for good, which is how he got his name. The story is about a boy with far too much strength. The Hanuman Jayanti significance lies in what happens next: the festival celebrates the moment that strength learns to serve.

## Why is Hanuman Jayanti on different days?

Ask three families when Hanuman Jayanti falls and you may get three answers. In much of North India it is kept on Chaitra Purnima, the full moon of the spring month of Chaitra, usually March or April, a week or so after Ram Navami. In Tamil Nadu, Hanuman Jayanti is kept in the winter month of Margazhi, on the new moon, traditionally linked with the Mula star, so it usually lands in December or January. In Andhra Pradesh and Telangana many families begin a period of devotion on the Chaitra full moon and close it on the tenth day of the waning moon in Vaishakha, which they keep as the birth day. In Karnataka many keep Hanuman Vratam in the month of Margashira. Other regions, including Kerala and Odisha, have their own customs too.

This confuses people, and it should not. It is the same devotion kept by several calendars, each region remembering the birth by its own reckoning. For the date your family or temple follows, check the current year's panchangam or a [Hindu festival calendar for the USA](/blog/hindu-festival-calendar-usa). The spring version sits in a crowded season, close to the lunar new year of [Ugadi and its bitter-sweet start](/blog/ugadi-significance) and the Tamil [Puthandu, kept far from home](/blog/puthandu-tamil-new-year-usa).

## The strength he forgot

There is a second story, and it is the one I think the festival is really about. The young Hanuman, full of mischief, made a nuisance of himself among the forest sages, tugging at their robes and scattering their fire offerings. One of them, exasperated, laid a curse on him: he would forget his powers, and remember them only when someone reminded him.

So Hanuman grew up forgetting. He served the monkey king Sugriva, capable and loyal, but ordinary. Then, in the Kishkindha Kanda of the Valmiki Ramayana, the search party looking for Sita reaches the southern shore and stares at the ocean. Nobody can leap that far. Everyone is despairing. And the old bear-king Jambavan turns to the one monkey sitting quietly apart and begins to tell him who he is: the son of the wind, the child who leapt for the sun. As Jambavan speaks, Hanuman grows, until he fills the horizon, and then he leaps across the sea to Lanka.

The story says a curse made him forget. The story is really about how often we need someone else to tell us what we can do. Most of us are sitting on some shore, sure the gap is too wide. Hanuman, the strongest figure in the epic, still needed a Jambavan.

## Hanuman Jayanti significance in the home

How the day is kept depends on where you are. In the South, the offering is often a *vadai malai*, a garland of crisp urad dal vadas strung together, and garlands of betel leaves; there is a recipe for [vadai mala for Hanuman](/blog/vadai-mala-for-hanuman) if you want to try it at home. In the North, the image is smeared with sindoor, and laddoos are offered. Across the country, people read the Hanuman Chalisa or the Sundara Kanda, the book of the Ramayana in which Hanuman crosses to Lanka and finds Sita.

Notice what all of these have in common. The strength is offered, not displayed. Hanuman is never shown lifting weights in the temple. He is shown kneeling, palms joined, or carrying a mountain for someone else's wounded brother. Every festival in the year teaches through its food and its images; Onam teaches abundance with its [ada pradhaman](/blog/ada-pradhaman-for-onam), and Hanuman Jayanti teaches service with a garland made of the humblest snack in the kitchen.

## Whom is your strength working for?

The strongest figure in the Ramayana spends the entire epic as someone's messenger. He could have ruled. He chose to carry things: a ring to Sita, a mountain of herbs to Lakshmana, news to Rama. On Hanuman Jayanti, ask yourself a quiet question. You have strengths, perhaps more than you remember. Whom are they working for? And who, around you, is sitting on a shore waiting for someone to remind them who they are?
