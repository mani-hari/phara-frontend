The Bhagavad Gita compares rebirth to changing clothes. "As a person sets aside worn-out garments and puts on new ones," Krishna says in the second chapter, "so the embodied self sets aside worn-out bodies and enters new ones." It is a comforting image, and conversations about reincarnation in Hinduism often begin and end with it. But the texts say more, and they do not all say the same thing.

## What do the texts actually say about reincarnation in Hinduism?

Two words first. Punarjanma means rebirth, literally "birth again". Samsara means the round of wandering: the cycle of birth, death and rebirth through which, the tradition says, beings move from life to life. The idea is faint in the oldest hymns, grows clearer in the Upanishads, and by the time of the Gita it is taken for granted.

The clothing image comes in chapter two, often called Sankhya Yoga, where Krishna answers Arjuna's grief on the battlefield. For readers new to the text, [the Bhagavad Gita for beginners](/blog/bhagavad-gita-for-beginners) suggests where to start.

The Brihadaranyaka Upanishad has a different image, and in some ways a better one. It compares the self at death to a caterpillar on a blade of grass. Before it lets go of one blade, it reaches out and takes hold of the next. The self, the text says, does the same: it gathers itself and reaches towards the next body before releasing this one. Nobody is thrown into the void. There is always a next blade.

## What is it that is reborn?

Here the texts are careful. The atman, the true self, does not change. In the Katha Upanishad and the Gita it is unborn and undying, not killed when the body is killed. So the atman is not what becomes someone else.

What moves on is the subtle body, the sukshma sharira: mind, intellect, and the samskaras, the impressions and tendencies left by what one has done and thought. Shaped by karma, the consequences of action, these tendencies carry forward like a current. [Karma explained simply](/blog/karma-explained-simply) goes through the first idea; [the three gunas](/blog/three-gunas-explained), the qualities of clarity, restlessness and inertia, are what colour those tendencies.

So in the Upanishadic picture, the one who is reborn is not quite the same person and not quite a different one. The Brihadaranyaka puts it plainly: as a person acts, so that person becomes.

## Is rebirth a punishment?

This is where people grow uneasy, and fairly so. Popular telling sometimes makes rebirth sound like a courtroom: you did wrong, so you come back as something lesser. Schools and teachers differ on the details, and some tellings do lean that way. But the texts more often use the language of learning and ripening. A tendency not yet worked through returns to be worked through. In the sixth chapter of the Gita, Krishna says that a person who falls short in their practice is reborn where the practice can continue, and picks up where they left off.

And rebirth is not the goal. The goal is [moksha](/blog/what-is-moksha), release from the round altogether. Read that way, the cycle is less a prison sentence than a long school, and moksha is not an escape from a courtroom but a kind of graduation.

Do all Hindus believe in reincarnation? Most Hindu traditions assume it, but belief varies from person to person, and many practising Hindus hold it loosely, as a question rather than a certainty. The tradition has room for both.

## Which tendencies are you strengthening?

Whether or not you hold to rebirth, the texts are asking the same question of this life. The caterpillar is always reaching for the next blade of grass: the next habit, the next reaction, the next choice. Which tendencies are you strengthening today? The Gita's answer, worked out in [work without attachment](/blog/gita-on-work-without-attachment), is that you can act fully without clinging to the result. That, too, is a kind of rebirth, one morning at a time.
