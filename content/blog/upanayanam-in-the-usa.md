In the old telling, the boy at his upanayanam turns first to his mother and holds out his hands for food. It is the first act of a student: to ask. Upanayanam is the sacred thread ceremony that begins a life of learning, and families planning an upanayanam in the USA, at a temple here or on a trip home, often find that this small moment of begging, called bhiksha, is the one everyone remembers. The first person to give is the one who has already given him everything. It is the lesson, placed at the very start.

## What does the sacred thread actually mean?

The word upanayana means "leading near", and the person the boy is led near to is the teacher. In the older texts, it was the moment a child symbolically left his parents' house for the house of learning. The thread itself, the yajnopavita, called the poonal in Tamil homes, is worn over the left shoulder and across the body.

It has three strands, and families explain them in different ways. Some say they stand for three debts: to the sages who gave us knowledge, to the ancestors who gave us life, and to the gods who sustain the world. Others read them as thought, word and deed, which ought to stay in line with one another. Grandmothers tend to give the simplest version: three strands, so you remember.

The heart of the ceremony is brahmopadesam, the whispered teaching of the Gayatri mantra. The father, or the officiating teacher, draws a cloth over himself and the boy and says the mantra into his ear. The story says a secret is being passed on. The story is really about intimacy: some things are learned only when someone leans close and speaks to you alone. Families who want to go through the words with their son beforehand can start with [what the Gayatri mantra means](/blog/gayatri-mantra-meaning).

## How do families plan an upanayanam in the USA?

Families usually plan it in one of three ways. Several larger temples hold group upanayanams, where a number of boys receive the thread together over a single morning, with priests guiding each family. Others hold a private ceremony at the temple or at home, with a priest who comes to them. And many families time it with a summer trip to India, so the grandparents can be present and the ceremony can happen at the family's own temple.

The age varies by community. The texts give an ideal age, but in practice many families choose somewhere between seven and the early teens, and abroad the question becomes practical: when can he understand what he is promising, and when does it fit around school? Schools are usually understanding, and there is a note on [asking school for a day off for a Hindu festival](/blog/school-day-off-for-diwali) that works for family ceremonies too. Look for a priest who explains each step in English as he goes. A boy who understands the fire, the thread and the whisper carries more away from the day.

The upanayanam is one of a series of rites that mark a Hindu life, and the overview of [the sixteen samskaras](/blog/sixteen-samskaras-explained) shows where it sits among them, after earlier childhood rites such as [the first haircut](/blog/mundan-first-haircut).

## What it asks afterwards

The ceremony takes a morning. What it asks for takes years. Traditionally, the boy now performs sandhyavandanam, the daily prayers at the twilights of dawn and dusk, with offerings of water and the Gayatri. He wears the thread and changes it when it wears out. Once a year, on Avani Avittam, which falls on the full moon of the Tamil month of Avani, he joins others to renew it with a fresh thread and a fresh resolve.

Here is the honest question: what can an American teenager keep? Some boys keep the full practice. Many keep a shorter version, the Gayatri said a set number of times each morning. Some take the thread off for swimming or sports, and families differ on whether that is acceptable. Many parents and priests suggest agreeing on one realistic daily practice and seeing what grows. Holding on to a practice far from home is its own discipline; anyone who has tried [celebrating festivals alone abroad](/blog/festivals-alone-abroad) knows the question from another side.

## A promise to keep learning

The story of the boy asking his mother for food is really about a promise: that he will stay the kind of person who asks, who is willing to be taught. The thread is only a reminder of that promise. Before the day, sit with your son and ask him which part of the ritual he most wants to understand. Then let him put that question to the priest himself. It may be the first thing he asks as a student. It will not be the last.
