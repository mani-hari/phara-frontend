When an illness passes, gratitude often arrives as restlessness. You want to do something. The body is back, or mostly back, and there is a feeling in the chest that needs somewhere to go. A thanksgiving pooja after recovery gives it that place. The tradition has a whole list of good somethings to choose from, from a coconut broken at a temple to a meal served to strangers, and almost all of them can be small.

## What is a thanksgiving pooja after recovery?

It is whatever act of worship you choose to mark the return of health. There is no single rite by that name. Families commonly do one of these:

- **An archana or abhishekam in your name.** An archana is a short recitation of the deity's names with flowers, done at a temple with your name and birth star; an abhishekam is the ritual bathing of the deity with water, milk or other offerings. Either can be done in person, or at a temple in India on your behalf.
- **A coconut broken at the temple.** The coconut is one of the oldest offerings in South Indian worship. Breaking it is often read as breaking the ego, the hard shell, to offer what is sweet inside; [why Hindus break a coconut](/blog/why-break-a-coconut) explains the custom.
- **A lamp lit at home.** Every evening for a week, or on the first day you felt well again.

Small and sincere is enough. A lamp lit by someone who can finally stand at the shrine again is a very large offering.

## How do you keep a vow made during illness?

In a frightening moment, many people make a promise to God. If I recover, I will visit this temple. I will offer my hair. I will feed a hundred people. In Tamil, a vow owed to God like this is called a nerthikadan, literally a debt of promise.

Common ones include a temple visit, tonsure at a temple such as Tirupati or Palani, annadhanam, the gift of food to anyone who comes, or a fast on a particular day, such as [the monthly Sashti viratham for Murugan](/blog/monthly-sashti-vratham). If yours involved feeding people, [annadhanam, feeding as worship](/blog/annadhanam-feeding-as-worship) explains why the tradition counts it as worship.

A few practical points.

- **Keep it as your body now allows.** If you promised to climb a hill temple and your knees no longer can, you are not in breach. The intention was gratitude; the form can change.
- **Ask a priest to adapt it.** Priests deal with this all the time. A vow to walk can become a vow to be carried, or to send someone in your place, or to perform an archana instead.
- **Do not fast if your doctor says not to.** The tradition has always exempted the sick and the recovering. If fasting was part of the vow, ask whether another form of offering would suit you better.
- **Keep it in time, but do not panic about time.** If travel is not possible for months, a small offering now and the full vow later is common.

Those living with a longer illness sometimes find the whole idea of a finished recovery does not fit. For them, I have written about [a practice that bends with chronic illness](/blog/chronic-illness-and-practice).

## Seven mornings, seven people

Here is a gratitude practice that needs no temple.

**Each morning, before rising,** lie still for one minute and name one part of your body that is working. The lungs. The knee that bends. The eyes that opened. Thank it, silently. Many people find this strange for a day or two and then oddly moving. The body did most of the healing, and it rarely gets thanked.

**Each evening,** write to or call one person who helped. Not a group message. One person, by name, for one specific thing. The nurse who was kind at three in the morning. The neighbour who cooked. The friend who drove you to the appointment. The cousin who kept your parents updated.

Seven mornings, seven people. By the end of the week you will have a fairly accurate map of how many hands it took to get you well.

## Gratitude turned outward

Gratitude, in the end, is attention turned outward. The pooja, the coconut, the vow are all ways of pointing that attention at something larger than your own relief. Prasadam, the offered food returned as blessing, works the same way, as [prasad and gratitude](/blog/prasad-and-gratitude) explains.

So include everyone in the circle: the deity, the doctors, the nurse, the neighbour, the person who looked after the one who looked after you. Caregivers often go unthanked; there is a separate piece on [spiritual strength for caregivers](/blog/caregiver-spiritual-strength) for them. And include the body itself, which did the quiet work while everyone else was worrying.
