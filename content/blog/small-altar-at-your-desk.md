A desk altar at work can be tiny: one small figure or a smooth stone, a fresh leaf, and a few seconds of attention before you begin. It does not need to be seen to do its job. A small Ganesha tucked behind the monitor stand is a reminder addressed to your own attention, not a statement to your colleagues. Nobody else has to notice it. You do, and that is the whole point.

Most of us already carry some private ritual into the workday: the same mug, the same first email, the same walk to the coffee machine. A desk altar simply makes one of those moments deliberate. Keep it small enough that it never asks anything of the people around you.

## What goes on a desk altar at work?

Less than you think. Start with one object that holds meaning for you. For many people that is a small brass Ganesha, the remover of obstacles who is traditionally greeted before any new undertaking; the story behind that habit is in [why Ganesha comes first](/blog/why-ganesha-comes-first). A small Ganesha for your office desk can be the size of your thumb. Others prefer a smooth river stone, a tiny picture of a family deity, or a single rudraksha bead.

Add something living if you can. A fresh leaf, a sprig of tulsi or one small flower in a shot glass of water changes the corner completely, because it needs you. It wilts, and you notice, and you replace it. To choose with some care, look up [which flower suits which deity](/blog/flowers-for-pooja). A folded square of cloth underneath keeps the arrangement looking intentional rather than cluttered.

Leave out anything that makes demands on shared space. No flame, no incense, no bell, nothing with a smell or a sound. A home shrine can hold all of that, and the ideas in [setting up a small puja corner in an apartment](/blog/small-puja-corner-apartment) work well there. The office version is quieter by design.

## Is it all right to keep religious items at work?

In most workplaces, yes. Offices usually allow small personal items on a desk, and a modest figure sits comfortably next to family photos and a plant. Still, check your workplace policy, especially if you hot-desk, work in a lab, or share a counter with customers. Some roles have rules about what can sit on a surface, and it is easier to know them in advance.

Keep anything religious on your desk small, tidy and easy to move. If a colleague asks about it, one friendly sentence is enough. "It is Ganesha. I say a quick hello to him before I start." Most people find that charming. You do not need to explain the whole tradition, and you do not need to apologise for it.

A practical note: if the figure ever chips or cracks, there is a gentle way to retire it, described in this piece on [what to do with old idols and photos](/blog/old-idols-and-photos).

## Before you log in

Here is the practice. Before you log in, touch the figure or the stone. Take one full breath, slower than usual, all the way out. Then name the day's main piece of work, silently, in one sentence. That is all. It takes perhaps ten seconds.

After lunch, do the same thing. Touch, breathe, name the afternoon's main task.

Praying at work does not have to look like prayer. You are not closing your eyes at your desk or asking anyone to wait. You are marking a threshold. The morning log-in and the return from lunch are two moments when the mind is at its most scattered: the commute is still in your body, or the meal is making you slow. A small ritual at exactly that hinge tends to gather the attention that would otherwise leak into the inbox.

Watch what happens to your afternoons over seven days. Many people find the post-lunch hour is where the ritual earns its place. This desk practice sits between two larger ideas: [treating work itself as an offering](/blog/work-as-worship), and [praying when you are looking for a job](/blog/prayer-for-a-job-hindu).

## The altar is a hinge

The altar is not the practice. It is a hinge between moods: from travelling to working, from eating to thinking, from reacting to choosing. The practice is noticing when you cross it. Some days you will touch the figure and feel nothing at all. That is fine. Touch it anyway, take the breath, name the work, and begin.
