The link between prasad and gratitude is simple: when you offer food before eating it, the meal stops being something you take and becomes something you receive. The same laddu tastes different after it has been offered at the altar. Nothing in the laddu changed. Something in you did. You slowed down, you paused, you treated it as a gift. That pause is the whole practice, and you can feel its effect in your body even if you are not sure what you believe.

## What is prasad, and why offer food first?

What is prasad in Hinduism? The word means grace. Before it is offered, the food is called naivedyam, the offering placed before the deity during worship. After it has been offered, it comes back as prasad, a blessing, and is shared among everyone present. That is why prasad is received with the right hand, never wasted, and almost always shared.

Why do we offer food to God first? The idea underneath is that nothing we have is really ours. The rain, the soil, the farmer, the cook, the body that can digest: all of it arrived as a gift. Offering the food first is a way of acknowledging that. You return the gift, and it is given back to you. The food is the same. Your relationship to it is not.

If you have ever received prasadam from a temple, you know the feeling. People eat a tiny pinch with a care they would never give a whole meal. [Why prasadam is shared](/blog/why-prasadam-is-shared) explores why it is always divided among many, and [what to do with prasadam](/blog/what-to-do-with-prasadam) covers the practical side when it arrives at your door.

## Prasad and gratitude at the table

In the Bhagavad Gita, Krishna says something disarming: whoever offers me a leaf, a flower, a fruit or water with devotion, I accept it. Not a feast. A leaf. The size of the offering does not matter; the attitude does.

This makes the practice available at every meal, not only on festival days. Many families keep a small version at home. Before eating, a little food is placed before the lamp or simply held in the mind as an offering, a few words are said, and then the meal begins. Some sprinkle water around the plate. Some say a verse. Some just close their eyes for a breath.

The key is the pause. In most of our lives, food arrives and disappears while we look at a screen. The offering interrupts that. It says: stop, look at what is here, notice where it came from.

## How food as offering changes the body and mind

Try this and watch what happens. Before a meal, pause. Look at the plate for a few seconds. Silently offer it, to God, to the earth, to whoever grew and cooked it. Then eat the first three bites slowly, with full attention on taste and texture.

Here is what many people notice. You eat more slowly, because the pause has already slowed you down. You taste more, because the first bites have your attention. You feel full sooner and more clearly, because you are actually listening to the body. And you waste less, because it is hard to scrape an offering into the bin. This is why many people see a connection between this practice and mindful eating in the Hindu tradition. The tradition's focus is devotion, but the effect on attention is something anyone can experience.

Food offered this way also connects to the rest of the day. The same pause before eating is the pause before a mantra, or before lighting a lamp; [Om Namah Shivaya](/blog/om-namah-shivaya-meaning) is a common verse to say at that moment. It is related to the reason puja and meditation work together, explained in [meditation vs puja](/blog/meditation-vs-puja), and to the question of [why Hindus do rituals](/blog/why-do-hindus-do-rituals) at all.

## Try this for a week

For seven days, choose one meal a day. Before eating, pause. Offer it silently, in whatever words make sense to you, or none. Then eat the first three bites with full attention: no phone, no conversation, just taste. After the week, notice what changed. Not in the food. In how you ate it, and how you felt afterwards.
