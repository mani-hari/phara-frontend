At the Guruvayur temple in Kerala, Krishna is not the charioteer of the Gita or the flute-player of the Gopis. He is a small child, standing, holding a conch, a discus, a mace and a lotus in his four hands, and people come to him the way you come to a child: gently, with something to eat, a little amused at themselves. He is called Guruvayurappan, the lord of Guruvayur, and for many Kerala families he is less a distant god than a member of the household.

## Why is it called Guruvayur temple?

The name holds its own story. Tradition says the image worshipped here was once in Dwaraka, Krishna's city by the sea. When Krishna left the world, Dwaraka was swallowed by a flood. Brihaspati, the guru of the gods, and Vayu, the wind god, found the image floating on the waters and carried it south, looking for the right place to install it. At a lotus pond in Kerala they met Shiva, who told them this was the spot. Guru plus Vayu gave the town its name: Guruvayur.

It is a story about rescue, and about the idea that something precious does not stay lost. Notice who does the carrying. Not a king, not an army. A teacher and the wind.

## What is the Narayaneeyam?

The Narayaneeyam is a Sanskrit poem that condenses the whole of the Bhagavata Purana, the great book of Vishnu and Krishna, into about a thousand verses. It was composed at Guruvayur by Melpathur Narayana Bhattathiri. Tradition says he was suffering from a painful illness and came to the temple to pray. He set himself a discipline: one dasakam, a set of about ten verses, each day, sung before the deity. He finished in about a hundred days, and tradition says his condition eased and that in the last dasakam he described the Lord as he saw him.

Whatever you make of the ending, the method is worth borrowing. Ten verses. One day at a time. A whole scripture held not in a single effort but in small daily portions.

Try reading one dasakam slowly, in translation if you need to. Read it aloud. Notice where your attention goes: which image holds it, and where it drifts off to lunch or tomorrow's meeting. That noticing is the practice. The verses will wait for you to come back.

## Thulabharam and feeding the child

The most visible custom at Guruvayur is thulabharam. A devotee sits on one side of a large balance, and on the other side the family piles an offering until the scales level: bananas, sugar, jaggery, butter, coconuts, even flowers. That amount is then given to the temple. It is a vow made physical. You are offering, quite literally, your own weight. Kerala's other great pilgrimage asks for a vow of a different kind, lived in the body for weeks: [the forty-one day Sabarimala vratham](/blog/sabarimala-ayyappa-vratham).

Butter and bananas are the offerings you would give a child, and that is the point. Krishna here is the boy who stole butter in Gokulam, and his devotees keep feeding him. Many Kerala families also bring their babies here for choroonu, a child's first taste of rice, fed in the temple so the first meal is shared with Krishna. How other regions keep the same ceremony, from Bengal to Kerala, is in [annaprashan, a baby's first rice](/blog/annaprashan-first-rice).

Another shrine with a strong sense of place lies to the east, where Shiva dances: [the Chidambaram temple and its empty space](/blog/chidambaram-nataraja-temple). And a very different Vishnu, reclining and at rest, is described in [the Srirangam temple guide](/blog/srirangam-temple-guide).

## Beginning the day by feeding someone

From afar, an archana, a chanting of Krishna's names, offered in your name keeps you close to this temple. On [Krishna Janmashtami at home](/blog/krishna-janmashtami-at-home), many families set out butter and sweets for the child god, and the Guruvayur spirit is easy to bring into that night.

But try something smaller first. For one week, each morning before your own breakfast, offer a little butter or a banana to Krishna at your home shrine, or simply on a clean plate by a picture. Say one line of the Narayaneeyam, or one line of anything. Then eat.

Notice how it feels to begin the day by feeding someone else. It changes the pace of a morning. For a few seconds, you are not the one who needs something. You are the one looking after a child.
