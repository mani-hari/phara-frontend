Take a clay pot. The space inside it seems to belong to the pot: it has the pot's shape, the pot's size, and you could say "the space in this pot" as if it were a possession. Now break the pot. Where does the space go? Nowhere. It was never separate from the space in the room. That, in one image, is most of Advaita Vedanta explained simply: what feels like a separate self is not other than the whole, and the boundary, however real it looks, is not the last word.

## Advaita Vedanta explained simply: not-two

Advaita means not-two. The claim is bold and short. Brahman, the one reality underlying everything, alone is real. The atman, the self you mean when you say "I", is not other than Brahman. Not a piece of it, not a spark from it, not a servant of it. The same thing, seen through a limit.

The teacher most associated with this view is Adi Shankara, who wrote commentaries on the principal Upanishads, the Bhagavad Gita and the Brahma Sutras, the three texts every Vedanta school has to answer to. He did not invent the idea. The Chandogya Upanishad already has a father telling his son, "Tat tvam asi": you are that. Shankara's work was to read the whole tradition through that sentence and show that it held. The two terms, taken one at a time, are the subject of [atman and Brahman](/blog/atman-and-brahman).

## The pot and the space

The pot image is an old one in Advaita teaching. The space inside the pot is called ghatakasha, pot-space. The space outside is mahakasha, great space. Nobody thinks the space inside is a different kind of space. It only looks bounded because there is clay around it.

The story says the pot breaks and the space is revealed. The story is really about limits that feel real without being final. Your body, your name, your history, your worries about the mortgage: these are the clay. They are not illusions in the sense of not existing. You cannot walk through the wall of a pot. But they are not what the space is made of.

Notice what the image does not say. It does not say the pot is bad or that you should smash it. A pot is useful. You can carry water in it. Advaita has no quarrel with living a life with a shape.

## Then why do I feel separate?

Here Shankara reaches for his most famous example. In dim light, you see a coiled rope on the path and take it for a snake. Your heart races, you step back, you may even shout. The fear is completely real. The snake is not. When someone brings a lamp, the snake does not run away. It simply was never there.

The name for the dim light is avidya, ignorance, not in the sense of stupidity but of not-seeing. The lamp is jnana, knowledge, the direct recognition of what was there all along. Liberation, in this view, is not going somewhere or becoming something. It is seeing the rope. That is why Advaita speaks of [moksha](/blog/what-is-moksha) as recognition rather than reward.

Not every Vedantin agrees, and the disagreement is where the tradition gets interesting. Ramanuja's Vishishtadvaita holds that the self is real and distinct, yet inseparable from God, as a body is from the one who lives in it. Madhva's Dvaita keeps the devotee and God distinct for good, because devotion needs two: someone who loves and someone who is loved. Many devotees who sing to Krishna in the morning would rather keep the snake, so to speak, than lose the one they sing to. The tradition allowed all three to argue for centuries, and nobody was asked to leave; [debate as a form of devotion](/blog/debate-as-devotion-shastrartha) is an old Hindu habit. Even [karma, explained simply](/blog/karma-explained-simply) looks different depending on which school you ask.

## Remember the space

It is easy to hear Advaita as cold, as if it asks you to stop caring about your life because none of it is ultimately real. That is not how the old teachers used it. They used it to take the edge off fear.

When you lose a job, or a person, or your temper, the pot is under strain. Advaita does not ask you to stop seeing pots. It asks you to remember the space. The limit is real enough to feel. It is not real enough to define you.
