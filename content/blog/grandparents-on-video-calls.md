On Sunday mornings in New Jersey, my father in Tamil Nadu chants the Suprabhatam into a phone and my son, about eleven, tries to keep up. He usually loses by the second verse and they both laugh. The Venkatesa Suprabhatam is a dawn hymn, and my father chants it at his dusk so that it reaches our dawn. I did not plan any of this. It started one winter while my father was recovering from heart surgery, and it never stopped. If you are wondering whether grandparents teaching prayers online can actually work, from what I have seen at our kitchen table, it works better than almost anything else we have tried.

## Grandparents teaching prayers online: why does it work?

Elders have two things parents rarely have in the evening: time and patience. My father will repeat a line twenty times without a sigh. I manage about three. He is also not responsible for homework, bedtime or screen limits, so a prayer from him arrives without any of the weight of a parent's instructions.

More than that, the prayer becomes a relationship rather than a lesson. My son does not think of the Suprabhatam as a thing to memorise. He thinks of it as Thatha's song, the thing they do together on Sundays. When he gets a line right, my father claps. When he gets it wrong, my father pretends to fall asleep. The verse is almost incidental. That is why it sticks.

For a first prayer, we started with something short. Karagre vasate Lakshmi, a morning sloka said while looking at the palms of your hands: Lakshmi at the fingertips, Saraswati at the centre, Govinda at the base. It is four lines, it involves a gesture, and a child can say it before getting out of bed. There are more like it in [simple mantras for kids](/blog/simple-mantras-for-kids).

## Getting the time zones right

The practical part matters more than people think. Chennai is nine and a half hours ahead of New Jersey in the summer and ten and a half in the winter, when the clocks here go back and India's do not. For years I got this wrong twice a year.

The sweet spot is a US morning, which is India's evening, or a US evening, which catches India's early morning. A Sunday breakfast call lands just as my parents are finishing their evening lamp. A weeknight call before my son's bedtime reaches them at dawn, just as my father sits down for his morning prayers, and a child can simply join what is already happening. If you also book pujas or watch temple ceremonies from here, [joining a puja across time zones](/blog/joining-a-puja-across-time-zones) has a fuller guide.

## Let the elders lead the lamp

On our calls, my mother lights the lamp at six, as she always has, and the camera shows it behind her. On our side, my son lights one too. Two small flames, two kitchens, half the world apart. Neither of them says anything special. They just light the lamp together.

Keep the routine short. A lamp, one verse, a question about school, done. A long recital over a crackling connection tests everyone's patience, especially a child's. Let the elder lead, and let the child have one job. Grandparents are also good at gentle answers to questions like [whether children should fast](/blog/should-kids-fast-hindu), because they have usually watched several generations try. If a grandparent is unwell, or the calls grow harder, [recording elders' chants](/blog/recording-elders-chants) is worth doing sooner rather than later. And when children eventually ask about the harder things, illness and death among them, [explaining death to kids](/blog/explaining-death-to-kids-hindu) may help.

## One small thing to carry into the week

Something I did late and wish I had done early: record them. Not the whole call. Just the verse.

This week, ask a grandparent to teach one line, and record them saying it slowly, twice. Save it with their name. Your child will learn the line. Years from now, they will have something more: the voice of someone who loved them, taking the time to say a prayer slowly enough for a small child to follow.
