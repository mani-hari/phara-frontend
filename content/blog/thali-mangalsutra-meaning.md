The groom ties the first knot. In many Tamil families, his sister ties the other two. A whole family's promise ends up around one neck, on a thread dyed yellow with turmeric. The plain mangalsutra meaning is "auspicious thread", and that is what it is: a thread, tied at the wedding, that stands for the marriage and for the good wishes of everyone who witnessed it. In Tamil homes it is the thali, and the tying of it is, for many families, the moment everyone throws rice.

## What does mangalsutra mean, word by word?

The word splits cleanly. Mangala means auspicious, full of good fortune. Sutra means thread, the same word used for the short aphorisms of philosophy, each one a single thread of thought. An auspicious thread.

What hangs on the thread changes from region to region. In Tamil wedding custom, the thali's meaning is tied to family and community: the pendant may carry the symbol of the family deity, a Shiva linga, a tulsi plant, a conch or a lotus, and the shape tells an attentive aunt which community the bride has married into. In Kerala it is the minnu, a small leaf-shaped disc. In Maharashtra and much of the North, the mangalsutra is a string of black beads, sometimes with a gold pendant. Different threads, same idea.

At the tying, called mangalya dharanam, the priest recites a verse while the groom holds the thread. Its meaning is simple and tender. It says, more or less: this is the sacred thread, the cause of my long life; I tie it around your neck; may you live a hundred autumns with me. It is a wish for a long life together, spoken aloud in front of everyone. In many weddings [the seven steps of saptapadi](/blog/saptapadi-seven-steps-meaning) follow soon after and complete the shape of the ceremony.

## Why three knots?

In Tamil this is moondru mudichu, the three knots, and everyone has an explanation. Ask three grandmothers and you will hear three. Some say the knots stand for body, speech and mind, which the couple now share. Some say they stand for the three worlds, or for the three gods of creation, preservation and dissolution. Others say they are the couple's three commitments: to each other, to their families, and to dharma.

In many Tamil weddings the groom ties the first knot and his sister, the bride's new nathanar or sister-in-law, ties the second and third. That detail tells its own story. The thread is not just a promise between two people. It binds a woman into a new family and binds that family to her. The story says a man ties a knot. The story is really about a household agreeing to carry someone.

## Renewing the thread

Here is the part people forget. The yellow thread, the manjal kayiru, is not meant to last forever. It frays. It fades. And the tradition built in days to retie it.

On Aadi Perukku, the monsoon festival of rivers in the Tamil month of Aadi, many married women change their thread; [the meaning of Aadi Perukku](/blog/aadi-perukku-meaning) explains why rivers and threads belong together. On Karadaiyan Nombu, when Savitri's story of winning back her husband from Yama is remembered, women tie a fresh yellow cord and cook a special adai; the recipe is in [Karadaiyan Nombu adai for the vratham](/blog/karadaiyan-nombu-adai). And on the day of the [Meenakshi Thirukalyanam festival](/blog/meenakshi-thirukalyanam-festival) in Madurai, when the goddess's own wedding is celebrated, many married women, especially in and around Madurai, retie their thali threads at the same hour as hers.

In many families the yellow thread is later moved to a gold chain, a change that happens a few months after the wedding. The thread becomes metal. But the old days of retying remain, as if to say that even gold needs to be renewed now and then.

## A thread, not a chain

A thread is the weakest material a promise could be made of. It can be snapped with two fingers. That is exactly why it has to be looked after, and why the tradition keeps asking women, and families, to tie it again. The story is really about a promise that needs retying, which is what any honest marriage learns, sometimes the hard way; a column on [when marriage is hard](/blog/when-marriage-is-hard) says more about that.

Many women today choose how and when to wear the thali or mangalsutra, at weddings and festivals, every day, or tucked away in a drawer. The meaning survives all of those choices. It was never really in the gold.
