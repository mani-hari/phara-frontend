My son asked me once, when he was small, whether gods have birthdays. I told him Murugan's falls in the Tamil month of Vaikasi, on the day of the star Visakam, and that we could make him payasam. He was entirely satisfied. So, it turned out, was I. Vaikasi Visakam has been a small, sweet fixture in our New Jersey kitchen ever since, kept on whatever weekday it happens to land.

## What is Vaikasi Visakam?

Vaikasi Visakam is the day in the Tamil month of Vaikasi, usually May or June, when the moon is in the nakshatra, or birth star, called Visakam. It is celebrated as the birth of Murugan, also known as Kartikeya, Skanda and Subramanya, the son of Shiva and Parvati and perhaps the most Tamil of all gods.

The Kanda Puranam, the Tamil retelling of the Skanda Purana, says the gods needed a warrior against the demon Surapadman, and Murugan was born from six sparks of Shiva's third eye, nursed by the six Krittikas in the Saravana pond until Parvati gathered the six babies into one child with six faces. That is why he is called Arumugam, the six-faced one, and Saravanabhava, born in the Saravana pond; the battle he was born for is told in [the Soorasamharam story](/blog/soorasamharam-story-meaning).

My son liked the part about six babies becoming one. I like that it took so many hands to raise him.

## Vaikasi Visakam at home, in a small kitchen

Our Vaikasi Visakam at home is modest. A framed picture of Murugan, and a small brass *vel*, his spear, which my mother sent from Chennai years ago. In the morning, before work, we pour a little milk slowly over the vel, or panchamrutham if my wife has had time to make it: milk, curd, honey, ghee and banana. Red flowers if the grocery store has them, because red is his colour. A lamp.

Then we read or play the Kanda Sashti Kavasam, the armour-hymn to Murugan, or a few lines of Arunagirinathar's Thiruppugazh, songs packed with his names. My son now knows the opening lines better than I do.

The naivedyam is payasam. On a weekday it is the simplest version, rice, milk and sugar with a little cardamom; my wife's method is close to [paal payasam as naivedyam](/blog/paal-payasam-naivedyam), and it fits between breakfast and the school bus. We eat it for dessert that evening.

## Where is the nearest Murugan temple?

This is the part that takes planning in America. Many larger US Hindu temples have a Murugan shrine, and some are dedicated to him entirely. Vaikasi Visakam draws crowds for the abhishekam, the ritual bathing of the deity, so call ahead to ask the time. If it falls on a Tuesday, as it did one year for us, the temple may hold the main celebration on the nearest weekend.

For the day itself, there are other ways to take part. Palani, one of Murugan's [six sacred abodes](/blog/six-abodes-of-murugan), celebrates the day with enormous devotion, and I have written about why [the Palani Murugan temple](/blog/palani-murugan-temple) is the one my family holds closest. Some families ask relatives in Tamil Nadu to visit on their behalf, or book an online sankalpam so a priest offers the worship in their name. Thatha kept a small picture of Murugan on the wall of his cloth shop in Kumbakonam, a few feet from the cash box, where customers could see it. Around the same season come [Chitra Pournami, the record-keeper's full moon](/blog/chitra-pournami-meaning), and Madurai's great [Meenakshi Thirukalyanam](/blog/meenakshi-thirukalyanam-festival), so early summer is full.

## Say his name once

Arunagirinathar's Thiruppugazh is full of calls to Murugan by name. Hundreds of songs, and his names ring through almost every line: Kanda, Kumara, Velava, Muruga. The saint seems to have believed that saying the name was itself a kind of arrival.

So one small thing for this week: say his name once each morning on the way to work. In the car, on the platform, in the elevator. "Muruga." That is all. My son says it when he is nervous before a test. I have started saying it before difficult meetings. It does not change the meeting. It changes who walks into it.
