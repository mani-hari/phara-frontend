A panchangam is simply a description of the day in five parts. That is what the word means: pancha, five, and anga, limb. Once you know the five words, learning how to read a panchangam takes about a minute, and the dense little almanac on an aunt's shelf, or the app on your phone, becomes readable. Before the five limbs, though, there is a story about why the Moon, which drives most of them, keeps changing its shape.

## Why does the Moon wax and wane?

The Puranas answer with a family quarrel. Daksha married his twenty-seven daughters, the nakshatras, to Chandra, the Moon, who was meant to visit each in turn and kept lingering with one of them; the whole story is told under [Rohini, the Moon's favourite](/blog/rohini-nakshatra). Daksha cursed him to waste away, and since a curse could only be softened, never undone, it was bent into a cycle: fifteen days of waning, fifteen of waxing, forever.

The story says a husband was punished for favouritism. The story is really about why the tithi exists. The lunar day is the measure of the Moon's growing and shrinking, and the Moon's visit to each of his twenty-seven wives is the nakshatra. Two of the five limbs are sitting inside one family quarrel, which is a good reason to learn them first.

## How to read a panchangam: the five limbs

Here are the five, in the order most panchangams list them.

1. **Tithi**, the lunar day. There are thirty in a lunar month, fifteen in the waxing fortnight, shukla paksha, and fifteen in the waning one, krishna paksha. Festivals and ancestral rites are mostly fixed by tithi.
2. **Vara**, the weekday, each linked to a planet: Sunday to the Sun, Monday to the Moon, and so on through the week.
3. **Nakshatra**, the star the Moon is passing through. Your birth star is the nakshatra at the moment you were born, and the note on [how to find your nakshatra](/blog/how-to-find-your-nakshatra) explains how to look it up.
4. **Yoga**, a combination worked out from the positions of the Sun and Moon together. There are twenty-seven; most are considered neutral or helpful, a few are avoided.
5. **Karana**, half a tithi. There are eleven, and they matter mostly to priests fixing precise times.

For a family planning a pooja, tithi and nakshatra are usually the ones that matter. Yoga and karana refine the choice, and a priest choosing [an auspicious muhurtham](/blog/what-is-a-muhurtham) weighs all five.

## Reading today's entry

Find the weekday first; it is the one limb that does not move. Then find the tithi and the nakshatra, each listed with an ending time. This is the part that confuses newcomers. Because tithi and nakshatra follow the Moon rather than the clock, they begin and end at odd hours: a tithi might end in the middle of the morning, and the next begins at once. So one calendar day can hold two tithis.

The rest of the entry usually includes sunrise and sunset, and daily windows traditionally avoided for new beginnings, such as [rahu kalam](/blog/rahu-kalam-explained), which depend on local sunrise.

The last lesson is location. A panchangam made for Chennai is calculated for Chennai's time zone and sunrise. In New Jersey or California, the same tithi ends at a different local clock time, sometimes on a different calendar day. Use a panchangam made for your city, or one that converts correctly. The same principle comes up with birth times, as the piece on [a US-born baby's birth star](/blog/birth-star-for-us-born-baby) explains.

## A week of reading the sky

Try reading the day's five limbs each morning for a week. Say them aloud: weekday, tithi, star, yoga, karana. By the fourth day you will notice the tithi climbing towards the full moon or falling towards the new. By the end of the week, the sky stops being background and becomes a clock. For most of human history, that is exactly what it was.
