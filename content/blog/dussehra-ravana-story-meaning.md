On Dussehra, towns across north India build a towering figure of a ten-headed man, pack it with fireworks and set it alight at dusk. The man is Ravana, and the story says he was a great scholar, a gifted musician and a devoted worshipper of Shiva. So what exactly are we burning? The Dussehra significance lies in that question. The festival marks the victory of Rama over Ravana and of Durga over Mahishasura, but the figure in the flames is less an enemy outside than a warning about what learning becomes without humility.

## Why is Dussehra celebrated?

Two stories meet on this one day, the tenth day after the nine nights of Navratri.

The first is from the Ramayana. Ravana, king of Lanka, carries Sita away. Rama, his brother Lakshmana and an army of monkeys and bears led by Hanuman cross the sea to bring her back. After a long war, Rama's arrow finds Ravana, and the tenth day becomes the day of that victory. In some tellings, such as the Bengali Ramayana of Krittivasa, Rama first worships the Goddess before the final battle, which is how the two festivals knot together.

The second is from the Devi Mahatmyam, where the Goddess defeats the buffalo demon Mahishasura after nine nights of battle; you can read that story in [why Navratri lasts nine nights](/blog/why-navratri-lasts-nine-nights). The tenth day is called Vijayadashami, "the tenth day of victory", and in the east and south the Goddess is the centre of it.

Two heroes, two demons, one day. The calendar is not confused. It is saying the same thing twice, in case we missed it the first time.

## Dussehra significance: what the ten heads stand for

Here is the odd part. Ravana is not a brute. The tradition credits him with mastery of the Vedas, of music and of medicine. A popular story says he composed a hymn to Shiva so beautiful that Shiva forgave him for trying to lift Mount Kailasa. He is, by most accounts, the most learned person in the Ramayana.

And yet. He cannot hear the word "no". He wants Sita, so he takes her. His own brother Vibhishana warns him, and he throws his brother out. All that learning, and none of it reached the part of him that wanted.

The ten heads are often read as ten passions: desire, anger, delusion, greed, pride, jealousy, and the mind, intellect, will and ego when they run without restraint. Other families read them as the ten senses turned outward. The number matters less than the image. A man with ten heads has ten voices, all talking, all certain. The story says Rama had to cut the heads again and again because they kept growing back. The story is really about how a clever mind can argue for anything.

So when the effigy burns, we are not celebrating the death of a scholar. We are watching arrogance go up in smoke, and hoping some of ours goes with it.

## Vijayadashami as a beginning

The tenth day is not only an ending. In the south it is one of the most auspicious days to begin something. Children are brought to write their first letters in rice or sand, a ceremony called Vidyarambham, the beginning of learning. Musicians and dancers start new students. In Maharashtra and Karnataka, people exchange leaves of the shami tree as tokens of gold, remembering the Pandavas, who hid their weapons in a shami tree and found them there when they returned, a story told more fully in [the meaning of Ayudha Pooja](/blog/ayudha-pooja-meaning).

The pairing is wise. Having watched what learning without humility looks like, we begin learning again, this time on our knees before Saraswati. If a student in your house is starting something new, [Saraswati and Hayagriva, the deities of learning](/blog/saraswati-and-hayagriva) makes a good companion read. In some homes, the Navratri worship of young girls as the Goddess, explained in [Kanya Puja and its meaning](/blog/kanya-puja-meaning), comes just before, which is its own lesson in humility.

## One head to put down

You do not need to burn an effigy to keep Dussehra. Try something smaller. Name one of your ten heads, the one that talks loudest: the anger that arrives on the drive home, the pride that will not apologise, the want that will not wait. Write it on a piece of paper and, if you can do it safely, burn it in the lamp.

Then begin one small new thing on Vijayadashami: a book, a practice, a letter to someone. The victory the story describes is not a single arrow. It is quiet, and it has to be won again most days.
