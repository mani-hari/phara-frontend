Punarvasu means "the good returns again". The prince born under it spent fourteen years in the forest proving the name. Punarvasu nakshatra is Rama's birth star, and its whole meaning is return: arrows shot and gathered back, light lost and found again. The story says Rama came home. The story is really about what it takes to come back.

## Punarvasu nakshatra: Aditi, Jupiter and a quiver

Jyotish, Indian astrology, sets a god over each of its twenty-seven nakshatras, the lunar mansions, and a planet beside the god, called the adhipati. Punarvasu's god is Aditi, the mother of the gods in the Vedas, whose name means the boundless, the unbound. She is space itself, the open sky that holds everything. Its adhipati is Guru, Jupiter, the planet of wisdom and grace. In Tamil it is Punarpoosam, and it spans Mithuna, the twins, and Kataka, the crab.

Its symbol is a quiver of arrows. An arrow goes out and, if you are a patient archer, you walk over and pick it up and put it back. Nothing is lost for good.

Punarpoosam-born people, the old sketches say, are generous, forgiving and resilient. They bounce back. Plenty of Punarvasu people find recovery hard, and the star does not blame them.

## Why is Rama's birth star about returning?

The Bala Kanda, the first book of the Valmiki Ramayana, describes Rama's birth with the Moon in Punarvasu, on the ninth day of the bright half of Chaitra, the day now kept as Ram Navami. The rest of his life reads like a commentary on the star.

He is sent away on the eve of his coronation. He loses his home, his father, and then his wife. He wanders, grieves, gathers allies, fights a war. And then he returns to Ayodhya, and the city lights lamps to welcome him. In the north, that homecoming is remembered at Deepavali. I have written about [Rama as maryada purushottama](/blog/rama-maryada-purushottama), the one who kept to the limits of dharma even when it cost him.

What strikes me is that the return is not a reset. Rama comes back changed. The forest has made him. The story does not pretend fourteen years can be undone; it says only that a person can come home.

Aditi tells the same story in the sky. In the old texts, she is the mother who bears the gods again after every age, when the worlds are dissolved and begin anew. The boundless keeps giving birth. That is Punarvasu too.

## Aditi's temple at Vaniyambadi

Punarvasu's temple is the Athitheeswarar temple at Vaniyambadi, whose name recalls Aditi herself, who is said to have worshipped Shiva there. Those born under it may ask for an archana, a recitation of names with flowers, there or at their nearest temple.

Because Guru rules the star, Thursday prayers to Jupiter fit naturally; [Thursday worship of Guru](/blog/guru-graha-thursday-worship) explains how. On the monthly janma nakshatra, the day the Moon returns to Punarvasu, many families light a lamp and read a few pages from the Ramayana's return chapters, the Yuddha Kanda's close, where Bharata welcomes his brother home.

Punarvasu's yearly return in your birth month is a good day to begin something again. The sign it opens in is covered in [Mithuna rasi](/blog/mithuna-rasi). The stars on either side are [Ardra, the storm star](/blog/ardra-nakshatra), and [Pushya, the nourishing star](/blog/pushya-nakshatra), and the sequence tells a small story of its own: storm, return, nourishment.

## Your own small return

Every one of us is in some exile. A job lost, a friendship broken, a home left for another country, a practice dropped years ago. None of these ask to be undone. They ask to be revisited.

On your Punarvasu day, take one step back toward something you walked away from. Send the message. Open the book. Sit on the mat again. It does not have to be a triumphant homecoming with lamps along the road. Rama's story reminds us that the arrow can be picked up. That is enough.
