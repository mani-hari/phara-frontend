On a delayed train out of Edison one winter evening, with no signal and a pen in my bag, I wrote Sri Rama Jayam down the margin of a meeting agenda for twenty minutes. I had not planned to. It was something I had watched the elders in our family do when I was a boy, and it came back to my hand before it came back to my mind. Likhita japa, writing a mantra by hand, turned out to be the calmest part of my week. I have kept a small notebook for it ever since.

## What is likhita japa?

Likhita means written. Japa means repetition, usually of a mantra or a divine name. Likhita japa is the practice of writing a name or a mantra many times, slowly, by hand.

Almost any mantra can be written. Om Namah Shivaya, Om Namo Narayanaya, the name of Murugan or Ganesha. In Tamil and Telugu homes, one of the most common is Sri Rama Jayam, victory to Rama. I remember it written in lined school notebooks, page after page, in small careful letters. Sri Rama Jayam writing was something many people of my grandparents' generation did every day, often in the quiet hour after lunch.

There is also the tradition of Rama Koti, which means a crore, ten million, of Rama's name. Families and groups aim to write that many names together, sometimes over years, and then offer the completed notebooks at a temple. Rama Koti writing is less about the number than about the intention of doing something so large, one line at a time.

## How do you begin likhita japa?

It is simpler than it sounds.

Get one notebook and keep it only for this. Not your shopping list, not your work notes. Something about a dedicated notebook makes the practice feel like a practice.

Use one pen, and ideally the same one each day.

Choose one mantra and stay with it. Changing mantras every few days is like changing the seed you are planting every morning.

Write a few lines a day. You do not need to fill pages. Three lines, five lines, a quarter of a page is enough to start.

Write neatly. Neatness matters more than speed. The old people used to say that each written name should be written as if it were the only one.

And say the words silently as you write. The hand and the inner voice move together. That is what makes it japa and not just copying. If you are new to mantras, [simple chanting for beginners](/blog/chanting-for-beginners) may help you choose one.

## Why does writing slow the mind down?

I have thought about this on many train rides.

The hand is slower than thought. When you write a mantra, the mind has to wait for the pen. It cannot race ahead to the next email or the next worry, because the hand is still forming the letters of Rama. That waiting is where the calm comes from.

There is also something honest about it. When you chant aloud, the mind can wander while the mouth keeps going, and you may not notice. When you write, you can see it. The page shows you where your attention slipped: the letters get hurried, a word is misspelled, a line wanders off the margin. Writing a mantra in a notebook gives you a record of your own attention, day by day.

For me, it also connects to the Gita's idea of [the steady mind, the sthitaprajna](/blog/sthitaprajna-steady-mind). I am not steady. But for twenty minutes on a stalled train, I was closer than usual. Some people pair it with [surya namaskar as a morning greeting](/blog/surya-namaskar-meaning), movement first and then the pen. And if you prefer the practice early, [waking in brahma muhurta](/blog/brahma-muhurta-waking-early) gives you an hour when nobody needs you. Some find that [sitting on the floor to pray](/blog/sitting-on-the-floor-to-pray) makes the notebook feel more like an altar.

## One small thing to carry into this week

Write one line of your mantra before you open your laptop each morning. One line. Keep the notebook beside the laptop so you cannot miss it.

By Friday, look back at the week's lines. Notice which days the letters were steady and which days they were hurried. The elders who wrote those school notebooks would have said the page knows your week better than you do. They were usually right.
