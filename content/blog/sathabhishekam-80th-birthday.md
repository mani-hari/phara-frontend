A person a little past eighty has watched about a thousand full moons rise. Somewhere along the way, the tradition decided that anyone who has kept watch that long deserves a ritual of their own. Sathabhishekam is the Hindu ceremony for that milestone, usually held in the eighty-first year: a homam for long life, a bath from many sacred pots, the thali tied once more, and several generations bowing to the one who has seen the most moons.

## What is sathabhishekam, and why a thousand full moons?

The arithmetic is simple and rather lovely. A lunar month, from one full moon to the next, lasts about twenty-nine and a half days. A thousand of them add up to a little over eighty years. So somewhere in the eighty-first year, a person crosses the line of a thousand full moons.

The ritual has another name that says this directly: Sahasra Chandra Darshana Shanti, the peace ritual for one who has seen a thousand moons. Sahasra is a thousand, chandra the moon, darshana seeing. The moon in Hindu thought is the keeper of the mind and of time's passing, and [the moon, Chandra, in worship](/blog/chandra-graha-worship) is honoured for both. To have seen a thousand of its returns is to have lived through a very great deal of time.

Satha, in the more common name, is a hundred, and abhishekam the ritual bath. Explanations of why a hundred differ. One reading is that the ritual looks forward, praying that the person reach a hundred.

## The ritual

The shape will be familiar to anyone who has attended a sixtieth birthday rite, the [shashtiabdapoorthi](/blog/shashtiabdapoorthi-60th-birthday), only grander and slower.

It begins with a Ganapati puja and the sankalpam, the statement of intention naming the elder, the family lineage and the birth star. Then kalasa pujas, where pots of water are consecrated, each invoking a deity; for a sathabhishekam there may be dozens of them. Homams follow: the Ayushya homam for long and healthy life, described in [the Ayushya homam and the star birthday](/blog/ayushya-homam-star-birthday), and the Mrityunjaya homam, offered to Shiva as the one who conquers death.

Then the couple, if both are living, sit side by side, and the water from the many kalasams is poured over them. The husband ties the thali on his wife again, sixty years or so after he first tied it. Grandchildren and, increasingly, great-grandchildren come forward to touch their feet and receive their blessings. Four generations in one hall, sometimes five.

## How can you join a sathabhishekam from abroad?

Timing comes first. The ritual is fixed by the elder's birth month and nakshatra, the birth star, so ask the family priest to find the day well in advance.

Then the harder decision. Long flights are hard on elders past eighty, so most families bring themselves to the elder rather than the other way round. That may mean children and grandchildren flying from three continents to one town in Tamil Nadu. If the whole family cannot travel, sponsoring the homam at a temple and taking part live is a sincere alternative.

And record everything that matters. Not the whole ceremony, but the elders' blessings, spoken to each grandchild by name. Nobody can promise that the voices you hear at eighty will still be there at ninety. The advice in [recording your elders' chants](/blog/recording-elders-chants) applies just as well to blessings. For those who carry this from a distance, [caring for ageing parents from abroad](/blog/caring-for-parents-from-abroad) may help.

## The story of witness

The story says the ritual is for long life, for the planets, for peace. The story is really about witness. A person at eighty has seen wars end, cities grow, children born and parents die. They have seen the family change shape more than once. The ritual gathers everyone to say: we see what you have seen.

So ask the person at eighty to tell the family one story from each decade they have lived. Eight stories, maybe nine. Write them down, or record them, and give a copy to every grandchild. A thousand moons should leave more than a photograph.
