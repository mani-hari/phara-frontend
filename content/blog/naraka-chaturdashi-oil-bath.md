The smell of warm sesame oil in the dark. My grandmother's thumb pressing oil into my scalp, too hard, while a cracker went off somewhere down the street. That is Deepavali morning in a Tamil house, and it belongs to Naraka Chaturdashi, the fourteenth day of the waning moon, when the south celebrates Krishna's victory over the demon Narakasura. The day is kept with a pre-dawn oil bath, new clothes, sweets and a greeting that sounds like a question: "Ganga snanam aachaa?" Have you had your Ganga bath?

## What is Naraka Chaturdashi?

The story is in the Bhagavata Purana. Narakasura, a powerful king, had grown arrogant. He imprisoned thousands of women, took the earrings of Aditi, the mother of the gods, and terrorised heaven and earth alike. Krishna rode out against him with his wife Satyabhama beside him on the chariot, and in the telling most Tamil families know, it was Satyabhama who struck the final blow.

What I remember most, though, is the ending my grandmother told. As Narakasura lay dying, he asked for one thing: that people remember the day not with mourning but with light, with oil baths and new clothes and lamps. So we do. It is a strange mercy at the heart of a festival, a demon's last wish granted by the people he once frightened. The north has other stories for this season, gathered in [why Diwali is celebrated](/blog/why-is-diwali-celebrated), but in our house this was the one.

## Why the pre-dawn oil bath?

The bath has to happen before sunrise, in the dark, which is why children are dragged out of bed at four in the morning. Oil is warmed, sometimes with a pepper or a betel leaf dropped in, and rubbed into the hair and body. After a short wait comes the bath with shikakai, a soap-nut powder that takes the oil out, and then the new clothes laid out the night before.

The belief behind the greeting is that on this one morning, Ganga, the sacred river, is present in all water everywhere. The bucket in a Chennai bathroom and the shower in a New Jersey apartment are, for that hour, the same river. "Ganga snanam aachaa?" is how you ask someone whether they have been to the Ganga, without either of you leaving home.

Andal's Tiruppavai opens with an invitation to the girls of the village to rise in the cold dawn of Margazhi: "neeraadap poduveer, poduminoe", come, all who wish to bathe. It is about a different month and a different vow. But I think of that line every Deepavali morning, standing under the water while the house is still dark. There is something about bathing before the sun that makes a day feel chosen.

After the bath comes the Deepavali marundhu, a dark, sticky paste of ginger, jaggery and spices, to settle the stomach before the sweets arrive. Then crackers, then breakfast, then visits.

## Keeping the oil bath when you live far away

Deepavali in the United States usually falls on a working day, and the tithi, the lunar day, does not always line up with India's. Check the current year's panchangam for your city; [when Diwali falls in the USA](/blog/when-is-diwali-in-the-usa) explains why the day can shift. The morning itself adapts easily.

What we do now is quieter. I set an alarm forty minutes earlier than usual. I warm a little sesame oil in a steel cup, work it into my son's hair while he complains exactly as I used to, and we take turns in the shower. My mother calls on video, already in the evening of her Deepavali, and asks the question. We answer yes. There is no cracker, but there is usually a neighbour's doorbell somewhere, and that does.

If you have time the evening before, a small kolam, the rice-flour pattern drawn at the threshold, helps the morning feel different; [the meaning of kolam and rangoli](/blog/kolam-and-rangoli-meaning) is a good place to start. The rest of the festival carries on through [the five days of Diwali](/blog/five-days-of-diwali), with lamps on the new-moon night still to come.

## One small thing to carry into the week

On Naraka Chaturdashi morning, wake a little earlier than you need to. Warm some oil, put it in your hair, bathe slowly and put on something you have not worn before. Then greet one person, in whatever language you like, with "Ganga snanam aachaa?" Notice who smiles.
