Do Hindus believe in one God? Most Hindus would answer yes, and then, in the same breath, tell you about Ganesha, Murugan, Lakshmi and the goddess of the village temple. That is not confusion. It is one ultimate reality known through many forms, the way one woman can be a daughter, a mother and a doctor at once, and nobody thinks she is three people. The question sounds simple from outside. From inside the tradition, it sounds a little like asking whether the ocean is one thing or many waves.

## Do Hindus believe in one god, many, or both?

The Upanishads, the philosophical texts at the end of the Vedas, have a word for the ultimate reality: Brahman. Not to be confused with Brahma, the creator god, Brahman is the ground of everything, beyond name, form and description. Brahman in Hinduism is not a god among gods. It is what all the gods, and everything else, are made of. The Upanishads' own words for this are [atman and Brahman](/blog/atman-and-brahman).

The devas and devis, the gods and goddesses, are then understood as forms, faces and powers of that one reality. Agni is its fire. Saraswati is its learning. Ganesha is its patience with beginnings. You approach the one through the many, because the one is too vast to approach directly.

Some scholars call this henotheism: worshipping one god as supreme at a time, without denying that others exist. Henotheism in Hinduism is a useful label, but it misses something. Most Hindus would not say they are choosing one god among rivals. They would say they are addressing the same reality by a name they love. The piece on [ishta devata, the chosen deity](/blog/ishta-devata-meaning) explores this.

## What about the 33 crore gods?

You will often hear that Hinduism has thirty-three crore, or three hundred and thirty million, gods. It is a famous number and a misleading one.

The Vedas speak of thirty-three devas: eight Vasus, eleven Rudras, twelve Adityas, and two more, often Indra and Prajapati. The Sanskrit word used for them is koti. Koti can mean type or category, and it can also mean ten million. Read one way, there are thirty-three kinds of gods. Read the other way, there are thirty-three crore. The 33 crore gods meaning, then, is more a reading than a census.

There is a wonderful passage in the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad that settles the matter in its own way. A student named Vidagdha asks the sage Yajnavalkya how many gods there are. Yajnavalkya answers: three thousand three hundred and six. Vidagdha asks again. Thirty-three, says the sage. Again. Six. Again. Three. Again. Two. Again. One and a half. And again. One. The story says the number keeps shrinking. The story is really about the question: how many depends on how closely you look.

## How do Shaiva, Vaishnava and Shakta Hindus fit in?

The great traditions within Hinduism each name one deity as supreme. For Shaivas, it is Shiva. For Vaishnavas, it is Vishnu, or Krishna. For Shaktas, it is the Goddess, Devi. Each sees the other deities as aspects, attendants or forms of the one they worship. A Vaishnava temple may have a small shrine to Shiva; a Shaiva temple will certainly have Ganesha.

In the tenth chapter of the Bhagavad Gita, Krishna describes this directly. Among the Adityas, he says, I am Vishnu. Among the Rudras, I am Shankara. Among lights, the radiant sun. Among rivers, the Ganga. Among letters, the first letter. It is a long, dizzying list, and its point is simple: wherever there is glory, power or beauty, that is a spark of the one. The many are not rivals. They are the one's glories.

This does not mean every Hindu agrees on everything. They do not, and the arguments have gone on for centuries. But the arguments are mostly about which name to use for the centre, not whether there is one. Even prayer itself, as the piece on [whether prayer works](/blog/does-prayer-work-hindu-view) explores, is addressed to whichever face of the divine the heart turns to.

## A reply for a friend

If a friend asks you this question, try the light-and-lamps answer. There is one fire. From it you can light a hundred lamps: a brass one in the kitchen, a clay one on the doorstep, a silver one in the prayer room. Each lamp has its own shape and its own place, and you might love one more than the others. But nobody in the room is confused about which fire is burning. If they want to know what to do with all those forms, the piece on [why Hindus do rituals](/blog/why-do-hindus-do-rituals) is a good next step.
