Hinduism names four aims of a good life, the four purusharthas, and two of them are wealth and pleasure. I did not learn that in a scripture class. I learned it from my Thatha, who ran a cloth shop in Kumbakonam and once told a nephew who was embarrassed about wanting a raise: "A cloth merchant who does not want profit should become a monk. And a monk who wants profit should become a cloth merchant." He was laughing, but he meant it. The tradition does not ask you to feel guilty for wanting a decent life. It asks you to want it in the right order.

## What are the four purusharthas?

Purushartha means an aim of a person, something worth pursuing. There are four. Dharma is right conduct, the duties and decencies that hold a life and a society together. Artha is wealth and security: a salary, a roof, savings for a child's education. Kama is pleasure, love and beauty, from music to a good meal to the affection between two people. Moksha is liberation, the release from the cycle of wanting and becoming that the tradition says is the final goal.

Tamil readers will recognise the first three at once. The Tirukkural is divided into three books: Aram, which is virtue, Porul, which is wealth and statecraft, and Inbam, which is love. Thiruvalluvar never wrote a separate book on liberation, and many readers take that as his quiet point: live the first three well and the fourth takes care of itself. I followed that thread in [what the Tirukkural says about wealth](/blog/tirukkural-on-wealth).

## Why wealth and pleasure are allowed

Growing up, I absorbed a vague idea that the truly spiritual person wants nothing. Money was slightly dirty and enjoyment was slightly suspect. The four purusharthas say otherwise. Dharma artha kama moksha is a list of aims, not a list of three temptations and one reward.

What the tradition insists on is the frame. Artha pursued inside dharma is honest work and fair dealing. Artha pursued outside it is theft with better paperwork. Kama inside dharma is a marriage, a friendship, a holiday with your family. Outside it, pleasure starts eating the people around you. The problem was never the salary or the good meal. The problem is when they stop answering to anything.

I think of this every appraisal season. Wanting the promotion is not a failing. Wanting it badly enough to take credit for a colleague's work is. [Dharma in everyday life](/blog/dharma-meaning-in-everyday-life) is mostly made of choices that small.

## How do the four purusharthas fit a working week?

The picture that helps me is a house. Dharma is the frame and the walls. Artha and kama are what you put inside: furniture, food, the people you love, the things that make you laugh. Moksha is the window, the direction the house faces, the reminder that there is sky beyond the rooms.

On the Monday train out of Edison, I am mostly in artha: emails, a budget meeting, a deadline. That is fine. At dinner, when my son tells me at length about a video game, I am in kama, and that is fine too. My wife and I argue about whether to spend on a trip to Chennai or save it, and that argument is dharma in action, weighing duty to parents against duty to our own household. And for two minutes each night, standing at the small lamp in our kitchen, I am looking out of the window. That is as close to moksha as a Tuesday gets, and the tradition says that is enough to keep the direction right.

The fourth aim gets its own treatment in [what moksha actually means](/blog/what-is-moksha). And if you are curious why some days feel heavy and others restless, [the three gunas explained](/blog/three-gunas-explained) is a good companion to this one.

## One small thing for the week

At the end of each day this week, before sleep, ask yourself two questions. Which of the four did I serve today? And did dharma hold the others in place?

Some days the answer will be "only artha, and barely". That is an honest answer. My Thatha would have said that a shop open six days a week has earned its Sunday. Notice the pattern over seven days, not the score on any one of them. A full life, the tradition says, is not one aim pursued perfectly. It is four aims kept in their proper order, the way a good merchant keeps his accounts.
