Once a year, in the Tamil month of Margazhi, Vishnu temples across the south open a gate on the northern side that stays shut the rest of the year, and devotees queue through the night to walk through it. That gate is the heart of the Vaikunta Ekadashi significance. It stands for the door of Vaikuntha, Vishnu's own realm, opened for one day, and it is paired with a fast and a night of waking. The story behind it, as with most good stories about doors, is really about wanting to be let in.

## Vaikunta Ekadashi significance

An Ekadashi is the eleventh day of each lunar fortnight, and there are about twenty-four in a year; each carries its own name and story. Vaikunta Ekadashi is the Ekadashi of the waxing moon in Margazhi, the month known in Sanskrit as Dhanu, usually in December or January. Many Vaishnavas, followers of Vishnu, consider it the most important of the year. The Padma Purana describes Ekadashi itself as a goddess born from Vishnu's body to defeat a demon called Mura, and says that Vishnu, pleased, made her day one of fasting and remembrance.

Margazhi is already a month of early mornings and devotion in the south, the month of Andal's Tiruppavai sung before dawn; [the meaning of the Margazhi month](/blog/margazhi-month-meaning) describes its quiet discipline. Vaikunta Ekadashi arrives in the middle of it like a high point.

## Why does the Vaikunta Dwaram open?

The best-known temple story involves two demons, Madhu and Kaitabha. They were born, the stories say, from the ear-wax of Vishnu himself while he slept on the cosmic ocean, and they tried to steal the Vedas from Brahma. Vishnu woke and fought them. In the version told in south Indian temples, the demons, defeated, did something surprising. They asked for a favour. They asked Vishnu to open the gate of his heaven to them, and to open it every year on this day so that anyone who remembered this story and walked through the northern door would be received there too.

Vishnu agreed. So at Srirangam, the great island temple on the Kaveri, the northern door called the Paramapada Vasal, the gate of the highest abode, is opened before dawn on Vaikunta Ekadashi. At Tirumala, the passage around the sanctum known as the Vaikunta Dwaram is opened for devotees. The crowds are enormous. People wait for hours in the cold simply to pass through a doorway.

Notice who asked for the gate. Not a sage, not a god. Two demons, beaten, with nothing left to bargain with. The door opened because they asked. What the story is doing is reminding us that the ones who most need a door are rarely the ones who have earned it, and that asking is enough.

## Fasting, waking and the idea of the gate

The day is kept with a fast, often complete, sometimes without even water for those who are able, and a night of jagaram, staying awake in prayer and song. Many people break the fast the next morning after the sunrise prayer, at a set time called parana. The practical side of all this, from what to eat to how to break a fast gently, is laid out in [the Ekadashi fasting rules](/blog/ekadashi-fasting-rules).

Why fast before a gate? Perhaps because hunger thins the walls a little. When the body is not busy digesting, the mind has fewer places to hide. Many people describe a clarity on fasting days that they do not find otherwise; [fasting and focus](/blog/fasting-and-focus) looks at that honestly, including the days when it does not come.

The gate at Srirangam is stone and wood. But the tradition has always suggested that there is another door, inside, that opens the same way: not by force, only by asking and waiting. Staying awake all night is less about proving devotion and more about being there when it opens. For anyone who spends nights awake for less peaceful reasons, [small rituals for anxious days](/blog/rituals-for-anxious-days) offers a gentler place to begin.

## If you cannot reach a temple

Most of us will not stand in the queue at Srirangam or Tirumala. The gate still counts. On Vaikunta Ekadashi, keep a simple fast or eat one light meal. In the evening, read or listen to the Vishnu Sahasranamam, the thousand names of Vishnu from the Anushasana Parva of the Mahabharata. It takes about half an hour.

Then, before you sleep, walk slowly through your own front door, from outside to inside, and pause on the threshold. Ask for whatever you need to be let into. Like the demons in the story, you do not need to have earned it.
