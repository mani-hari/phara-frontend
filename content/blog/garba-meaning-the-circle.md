At the centre of every garba circle there is, or once was, a lamp burning inside a clay pot. That is the heart of the garba meaning: the pot is the womb of the Goddess, the flame is the life she holds, and the dancers who circle it are acting out the oldest idea in the tradition, that life goes around. The story says the Goddess is being welcomed for nine nights. The story is really about the thing at the centre that does not move while everything else does.

## Garba meaning: the pot, the lamp and the womb

The word garba comes from garbha, the Sanskrit for womb. The clay pot at the centre of the circle is called the garbo or garbha deep, the lamp of the womb. It is pierced all over with small holes, and light spills out through them across the floor.

Look at it as a symbol and it becomes quite plain. The pot is a body. The flame inside is the spark that makes a body alive, which the tradition sees as the Goddess herself, living in every one of us. The holes are the senses, the doors through which that inner light leaks out into the world. In Gujarati villages, women carried the garbo from house to house during Navratri, the nine nights of the Goddess, and the dance followed the lamp wherever it went.

So when a hall in New Jersey or Texas fills with a few hundred people spinning in bright chaniya choli and kurtas, they are, whether they know it or not, dancing around a pregnant pot of light. It is a celebration of fertility in the widest sense: fields, families, anything that grows.

## Why does the dance move in a circle?

Processions move forward; pilgrims climb. Garba goes nowhere. It turns.

That is the point. Hindu thought pictures time not as a straight line from creation to the end of the world but as a wheel: birth, growth, decay, death, and birth again, the same seasons coming round. The Devi Mahatmyam, the text most read during Navratri, describes the Goddess as the one who creates, sustains and dissolves the universe, over and over. The circle of dancers is that wheel made visible. Ask why garba is danced in a circle and the dance answers for itself: because that is how everything moves.

But a wheel needs an axle. The dancers keep changing, the young ones fast and the grandmothers slow. Only the lamp at the centre stays exactly where it is. The dance is about the relationship between the two: all that motion is possible because one thing is still.

## What is the difference between garba and dandiya?

People new to Navratri in America often use the two words interchangeably. The garba and dandiya difference is simple once you see it.

Garba is danced with the hands: clapping, turning, sweeping gestures, feet following a pattern around the centre. Dandiya raas is danced with a pair of decorated sticks, usually in two facing lines or partners, the sticks struck together on the beat. Many nights begin with garba and move to dandiya as the evening warms up.

The sticks carry their own story. They are often said to be the swords of Durga in her nine-night battle with Mahishasura, the buffalo demon, a story told in full in the Devi Mahatmyam and retold in [the meaning behind Dussehra and Ravana](/blog/dussehra-ravana-story-meaning). So garba is the womb and dandiya is the war. One celebrates the Goddess who gives life; the other celebrates the Goddess who protects it. Navratri holds both, which is why it needs nine nights.

## Garba far from Gujarat

Most families abroad meet garba in a rented community hall or a school gym rather than a village square, and the clay pot is sometimes replaced by a framed picture of Amba Ma on a table. That is fine. Traditions travel by adapting. ## Look at the centre

The next time you join a circle, glance at the middle of it before the music starts. Find the lamp, or the picture, or whatever stands in for it. Then dance as fast as you like. The women who carried the garbo from door to door knew what the dance was for: to go around a small, steady light and not lose sight of it. It is a good thing to practise, even on a weeknight in a school gym. For more on why that flame matters so much, read [why Hindus light lamps](/blog/why-do-hindus-light-lamps).
