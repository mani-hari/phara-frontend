On our street in New Jersey, the clay lamps from Deepavali never quite go back in the box. By December they sit on the windowsill beside the neighbours' strings of lights, and my son says our street has one long festival of light. For families celebrating Diwali and Christmas in one home, whether because of [an interfaith marriage](/blog/interfaith-marriage-hindu) or simply because they live in America, that is a good place to start: light is the common thread, and each festival can stay entirely itself.

## Celebrating Diwali and Christmas: can one home hold both?

The calendar makes it easier than it sounds. Deepavali falls on the new-moon night of the lunar month of Kartika, which usually lands in late October or November. A few weeks later comes Karthigai Deepam, on the full moon when the moon is in the Krittika star, when Tamil homes fill every doorstep with small lamps. Then, in the darkest weeks of the year, Christmas.

So the season runs from one set of lamps to another. In our house the brass lamps come out for Deepavali, stay out for Karthigai, and by the time the neighbours' reindeer go up, we are still lighting a lamp on the sill each evening. My son, when he was small, assumed the whole world was celebrating the same thing. In a way, he was not wrong.

If your family is newer to this, the practical guide to [celebrating Diwali in the USA](/blog/celebrate-diwali-in-the-usa) covers the weekday problem, the school calendar and the smoke alarm. And there is more on the second wave of lamps in [Karthigai Deepam at home](/blog/karthigai-deepam-at-home).

## The school concert and the tree question

Every December brings the school holiday concert, and with it, questions from classmates. "Do you have a tree?" "Does Santa come to your house?" My son used to come home with these like small stones in his pocket, not sure whether to be embarrassed.

Some Hindu families keep a small tree. Some do not. Some put a lamp beside it, or a small Ganesha under it. I do not think there is one right answer, and I have stopped pretending there is. A friend in Ohio, married to a Lutheran, keeps a tree in the living room and a puja shelf in the kitchen. She tells her children that the tree is how their father's family says welcome to winter, and the lamp is how her family says thank you for light. Her children seem entirely at peace with both.

Can Hindus celebrate Christmas? Many do, as a cultural festival of family, generosity and a very good dinner. Hinduism has always had room for this, as [what Hinduism says about other religions](/blog/hindu-view-of-other-religions) explains.

## Keeping each festival itself

The risk is not celebrating both. The risk is blending them into one vague winter holiday where neither means much.

So keep each festival itself. For Deepavali, the pre-dawn oil bath, the new clothes, the crackers if you can manage them, the Lakshmi puja in the evening. The oil bath is my favourite part of the whole festival; I have written about [the Naraka Chaturdashi oil bath](/blog/naraka-chaturdashi-oil-bath) and my grandmother's hands in my hair. For Christmas, hospitality: gifts, a meal with friends, neighbours dropping by, cards on the mantel.

Let each festival have its own food, its own stories, its own songs. Deepavali has the story of Krishna and Narakasura; Christmas has its own. Tell each one properly, in its season. A child can hold two stories. Children are good at that. It is adults who worry that one will crowd out the other.

## One small thing to carry into the week

One December evening, light a lamp on the windowsill, next to whatever else is there. When your child asks which festival it belongs to, tell them the truth: it can belong to Karthigai, to the season of light, or simply to your house on a cold night. Both answers are fine.
