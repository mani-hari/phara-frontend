Aarti is the moment at the end of a pooja when you stop asking for anything and simply show the light. If you are wondering how to do aarti at home, the answer is short: hold a plate with a small flame, circle it clockwise before the deity, and give it your full attention for one song or one breath. Everything else, the bell, the song, the number of circles, is detail that families fill in their own way.

## How to do aarti at home, step by step

Keep it simple the first few times.

1. Prepare a plate. A small ghee lamp with a cotton wick, or a piece of camphor on a little metal holder, set on a flat plate. Add a flower and a pinch of kumkum if you like.
2. Light the flame after the rest of your pooja is done, after the flowers and the food offering.
3. If you use a bell, hold it in your left hand and ring it steadily; [why we ring a temple bell](/blog/why-ring-a-temple-bell) explains what the sound does.
4. Hold the plate in your right hand and circle the flame clockwise in front of the deity.
5. One common pattern is a few circles at the feet, then at the middle, then at the face, then a large circle around the whole form. Families and regions vary; some do seven circles in total, some simply three.
6. Set the plate down, or bring it to others.

Camphor is the usual choice in South Indian homes; the camphor burns bright and fast and leaves nothing behind. A ghee lamp burns longer and more softly. Either is fine. If you have set a kalasha for the pooja, the aarti is the natural end of it; the piece on [what the kalasam means](/blog/kalasam-meaning) describes how the two belong together.

## Why circle a flame?

The word aarti is often traced to the Sanskrit aratrika, the waving of light, especially the light waved at dusk or at the end of worship. Tradition sees it as offering the best of what the eyes can see back to the divine. You have been looking at the deity; now you show the deity to itself, lit up.

Camphor has its own meaning. It burns away completely, leaving no ash and no residue, and tradition reads that as the ego leaving nothing behind. [Why camphor is used in aarti](/blog/camphor-aarti-meaning) follows that idea to the last flicker. Incense usually comes earlier in the sequence, and [why incense belongs in puja](/blog/incense-and-dhoop-meaning) covers that step.

As for what to sing, the choice is wide. Many North Indian families sing Om Jai Jagadish Hare. Tamil homes might sing a short deeparadhanai verse, sung as the lamp is shown to the deity, or a line from a favourite devotional song. Some people sing nothing at all and simply watch the flame. The direction and the number of circles matter less than the attention you bring to them. If your eyes are on the flame and your mind is with them, you are doing it right.

## How do you take aarti?

When the circles are done, the plate is brought to each person present. Pass your palms, cupped, just over the flame, close enough to feel the warmth, and then bring them gently to your eyes and over the top of your head. Children usually love this part. Some families also touch the plate to the forehead or drop a coin on it at the temple.

A few practical notes for apartments. Open a window, because camphor smokes. Know where your smoke alarm is, and do not hold the plate right under it. Keep the flame away from curtains and hair. Never leave camphor burning unattended; let it finish, or cover it with a small metal lid. Clean the plate after, since soot builds up quickly on brass; the guide to [cleaning brass puja items](/blog/cleaning-brass-puja-items) makes this easier.

The same aarti works outdoors, for a new car on the driveway during a [vahana pooja](/blog/vahana-pooja-new-car), or for a new house.

## Thirty seconds at the end of the evening

For one week, end your evening lamp with a thirty-second aarti. Just the plate, the flame and three slow circles. Do not count seconds. Watch the flame instead of the clock. At the end of the week, notice whether the evening feels more finished than before.
