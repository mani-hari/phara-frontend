Hindu practice at college fits in a shoebox. A small picture of your deity, a square of cloth, a jar of vibhuti, maybe an LED lamp, and two minutes before the first class. That is enough to begin, and for many students it turns out to be enough, full stop. I have been thinking about this box for a while. My son is about eleven, and the question of what he takes to college is only a few years away.

## Can you do puja in a dorm room?

Yes, with adjustments. Most dorms ban open flames, candles and incense, and the smoke detectors are sensitive; setting one off at seven in the morning is a fast way to meet your whole floor. So the lamp becomes an LED diya, the kind Indian stores sell for exactly this. A flower from the dining hall, or one picked on the walk back, goes in front of the picture. A Saraswati picture on a shelf above the desk, facing east if the room allows, is the whole shrine.

The prayer is the flame. What makes it a puja is not the fire but the attention: standing for a moment, saying a verse, meaning it. The ideas for [a small puja corner in an apartment](/blog/small-puja-corner-apartment) scale down neatly to a dorm shelf.

## Practice between classes

A good deal of practice at college happens on foot. A mantra said silently on the walk to lectures, the same one every day: Om Namah Shivaya, a Ganesha verse, or the Gayatri, if the student has had [the sacred thread ceremony](/blog/upanayanam-in-the-usa) or the family simply says it. Before exams, the Saraswati Vandana, the prayer to the goddess of learning. [Prayers before exams](/blog/prayer-before-exams) has a few short ones, and students who want to know who they are praying to can meet [Saraswati and Hayagriva](/blog/saraswati-and-hayagriva).

Fasting days need a sentence of explanation to a roommate. "It is Ekadashi, I eat light today" is usually met with curiosity rather than confusion. A roommate who knows why you are skipping pizza will often save you some fruit.

For festivals, many campuses have a Hindu Students Association, and many have an interfaith chaplain whose office can help book a room for a Diwali puja or a Holi afternoon. Being alone on a festival is harder than it sounds, and most of what I suggested for [celebrating festivals alone abroad](/blog/festivals-alone-abroad) works just as well in a dorm.

## What can parents pack?

A small shrine box, the kind with a lid, so the whole puja can be put away when friends come in. Inside: a small picture or idol, a cloth, a little vibhuti or kumkum, and a strip of cotton wicks even if they cannot be used, because they smell like home. A list of the year's festival days, since a student will not know that Navratri moved. For the curious ones, send along [how to read a panchangam](/blog/how-to-read-a-panchangam). And the address and phone number of the nearest temple, written on paper, not only saved in the phone.

Then comes the harder part: let them shape it. The practice a student keeps at nineteen will not look like yours at forty-five. That is fine. Tiruvalluvar says, in Kural 400, that learning is the one wealth that does not decay, and that nothing else is really wealth at all. Faith that a young person has chosen for themselves is a little like that. It lasts because it is theirs.

## One small thing to carry

Say one line of prayer at the same moment each day. Not a long one. Just before opening the laptop to study, or before the first coffee of the morning. The same line, the same moment. Four years of that is a practice, and it goes with you long after the shrine box has been lost in a move.
