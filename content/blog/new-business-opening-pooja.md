The Tirupati story says that even Venkateswara, Vishnu on his hill, borrowed money from Kubera to pay for his wedding, and is still repaying it with interest; [the story of Tirupati Balaji](/blog/tirupati-balaji-temple-story) tells how, and why pilgrims fill the hundi, the temple's offering box, to help. It is also why a new business opening pooja honours Kubera, alongside Ganesha and Lakshmi, before the shop counts a single dollar or rupee. The gods themselves, in this telling, keep accounts.

## Why Ganesha, Lakshmi and Kubera?

The three appear together at so many shop openings that the pairing deserves an explanation.

**Ganesha** is the lord of beginnings and thresholds. He is worshipped first in every rite, and a shop, quite literally, is a threshold that strangers will cross every day.

**Lakshmi** is the flow of prosperity. The stories say she is restless and does not stay where there is disorder, dishonesty or neglect. She comes to the clean house and leaves the quarrelsome one; the reasons are gathered in [why Lakshmi is called the restless goddess](/blog/lakshmi-restless-goddess).

**Kubera** is the keeper of wealth, the treasurer of the gods, who lends to Venkateswara and guards the treasures of the north. He does not create wealth. He looks after it, and [what the Kubera story means](/blog/kubera-story-meaning) turns mostly on that difference.

The story says three gods bless a business. The story is really about three virtues a business needs. A clean start, which is Ganesha. Good conduct, which is what Lakshmi rewards. Careful accounts, which is Kubera's whole character. Put that way, the pooja is a short lesson in [Hinduism and business ethics](/blog/hinduism-business-ethics), given before the doors open.

## What does a new business opening pooja include?

The shop opening puja varies by region and family, but these elements recur.

1. **A muhurtham.** An auspicious time, usually chosen by an astrologer from the panchangam, so that the first customer walks in at a moment the family considers well begun.
2. **The kalasam.** A pot of water with mango leaves and a coconut, set up to hold the deities' presence.
3. **Ganesha first.** Either a full Ganapathy homam, a fire ritual, or a simple Ganesha puja with flowers and modakam. If you choose the homam, it helps to know [what happens in a Ganapathi homam](/blog/ganapathi-homam-meaning) before the priest arrives.
4. **Lakshmi and Kubera.** Invoked with their own verses, often with coins, a new cash box or a small silver image before them.
5. **A coconut broken at the threshold.** The coconut is smashed on the step or just outside the door, marking the entrance as blessed.
6. **The first ledger.** The account book, register or, now, the laptop is blessed with turmeric and kumkum. Some owners write the name of a god on its first page before any number goes in.

## Opening abroad

For many owners in the US, the "shop" is a storefront in a strip mall, a home office in a spare bedroom, or a rented desk in a co-working space for a startup. The pooja can shrink to fit.

- **A small shrine.** A picture or small image of Ganesha and Lakshmi on a shelf near the entrance or on the desk. The same idea scales down to [a small altar at your desk](/blog/small-altar-at-your-desk).
- **No fire where the rules forbid it.** Leases and fire codes often do not allow an open flame, let alone a homam. An electric lamp is not a failure. Some owners have the homam performed at a temple in their name and keep only a lamp at the shop.
- **A lamp on the first morning.** Before the first customer, before the first email. One lamp, the three names, and a moment of silence.

## The promise made out loud

A new business opening pooja is less a transaction with the gods than a promise the owner makes out loud, before witnesses, about how the business will be run. Ganesha is asked to clear the way, which means the owner commits to beginning honestly. Lakshmi is invited, which means the owner commits to keeping the place in order. Kubera is honoured, which means the books will be kept straight.

Venkateswara, the story says, is still repaying his loan. The detail is usually told as a joke. It is also a very serious idea: that even a god, having borrowed, keeps paying. A business that remembers that is well begun.
