Look at a nei appam pan with its rows of round hollows, each filled with bubbling ghee. It looks like a tray of lamps, and in Tamil homes on Karthigai Deepam, that is almost what it is. A nei appam recipe is simple: rice, jaggery, banana and ghee, fried into small brown domes that are crisp outside and soft within. What is less obvious is why this particular sweet belongs to Ganesha, and why it shows up on the night of lamps.

## Nei appam recipe: rice, jaggery, banana, ghee

Nei means ghee in Tamil. Kerala calls the same sweet unniyappam, little appam, and often fries it in ghee or coconut oil.

**Ingredients (makes about 20)**

- 200 g raw rice (1 cup), soaked for three hours
- 150 g grated jaggery (¾ cup), melted in 60 ml water (¼ cup) and strained
- 1 small ripe banana
- ½ teaspoon cardamom powder
- A pinch of dry ginger powder and a pinch of salt
- 3 tablespoons small coconut bits, fried golden in a spoon of ghee
- Ghee for frying

**Method**

1. Drain the rice and grind it with the warm jaggery syrup and the banana to a thick, slightly coarse batter, like a heavy cake batter.
2. Stir in cardamom, dry ginger, salt and the fried coconut bits.
3. Rest the batter for an hour, or longer if you can.
4. Heat the appam pan (the same pan sold as an appe or paniyaram pan). Add half a teaspoon of ghee to each hollow.
5. Spoon batter into each hollow until about three-quarters full.
6. When the edges are brown and the top looks set, turn each appam with a skewer and cook the other side.
7. Lift out and drain.

## Why does Ganesha love appam?

Ganesha is almost never pictured empty-handed. One hand holds a goad, one a noose, one is raised in blessing, and one holds something sweet: a modakam, or in many southern images, an appam. Kerala temples offer unniyappam to Ganapathy in great quantities, and devotees take it home as prasadam, food returned as blessing.

The story says the child god simply loves sweets, and grandmothers tell it that way, with a smile. The story is really about what the one who removes obstacles keeps in his hand. Not a weapon this time. Abundance, small, round and ready to be given away. That is one more answer to [why Ganesha comes first](/blog/why-ganesha-comes-first). You begin with the god whose hands are full.

## What does the appam have to do with lamps?

Karthigai Deepam falls on the full moon of the Tamil month of Karthigai, when the star Krittika is in the sky. On that evening, houses are lined with clay lamps, and nei appam and pori, puffed rice sweetened with jaggery, are offered as the lamps are lit. The meaning of the festival is told in [the significance of Karthigai Deepam](/blog/karthigai-deepam-significance).

Now look at the pan again. The ghee in each hollow is the same ghee that feeds the lamp by the door. Food and light, made from one substance, on the same night. The tradition rarely explains this kind of thing out loud. It simply puts the two side by side and lets you notice.

## Seven on a leaf beside the lamp

Make a small batch for the next Chaturthi or Deepam. Offer seven appams on a leaf beside the lamp; the leaf has its own logic, set out in [eating from a banana leaf](/blog/eating-on-a-banana-leaf). For the steps of the pooja itself, [puja at home, step by step](/blog/puja-at-home-step-by-step) is enough.

Then give the rest away while they are still warm. A tray of little lamps, after all, is meant to be passed around.
