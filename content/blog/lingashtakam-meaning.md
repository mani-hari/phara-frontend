My son, who is about eleven, stood beside me at the Shiva shrine in our local temple and asked, not quietly, why we were pouring water over a stone. I gave him a clumsy answer. A better one, I later realised, was already sitting in the little prayer book in our car: the Lingashtakam, eight short verses, each ending on the same refrain, I bow to that eternal Shiva lingam. The Lingashtakam meaning is not an explanation of the stone. It is a way of looking at it.

## Where does the Lingashtakam come from?

The Lingashtakam is a short Sanskrit hymn of eight verses, ashtakam meaning a set of eight. It is traditionally attributed to Adi Shankara, the great teacher who is credited with many such hymns, though scholars are not certain he wrote it, and the tradition does not insist. What matters more is that it is chanted in temples, in homes and on long car journeys, and that a child can learn it.

Each verse is four lines. The first three describe the lingam, and the fourth is always the same: tat pranamami sadashiva lingam, I bow to that lingam, the ever-auspicious Shiva.

## Lingashtakam meaning, verse by verse in outline

The first verse is the one people know best. The opening words, brahma murari surarchita lingam, describe the lingam worshipped by Brahma, by Murari, which is Vishnu, and by all the gods; pure, shining, beautiful; the lingam that destroys the sorrow that comes with birth.

The second verse sees the lingam honoured by the great sages, the one that burned the god of desire and is full of compassion, and the one that destroyed Ravana's pride. The third describes it anointed with every fragrance, the cause of growing understanding, worshipped by siddhas, the perfected ones, and by gods and demons alike.

The fourth verse adorns it with gold and great jewels and wraps it in the king of serpents; it is the lingam that ended Daksha's sacrifice. The fifth, the verse my mother likes most, sees it smeared with kumkum and sandalwood paste and garlanded with lotuses, destroying the accumulated wrongs of the past.

The sixth has it worshipped by hosts of gods with devotion and sincere feeling, bright as ten million suns. The seventh places it on an eight-petalled lotus, the cause of all creation, removing the eight kinds of poverty. The eighth sees it honoured by the teacher of the gods with flowers from the heavenly gardens, beyond the highest, the supreme self.

Then a closing verse, the part of the hymn that speaks of the hymn's benefits: whoever recites this sacred hymn near Shiva reaches Shiva's world and rejoices with him. I read that line less as a travel booking and more as a description of what recitation feels like on a good day.

## Why the lingam and not a face?

This is what my son was really asking. Why a smooth stone and not a god with eyes and hands?

The simplest answer I have found is that the lingam is the formless taking a shape we can touch. Shiva, in the tradition, is beyond any form, and the lingam is almost the least form possible, a mark, a sign. The same reasoning sits behind [why Hindus worship through images](/blog/why-hindus-worship-idols) at all. And the oldest account of the lingam is a story rather than an argument: [Shiva's column of fire on Shivaratri](/blog/maha-shivaratri-meaning), a pillar of light whose top and bottom neither Brahma nor Vishnu could find.

The answer that stays with me is a personal one. The week my father was in hospital in Chennai for his heart surgery, years before my son asked that question, I stopped at a temple on the way back one night. I had nothing useful left to do for him. The priest had closed the inner shrine, but there was a small lingam outside, and a tap. I poured a little water over it and said the refrain, the only line I knew. It was not a formless god I needed that night. It was something to offer, and somewhere to put it.

## Learning the refrain

Learn the refrain. Tat pranamami sadashiva lingam. Say it once this week with a little water poured over a lingam, if there is one near you, or simply over your own folded hands at the kitchen sink. If you want a companion hymn, the [Kolaru Pathigam](/blog/kolaru-pathigam-navagraha) is Tamil, and just as short to begin with, and [Om Namah Shivaya](/blog/om-namah-shivaya-meaning) is shorter still.
