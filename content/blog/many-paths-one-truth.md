Truth is one; the wise call it by many names. Ekam sat vipra bahudha vadanti is the most quoted line in Hinduism, printed on posters, spoken at interfaith dinners and offered as proof that all religions agree. It comes from the Rig Veda, and the verse around it deserves a reading before anyone quotes it again. In context, it says the gods of the Vedic hymns are many names for one reality. That is a remarkable thought in its own right, and a little different from the slogan.

## What does ekam sat vipra bahudha vadanti actually say?

The line is from the Rig Veda, book one, hymn 164, verse 46. Hymn 164 is long and strange, a riddle hymn traditionally attributed to the seer Dirghatamas. It asks questions it does not answer. Who has seen the first-born? Where was the life, the blood, the self of the earth? What is the one who propped apart the six realms? The poet admits, more than once, that he does not know.

Then comes verse 46. In a plain rendering: they call it Indra, Mitra, Varuna, Agni; it is also the heavenly, winged Garutman. The one being the sages call by many names: they call it Agni, Yama, Matarishvan.

So ekam sat, in its setting, means something specific. Ekam, one. Sat, that which is, being, truth. Vipra, the inspired poets. Bahudha, in many ways. Vadanti, they speak of it. The many names in the verse are the names of Vedic deities: fire, the lord of the storm, the god of death, the wind. The seer is saying that behind all of them stands a single reality.

## Does it mean all religions are the same?

Not in its original setting. The Rig Veda knew nothing of the world's later faiths, and the verse is about the gods of its own hymns. Reading it as a modern declaration about every religion is a stretch, and it helps to be honest about that.

But traditions grow by reading. Later Vedanta teachers took "truth is one, sages call it by many names" as a statement about Brahman, the single reality behind all appearances, and to know it, they said, is freedom itself, which is [what moksha means](/blog/what-is-moksha) in Vedanta. In the modern era, Swami Vivekananda quoted the verse to the world as a sign of Hindu openness toward other faiths, and many Hindus now read it that way.

Both readings are real. The first is what the verse says. The second is what the tradition did with it. A careful reader can hold both without pretending they are identical. The widened reading is not a betrayal of the original. It is the riddle hymn doing what riddles do: opening outward.

## Why has the line lasted?

Look again at the hymn it comes from. It is full of questions. It does not end with a creed. It ends, more or less, with the poet admitting how much remains unknown.

That habit of asking rather than asserting runs deep in Hinduism. The same Rig Veda contains the Nasadiya hymn on creation, which ends by wondering whether even the highest being knows how the world began. The Upanishads are dialogues. The philosophical schools argued with one another for centuries, and wrote down the arguments. Some of those arguments survive in [debate as devotion](/blog/debate-as-devotion-shastrartha), and they are livelier than the word "philosophy" suggests.

A tradition like that can keep many paths, schools and deities without insisting they collapse into one. [Meditation and puja](/blog/meditation-vs-puja) sit side by side. So do [the householder and the forest dweller](/blog/householder-and-forest-dweller). The line has lasted, perhaps, because it gave permission for all of that. If the reality is one, you do not need everyone to use your word for it.

## Saying it as a question

Hymn 164 is a riddle, and it is fitting that its most famous line has become a riddle too. Is it a statement about Vedic gods, or about all religions? Is it an answer, or an invitation?

Next time the line comes up, at a dinner or in a school project or a conversation with a friend of another faith, try saying it as a question as well as an answer. Is truth one? What names have I been using? What names have others found? The hymn it comes from would approve. Its poet asked more than he told, and he still managed to say something people are quoting thousands of years later.
