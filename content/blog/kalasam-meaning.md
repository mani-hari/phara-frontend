Take a pot, fill it with water, put five mango leaves in its mouth and a coconut on top. You have just built the most common altar in Hindu worship. The kalash meaning, simply put, is a vessel of water made into a seat for the divine: a full pot that stands for abundance, life and the whole universe held in a small space. Now try filling it slowly, and see what the act does to you.

## What does the kalash mean in worship?

In Tamil it is kalasam; in Sanskrit, kalasha; in most of North India, kalash. A pot, usually of brass, copper or silver, filled with water and crowned with leaves and a coconut. When it is fully dressed, it is called the purna kumbham, the full pot. The purna kumbham, the full pot, means abundance: nothing missing, nothing spilling over.

Tradition treats the kalasha as a small universe. A verse chanted when setting it up places the gods in its body: Vishnu at its mouth, Rudra at its neck, Brahma at its base, and the Mothers, the matrikas, in the middle. The oceans are said to be in its belly, and the Vedas in its water. Whether you take that literally or as a map, it tells you how seriously the pot is meant to be treated.

The story of the samudra manthan, the churning of the ocean in the Puranas, ends with a pot too: Dhanvantari rises from the waters holding a kalasha of amrita, the nectar of life. [What the churning of the ocean means](/blog/samudra-manthan-meaning) tells the rest of that story.

## What does each part hold?

Every element carries a meaning, and knowing them turns the setting up into a slow meditation.

- The water is life itself, and is associated with Varuna, lord of the waters.
- The mango leaves, usually five, are freshness and fertility; they are also practical, stopping the coconut from sitting directly in the water.
- The coconut stands for the head, or for consciousness, with its three eyes and hard shell. This is why the coconut on the kalash is placed last, crowning everything.
- The thread wound around the neck of the pot is connection, binding the whole together.

Inside, by family custom, there may be a pinch of turmeric, a dot of kumkum, a coin, a few grains of rice, a betel nut or a flower. There is no single correct list. Ask your family what yours put in, and keep it.

## Setting a kalash with attention

Here is where the practice lies. Kalash sthapana at home, the installation of the pot, can take two minutes or twenty. Take the twenty, at least once.

Use a clean pot; if yours is brass, the guide to [cleaning brass puja items](/blog/cleaning-brass-puja-items) will help. Pour the water slowly. Watch it rise. Take one breath for each pour. Arrange the leaves with their tips pointing up and outward, like a small crown. Place the coconut last, with its tuft upward. Tie the thread. Sit back and look at it for a moment before you begin anything else.

You will notice that the slowness changes the rest of the pooja. A kalash set in a hurry feels like a prop. A kalash set with attention feels like a presence.

The kalasha is central to many rites: [Varalakshmi Vratham at home](/blog/varalakshmi-vratham-at-home), where the pot itself is dressed as the goddess; [griha pravesh, entering a new home](/blog/griha-pravesh-puja-steps); Navratri; and nearly every homam. After the pooja, the water is not thrown away. Sprinkle it through the house, or pour it on plants. The coconut is broken and shared, or cooked.

## Try this for a month

Once a week for a month, set up a small kalash before your pooja. Any small pot will do. Do each step slowly: water, leaves, coconut, thread. Then do the rest of your practice. At the end of the month, notice how the pooja feels after the kalash compared to before. A flame usually ends the pooja, and [how to do aarti at home](/blog/how-to-do-aarti-at-home) closes the circle.
