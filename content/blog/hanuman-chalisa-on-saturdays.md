Saturday mornings in our house began with my father's voice. Before the car left for the market, before the newspaper was opened, he sat on the edge of the bed and recited the forty verses of the Hanuman Chalisa, not loudly, not fast, just all the way through. I asked him once whether he did it because of Shani. He said, "Partly. Mostly I do it so I feel braver." That, I think, is the truest account of the Hanuman Chalisa for Shani: less about fear of a planet, more about borrowing courage for the week.

## Why pray to Hanuman on Saturdays?

Saturday is Shani's day, the day of Saturn, the planet associated in Jyotish with time, hard work and slow consequences. So why do so many families pray to Hanuman on it?

A folk story, told in many regions and many versions, explains it. Ravana, at the height of his power, had imprisoned the planets, Shani among them, and kept him bound in Lanka. When Hanuman came to Lanka searching for Sita, he found Shani and set him free. Grateful, Shani promised that he would ease his hold on anyone who remembered Hanuman. In another telling, Shani tried to sit on Hanuman's shoulders to trouble him, and Hanuman simply grew larger until Shani begged to be let down.

These are not scripture in the strict sense. They are the stories families tell, and they carry a clear idea: steadiness and devotion are stronger than whatever weight Saturn brings. If you want to understand what Saturn's hard seasons actually are, [Shani sade sati explained](/blog/shani-sade-sati-explained) is a calm starting point.

## Hanuman Chalisa for Shani: what the verses actually ask for

Read the Chalisa slowly, and you notice it asks for very little that is worldly. Tulsidas, the poet who composed it in Awadhi, asks for strength, for intelligence, for knowledge, for the clearing of confusion. He praises Hanuman's service to Rama, his speed, his humility, his fearlessness.

And then there is the line most people remember: "Sankat kate mite sab peera, jo sumirai Hanumat balbeera." Trouble is cut away and all pain fades for the one who remembers the mighty Hanuman. Sankat, the word for trouble here, means something like a tight place, a narrow pass. The verse does not promise the trouble will never come. It says that remembering gives you a way through.

That is why the Hanuman Chalisa benefits people most, I think, in weeks when nothing is especially wrong. Every Saturday you rehearse the idea that you can face hard things. When a hard thing does come, the words are already in your mouth.

## Making it a weekly habit

My father kept it simple, and I have tried to do the same.

Ten minutes. That is roughly how long one unhurried recitation takes. People often ask how many times to chant the Hanuman Chalisa, and you will hear seven, eleven, even a hundred and eight on special days. But one recitation, said with attention, is complete. Better one slow reading every Saturday for a year than a hundred rushed ones once.

A lamp. Light one before you begin, sesame oil if you have it. There is more on the Saturday lamp and other steady practices in [simple sade sati remedies](/blog/sade-sati-remedies).

One place. The same corner each week, the same time if you can manage it.

If you have children, chant it with them, a few verses at a time; kids pick up the rhythm faster than we do, and there are gentle ideas in [simple mantras for kids](/blog/simple-mantras-for-kids). If your parents live far away, chant it on a video call with them on Saturday morning their time or yours. My father and I have done this across two continents. The time difference means one of us is always a little sleepy. It still works.

Women, men and children can all chant the Chalisa; nothing in the tradition of this prayer holds anyone back. If the Awadhi words feel unfamiliar, the notes on [chanting for beginners](/blog/chanting-for-beginners) help you find a pace. And when Jupiter changes sign, as in [Guru peyarchi](/blog/guru-peyarchi-jupiter-transit), the same weekly habit carries over.

## One line for Monday

Here is the small thing to carry into the coming week. Pick one line of the Chalisa, perhaps the one about sankat being cut away, and learn it by heart. Then, on Monday morning, when the inbox is full and the week feels heavy before it has begun, say it once under your breath. Not as a charm. As a reminder that you have done hard weeks before, and someone braver than you is on your side.
