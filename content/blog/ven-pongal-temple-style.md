Temple ven pongal tastes the way it does for two reasons: more moong dal than you think, and ghee added twice. Everything else is pepper, cumin and patience. If you have been looking for a ven pongal recipe that comes close to the soft, peppery mound handed out on a leaf at a Perumal temple, as Vishnu temples are called in Tamil Nadu, before sunrise, this is it, adapted for a US kitchen and meant to be offered as naivedyam, food cooked for the deity and placed before the lamp before anyone eats.

## Ven pongal recipe, the temple way

The ratio is the secret. Temple pongal keeps them close to equal, cooks them past the point you think is done, and treats ghee as an ingredient rather than a garnish.

**For the pongal (serves four)**

- 100 g raw rice (½ cup), sona masoori or any short-grain rice
- 100 g split yellow moong dal (½ cup)
- 900 ml water (about 3¾ cups), plus hot water to loosen
- 1 teaspoon salt
- 1 tablespoon ghee

**For the tempering**

- 3 tablespoons ghee, plus 1 more to finish
- 1 teaspoon whole black pepper
- 1 teaspoon cumin seeds
- 1 tablespoon grated ginger
- A sprig of curry leaves
- A pinch of asafoetida
- 12 to 15 cashews, broken

**Method**

1. Dry-roast the moong dal in a heavy pan over low heat for three or four minutes, until it smells nutty and turns a shade darker. Do not let it brown.
2. Rinse the rice and roasted dal together twice and drain.
3. Cook them with the water, salt and a tablespoon of ghee until the grains are completely soft and collapsing. Mash with the back of a ladle while hot.
4. In a small pan, warm the ghee. Fry the cashews golden and lift them out. Add pepper and cumin; when the cumin darkens, add ginger, curry leaves and asafoetida.
5. Pour the tempering over the pongal, add the cashews and stir well.
6. Just before offering, pour the last spoon of ghee over the top.

## Why is ven pongal offered at dawn in Margazhi?

Margazhi is the Tamil month that runs roughly from mid-December to mid-January, and it belongs to the early morning. In Vishnu temples across Tamil Nadu, the first pooja happens in the dark, and ven pongal is the classic offering. At the same hour, Andal's Tiruppavai is sung, thirty verses about girls waking one another before dawn to go and worship. To sing along with some understanding, read [the meaning of the Tiruppavai](/blog/tiruppavai-meaning) first.

Notice what the dish is: warm, plain, filling, nothing sharp except the pepper. It suits a cold hour and a body that has only just woken. The tradition did not choose a festive sweet for this time. It chose something steady.

Many families also make pongal on the day of the week they give to Vishnu; if you are unsure which that is in your family's practice, [which god is worshipped on which day](/blog/which-god-on-which-day) sets out the common pattern.

## How do you get the texture in a US kitchen?

The Instant Pot is your friend here. Use the pressure setting for about twelve minutes with natural release, or give a stovetop pressure cooker four or five whistles.

A few things make the difference:

- **Mash while it is hot.** Once pongal cools it sets firm, and it will not come back smooth.
- **Loosen with hot water, never cold.** Stir in a splash at a time until it falls softly from the ladle.
- **The second ghee pour.** This is what gives temple pongal its gloss. Do it at the very end.
- **Keep it plain.** No onion, no garlic, no vegetables. It is cooked to be offered.

Traditionally you cook after a bath, and you do not taste while cooking. Before the family eats, put a small portion in a bowl, set it before the lamp, and let the rest wait. If you want to widen the offering, [paal payasam cooked slowly for naivedyam](/blog/paal-payasam-naivedyam) sits well beside it, and [panchamrutham made at home](/blog/panchamrutham-at-home) completes a small abhishekam, the ritual bathing of the deity.

## A small pot before the house wakes

Try this on a winter weekday. Set the alarm twenty minutes earlier than usual. Start the pot while the house is still dark, do the tempering while it releases, light the lamp and offer the first bowl. Then serve everyone else.

You will notice the difference in how breakfast is eaten. Something that was offered first tends to be eaten more slowly. That is part of [why prasadam is shared](/blog/why-prasadam-is-shared), prasadam being food that has been offered and comes back as blessing: the food has changed status, and so, a little, have you. Let breakfast be prasadam, and let the day start from there.
