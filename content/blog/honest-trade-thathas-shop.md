My Thatha measured cloth with a wooden rod worn smooth at one end, and he never let the fabric stretch under his thumb. That was the whole of his business philosophy, and he never called it one. If you ask me what Hinduism teaches about honesty in business, I think of that rod before any verse. The tradition treats satya, truthfulness, as a daily practice rather than a doctrine, and it expects that practice to show up at the counter, in the measure and the price and the plain word to a customer.

## The Kumbakonam counter

Thatha's cloth shop was on a busy street in Kumbakonam, narrow and deep, with bolts of cotton and silk stacked to the ceiling. As a boy on summer visits I sat on a stool in the corner and watched.

Every morning, before the shutters went up, he lit a small lamp in front of a picture of Lakshmi and Ganesha on a shelf above the cash box. He did not linger. A few seconds, palms together, and then the shutters rattled open. The first sale of the day was treated as a small blessing. He would touch the first note to his eyes before putting it away, and he was never happy when the first customer went home without buying.

What I remember most is a woman who wanted a bright red cotton for her daughter. Thatha unrolled it, then paused. This dye will run in the first wash, he told her. Wash it separately, in cold water, with salt. Or take this one, a shade darker, which holds. She took the darker one. The assistant who helped at the counter said later that the red was the more expensive bolt. Thatha shrugged. She will come back, he said. She did, for years.

## Is honesty in business a Hindu idea or just good sense?

I have wondered this. Every culture has honest shopkeepers. Is there anything particularly Hindu about Thatha's rod?

Perhaps the answer is that the tradition refused to separate the two. Satya is one of the oldest values in Hindu ethics, listed in the Upanishads and the law books, and satyam eva jayate, truth alone triumphs, is a line from the Mundaka Upanishad. But for Thatha, satya was not a teaching he quoted. It was how he measured. Hindu values at work, for him, meant doing the ordinary thing without cheating, every day, and letting the lamp in the morning remind him who was watching.

Valluvar says it in the Tirukkural: the best trade for traders is to guard others' goods as their own. That idea of an honest measure in trade runs through Tamil life. I looked at the verse more closely in [Hinduism and business ethics](/blog/hinduism-business-ethics).

Thatha's version was simpler. An honest shop and a busy shop, he said, take the same number of years to build. The honest one lasts longer.

## A New Jersey version of the measuring rod

I do not sell cloth. I work in an office in New Jersey, and most of my days are meetings, spreadsheets and calls. For a long time I thought Thatha's rod had nothing to do with me.

But the rod turns up in odd places. A sales call where rounding an estimate up would be easy and nobody would check. A vendor who sends a generous gift just before a contract renewal. A colleague who did most of the work on a slide deck that is about to be presented under my name. None of these are crimes. They are thumbs on the cloth.

Satya in business, I have found, is mostly about these small moments that no one sees. The dramatic ethical choices, the ones in films, rarely come. The small stretch of fabric comes every week. In [the Kubera story](/blog/kubera-story-meaning), the god of riches is the guardian of wealth rather than its owner. Thatha, I think, saw himself the same way.

When I lose my temper at work, which happens, I notice I am also more likely to cut a corner. The two travel together. Valluvar has a chapter for that too, and [the Tirukkural on anger](/blog/tirukkural-on-anger) is the one I reread.

## One thing to carry into the week

I still think of the rod whenever I am about to hit send on something that matters. There is more on living this way in [dharma in everyday life](/blog/dharma-meaning-in-everyday-life).

One thing to carry into the week: before you send the number, the estimate or the promise, measure it once more without your thumb on the cloth.
