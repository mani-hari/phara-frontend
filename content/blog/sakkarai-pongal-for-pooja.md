- q: How do I melt jaggery for sweet pongal without grit?

  a: Yes. Cook the rice and roasted moong dal with extra water until very soft, around three whistles

Sweet pongal is the simplest serious naivedyam there is. Naivedyam is food cooked for and offered to the deity before anyone eats, and this sakkarai pongal recipe asks for only rice, moong dal, jaggery, ghee and twenty unhurried minutes of attention. You do not need a festival to make it. A Friday lamp is reason enough. What the dish really teaches is patience, because jaggery punishes hurry.

## Sakkarai pongal recipe: what goes in the pot

Sakkarai means sugar or sweetness in Tamil, and here the sweetness comes from jaggery, vellam, the unrefined cane sugar that gives this pongal its colour and its deep, caramel taste. The proportion that matters is roughly three parts rice to one part dal. This makes enough for a family, with a small portion for the offering.

- Raw rice, short-grain if you have it: 150 g (3/4 cup)
- Split yellow moong dal: 50 g (1/4 cup)
- Water for cooking: 750 ml (3 cups), plus 120 ml (1/2 cup) for the jaggery
- Jaggery, grated or broken small: 250 g (about 1 1/4 cups, packed)
- Ghee: 60 ml (1/4 cup), divided
- Cardamom: 4 pods, seeds crushed
- Cashews: 15 g (12 to 15 nuts)
- Raisins: 10 g (1 tablespoon)
- Edible camphor (pachai karpooram): a tiny pinch, only if your family uses it

A word on camphor: only the edible kind, sold for cooking, belongs in food. The camphor used for aarti is not edible. If you are unsure which you have, leave it out. The pongal is complete without it.

## Why does jaggery need patience?

This is where the dish is won or lost, and where the practice is.

1. Dry-roast the moong dal in a heavy pan on low heat, stirring, until it turns pale gold and smells nutty. This takes a few minutes and is worth every one.
2. Wash the rice and add it to the dal with the 750 ml of water. Cook until both are very soft and collapse when pressed with a spoon. In a pot this takes about twenty-five minutes; in a pressure cooker, four or five whistles on medium heat. Mash lightly.
3. While it cooks, melt the jaggery in the 120 ml of water over low heat until it dissolves completely. Strain it through a fine sieve. Jaggery almost always carries a little grit, and nobody wants that in an offering.
4. Take the rice and dal off high heat. Pour in the strained jaggery syrup and stir on the lowest flame. If you add syrup to a furiously boiling pot, the grains can seize and turn hard. Low and slow keeps them soft.
5. Add half the ghee and stir. Wait a minute. Add a little more. Let each addition melt in before the next.
6. In a small pan, heat the remaining ghee and fry the cashews until golden, then the raisins until they puff. Pour everything, ghee included, over the pongal. Add the cardamom and the camphor pinch, if using, and stir once more.

The pongal should be loose, glossy and soft, a little looser than you think, because it firms as it cools. Steps three to five are where most people reach for their phone. Try not to. Keep your eyes on the pot, not the screen. I find the whole dish changes when you do. Its savoury twin is [ven pongal, temple style](/blog/ven-pongal-temple-style), and the attitude behind both is in [cooking as an offering](/blog/cooking-as-an-offering).

## When is sweet pongal offered?

Sakkarai pongal appears across the South Indian ritual year. It is a common Friday offering for Lakshmi and for Amman, the Mother goddess worshipped in village and city temples alike; the guide to [Friday Lakshmi pooja at home](/blog/friday-lakshmi-pooja) fits naturally with it. It is cooked at dawn in Vishnu temples through the cold month of Margazhi, and offered on Navratri evenings. And it is the dish that gives the harvest festival its name; if you are cooking it far from Tamil Nadu, [making pongal in a US kitchen](/blog/making-pongal-in-a-us-kitchen) helps.

The traditional rules are few and gentle. Cook after a bath. Do not taste the dish before it is offered; this is the one habit that makes it naivedyam rather than dinner. Place a small portion in a clean bowl before the lamp, say a few words or simply sit for a moment, and then serve it to everyone as prasadam, the offered food returned as blessing. There is a thoughtful piece on [prasad and gratitude](/blog/prasad-and-gratitude) that explains why that shift matters.

## Try this on Friday

Make a small pot this Friday. Offer the first spoon before anyone tastes it, even if the only witness is the lamp. Then notice how different the second spoon feels. Same rice, same jaggery. Something has changed, and it is not the pot.
