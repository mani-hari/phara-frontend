Before the Bhagavad Gita says a word of philosophy, the conches sound. Krishna blows Panchajanya. Arjuna blows Devadatta. Bhima blows his great shell, Paundra, and the noise, the text says, makes the earth and sky tremble. The shell is the first voice of the battlefield, and in temples it is still the first voice of worship. That is where the meaning of the conch shell in Hinduism begins: the shankha is the sound of beginnings, the announcement that something important is about to happen.

## What does the shankha stand for?

Look at almost any image of Vishnu and you will find the shankha in one of his four hands, along with the chakra, the discus, the gada, the mace, and the padma, the lotus. The four together are sometimes read as a complete description of the divine at work: the conch that calls, the discus that protects, the mace that disciplines, the lotus that blooms regardless of the mud.

In most tellings, the shankha stands for sound, and the sound it stands for is Om. Hold a large conch to your ear and you hear the ocean. Blow into it and you hear a long, rising, resonant tone that fills a room. Devotees often liken it to the primal vibration from which, in the tradition's imagination, the world was sung into being.

The conch also comes from the ocean in the old stories. In the account of the churning of the ocean, when gods and demons churned the cosmic sea for the nectar of immortality, many treasures rose from the waves, and among them was a conch. So did Lakshmi, the goddess of fortune. That is why the shankha is linked with her and kept at many home shrines as a sign of auspiciousness. The piece on [the churning of the ocean](/blog/samudra-manthan-meaning) tells the whole story.

## Panchajanya and the conches of Kurukshetra

Krishna's conch has a history of its own. The Bhagavata Purana tells how Krishna's teacher, the sage Sandipani, lost his son to the sea. When Krishna went to bring the boy back, he found that a demon named Panchajana, who lived in a shell beneath the ocean, had taken him. Krishna defeated the demon and took the shell, and it became his conch, named after its former owner: Panchajanya.

Then comes the first chapter of the Gita. The two armies face each other at Kurukshetra, and the text pauses for a roll call of conches. Each warrior's shell is named. Krishna's Panchajanya, Arjuna's Devadatta, Yudhishthira's Anantavijaya. It reads almost like a list of battle horns, and it is easy to skip. But notice where it sits. Immediately afterwards, Arjuna collapses in despair, and the entire teaching of the Gita follows.

The story says the warriors blew their conches to begin a war. The story is really about announcing yourself before a hard thing. You sound the conch not because you are certain, but because you have decided to show up. Arjuna's doubt comes after the conch, not before. That is not a contradiction. That is how most of us face the hard things in our lives.

## Why is the conch blown in worship?

In temples, the shankha is blown at the start of aarti, at the opening of festivals, at weddings and on auspicious occasions. It clears the air of other sounds, much as [the temple bell does](/blog/why-ring-a-temple-bell), and it gathers attention toward the sanctum. Why blow a conch in puja at home? For the same reason: to mark the moment worship begins.

The conch is also used as a vessel. Filled with water, milk or other offerings, it is poured over the deity during abhishekam, the ritual bath. Some families keep a rare dakshinavarti shankha, a right-turning conch that spirals in the opposite direction to most shells, at their home shrine as a sign of Lakshmi's grace. And in parts of eastern India, especially Bengal, women blow the conch at dusk, as the evening lamp is lit, a sound that carries across whole neighbourhoods. Practices vary widely by region and family.

## Only breath makes it sing

Notice one thing about the conch. It is hollow. On its own it makes no sound at all. It only sings when breath passes through it, and the sound it makes depends entirely on that breath: long or short, strong or faltering. That is not a bad description of a devotee. We are not the source of the sound. We are the shell, and the practice, like the [forehead mark renewed each morning](/blog/vibhuti-kumkum-tilak-meaning), is simply staying open enough for the breath to pass through.
