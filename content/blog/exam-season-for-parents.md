It is past midnight. My son is asleep over his science textbook, one cheek on the periodic table, and I am in the kitchen making tea that nobody asked for. Exam season is hard on both sides of the bedroom door. If you have ever typed "prayer for child's exam" into a search bar late at night, here is what I have learned: the prayer is mostly for you. It gives your worry somewhere to go that is not your child. A lamp, a Saraswati verse and a quiet intention in their name are enough, and none of it requires them to do anything.

## Where does a parent's worry go?

You know the restlessness. You walk past their door three times. You ask about the syllabus at dinner, then hate yourself a little for asking. You read an article about sleep and forward it to them, which they do not open. The worry wants to be useful, and when it cannot be useful, it leaks.

A friend of mine, a surgeon, once told me that the hardest part of her job was not operating but waiting outside the operating theatre when her own mother was inside. "I had all this skill," she said, "and nowhere to put it." Parents in exam season are like that. We cannot take the exam. We can only stand outside.

Prayer, for me, became a place to set the worry down. Not to pass it on, not to turn it into pressure, but to put it somewhere safe for the night. If the worry is keeping you awake too, these [small rituals for anxious days](/blog/rituals-for-anxious-days) are meant for exactly that.

## A prayer for your child's exam that stays gentle

Here is what I do, and what I have seen other parents do.

In the evening, after the house is quiet, I light a small lamp in front of Saraswati, the goddess of learning. I say "Om Aim Saraswatyai Namah" eleven times, slowly. Then I make a quiet sankalpam, the spoken statement of intention, in my son's name: his name, his birth star, and a single sentence asking for clarity and calm. I do not ask for marks. I ask that he remember what he has learned and that he be at peace in the hall.

If you want to know more about the goddess you are praying to, and about Hayagriva, the horse-headed Vishnu many South Indian families also pray to for memory, [Saraswati and Hayagriva, the deities of learning](/blog/saraswati-and-hayagriva) tells their stories. Some parents also book a Saraswati homam, a fire ritual for learning, at a temple before a big exam. If you do, you do not need to announce it. A little prasadam, the blessed offering, quietly left beside their plate is enough.

## Being calm company

This is how to support a child during exams, as far as I can tell: food, sleep, and fewer questions.

- **Food.** Regular meals, at the usual times. A bowl of fruit on the desk without comment.
- **Sleep.** Protect it. The house goes quiet at a fixed hour, including your television.
- **Fewer questions about marks.** They already know what is at stake. Asking again only adds weight.

The verse I hold onto in exam season is from the Bhagavad Gita, chapter two, verse forty-seven: you have a right to your work, but not to its fruits. Krishna says it to Arjuna on a battlefield, which feels about right for a house in exam season. For students it means: prepare fully, then loosen your grip on the result. For parents it means something harder. We have a right to our care, our meals, our lamp. We do not have a right to the marks.

My son, it turns out, has his own small ritual. He lights a tea-light before he studies and says a mantra I taught him years ago and forgot about. His version is in this [five-minute prayer before exams](/blog/prayer-before-exams). When the exams are done, there is always a festival waiting. Navratri, with its day for books and learning, is a good one to share, and [why Navratri lasts nine nights](/blog/why-navratri-lasts-nine-nights) is worth reading together.

## One small thing to carry into this week

One evening this week, do not ask "how much did you study?" Just bring the tea, set it down beside the books, and sit with your child for five minutes. Say nothing about exams at all. They will remember that you sat there long after they have forgotten the marks.
