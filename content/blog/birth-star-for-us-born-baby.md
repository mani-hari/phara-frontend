A baby born at night in Texas and a baby born at that same instant in Chennai share a birth star. The Moon sits in the same place among the stars for both of them; only the clocks and the calendars disagree. So the nakshatra for a baby born in the US is worked out exactly as it would be in India, from the Moon's position at the moment of birth. What needs care is the conversion of time, and a few things that genuinely do change with place.

## One sky, many clocks

A nakshatra is one of twenty-seven segments of the sky along the Moon's path, each named for a star or a group of stars. The Moon passes through all twenty-seven in about a month, spending roughly a day in each. The old image, told in the Puranas, is of the Moon as a husband visiting a different star-wife each night: Ashwini, Bharani, Krittika, Rohini, and on through to Revati. Your janma nakshatra, the birth star, is simply whichever wife the Moon was visiting when you were born.

The Moon's position is a fact about the sky, not about any city. At a given instant it is in the same place whether you look up from Houston or from Madurai. The local date is not universal. When it is late on a Tuesday night in Texas, it is already Wednesday morning in India. The sky is one; the calendars are many. For adults checking their own star, there is a separate explanation of [how to find your nakshatra](/blog/how-to-find-your-nakshatra).

## How is a US-born baby's nakshatra worked out?

Three details matter: the exact birth time, the city of birth, and the time zone in force at that moment, including whether daylight saving time was on. The hospital records the time to the minute. Use that, not the family's memory of "around midnight".

An astrologer, or astrological software, converts the local time to a universal time and calculates where the Moon was. If you want to check against an Indian panchangam, which lists nakshatra ending times in IST, Indian Standard Time, you convert the birth time to IST first. This is where mistakes creep in. Forgetting daylight saving shifts the time by an hour, and if the Moon happened to be changing stars near the birth hour, that hour can change the nakshatra. The guide to [reading a panchangam](/blog/how-to-read-a-panchangam) explains why these ending times fall at odd hours.

## What changes with place, and what does not?

The nakshatra, and the rasi, the Moon sign, do not change with birthplace. The lagna, the rising sign, does. It depends on which part of the zodiac was rising on the eastern horizon of the birth city at that moment, and it changes roughly every two hours. In Jyotish, the twelve houses of the chart are counted from the lagna, so they depend on place too. The difference between the Moon sign and the birth star is set out in [rasi vs nakshatra](/blog/rasi-vs-nakshatra).

One more practical difference. The star birthday, the day each year when the Moon returns to your birth nakshatra in your birth month, is often marked with an archana or [an ayushya homam](/blog/ayushya-homam-star-birthday). Because it is usually reckoned from the star prevailing at local sunrise, it may fall a day apart in India and America. Grandparents in Chennai may celebrate on one day and parents in Texas on the next. Both are correct for where they stand.

## One child, one sky

The answer is simpler than the worry. Your child belongs to one sky, the same one their grandparents see from the other side of the world. Give the astrologer the local time, the city and the time zone, and let the numbers do the rest. The conversion is arithmetic. The star itself was never in question. And if the family also wants a name beginning with the syllable traditionally linked to that star, that is a question for [the naming ceremony in America](/blog/namakaranam-in-america), which starts from the same star.
