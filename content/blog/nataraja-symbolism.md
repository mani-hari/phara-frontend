The dancing Shiva is a diagram of the universe you can hold in your hand. Every limb is doing a different job, and all of them happen at once. Nataraja symbolism is best read piece by piece: a drum for creation, a flame for dissolution, a hand that says "do not be afraid", a foot pressing down on forgetfulness and another lifted in release, all inside a ring of fire. Nataraja means lord of dance, and the dance he performs is the life of the whole cosmos, begun, sustained and ended in a single movement.

## Nataraja symbolism, hand by hand

Start at the upper right. That hand holds the damaru, the small hourglass drum. Its beat is the first sound, the pulse from which time and speech and matter unfold. In the upper left hand, balanced on the palm, is a flame. It is the fire that ends things. The two upper hands face each other across the figure, creation on one side and dissolution on the other, and the dancer does not seem to prefer either.

The lower right hand is raised with the palm out, the abhaya mudra, the gesture that means "fear not". It is the hand of preservation, the hand that keeps the world going while it lasts. The lower left arm swings across the body like an elephant's trunk and points down to the lifted left foot. That pointing is the key to the whole image. Look there, it says. That is where the way out is.

Around him burns the prabhamandala, a ring of flame that stands for the universe itself, always burning, never consumed. His locks fly out sideways because he is spinning, and caught in them are the river Ganga and the crescent moon. The form we know best was fixed by the bronze casters of the Chola country in Tamil Nadu, who made the dancer so balanced that a single cast figure can stand on one foot for centuries.

## Who is the dwarf under his foot?

Under the right foot lies a small, squirming figure named Apasmara. Apasmara means forgetfulness, a loss of awareness; he stands for ignorance, the blur in which we lose track of what we are. Shiva stands on him, but notice that he does not kill him.

The texts and teachers explain why. If ignorance were destroyed completely, there would be nothing left to overcome, no learning, no seeking, and no reason for the dance to continue. So Apasmara is held down, firmly and permanently, and the dance goes on above him. It is an honest picture of how the mind works. Forgetting never disappears for good. It has to be stood on again every morning. There is a similar honesty in the story of the poison Shiva held in his throat, told in [the Neelakantha story](/blog/neelakantha-blue-throat-story): what cannot be destroyed can at least be contained.

## What is the ananda tandava?

Shiva has more than one dance. The fierce tandava of destruction belongs to the end of an age. The dance in the bronze is different. It is the ananda tandava, and ananda tandava means the dance of bliss, joy that has no cause outside itself. Tradition places it at Chidambaram, where the Lord is worshipped in exactly this form; [the Chidambaram Nataraja temple](/blog/chidambaram-nataraja-temple) guide describes what devotees experience there.

Shaiva tradition reads the whole figure as the five acts of Shiva, the pancha kritya: creation in the drum, preservation in the raised hand, dissolution in the fire, concealment in the planted foot that holds the world in its illusions, and grace in the lifted foot that frees the soul. All five at once, with no strain.

Tirumular, in the Tirumantiram, takes the image further inward. For him, the dance is not only in a distant hall. It happens within the body, in the heartbeat and the breath, and the true Chidambaram is the space of the heart. The festival of [Arudra Darshan](/blog/arudra-darshan-meaning) celebrates this dance each winter, and the twilight hour of [Pradosham](/blog/pradosham-meaning) keeps its rhythm fortnight by fortnight.

## What the calm face is telling you

Look last at the face. Everything else in the figure is moving: hair, hands, fire, feet. The face is still, faintly smiling, almost bored with how easy it is.

That is the reframe the bronze offers. We tend to think calm needs stillness, that peace comes when the five things demanding our attention finally stop. The dancer says otherwise. Creation, maintenance, endings, confusion and release are all happening at once, and the calm is in the face, not in the stillness of the body.

Next time the day feels like five things happening at once, remember the dancer balanced on one foot. You do not have to stop the dance to find your centre. You only have to keep your face.
