Seva is service in which you are not the point. The seva meaning is as simple as that, and as difficult: you attend to someone, or something, fully, without keeping track of what you get back. The moment you start counting how much you have given, it becomes something else. Still useful, perhaps. But no longer seva.

## What does seva mean, exactly?

The word comes from the Sanskrit root sev, to attend upon, to wait on, to serve. It is the attention a disciple gives a teacher, a host gives a guest, a devotee gives the deity. Temple volunteers do seva when they sweep the steps or serve food. A son does seva when he sits with an old parent. A stranger does seva when she stops for a lost child.

So what is seva in Hinduism, as opposed to ordinary kindness? The difference is the frame. Seva is offered as worship. The person served is treated, quietly, as a form of the divine. The Taittiriya Upanishad puts it in four short phrases every school child in India once learned: treat your mother as God, your father as God, your teacher as God, your guest as God. Seva extends that list to anyone in need.

Selfless service, in this sense, does not mean you have no self. It means the self is not at the centre of the act. The Bhagavad Gita calls this nishkama karma, action without craving for its fruit. Nishkama karma does not mean "do not care about results"; it means "do your best, and let go of the grip". The Gita's side of it is in [work without attachment](/blog/gita-on-work-without-attachment); [work as worship](/blog/work-as-worship) carries the same idea into an office.

## Why does seva feel different from helping?

You can test this. The next time there is a gathering at home, a festival lunch, a birthday, a pooja, volunteer to wash the dishes. Do it the ordinary way first: fast, a little resentful, keeping one ear on the conversation you are missing. Notice the irritation. It usually has a voice. It says: why is it always me?

Then try the next stack differently. Wash each plate as if it belonged to a guest you love. Rinse it properly. Dry it. Put it away. Give the plate the full attention you would give a person. You are not pretending to enjoy it. You are just paying attention.

Most people find something shifts. The irritation does not vanish, but it gets quieter, because the mind is busy with the plate instead of the story about the plate. That is the difference between helping and seva. Helping keeps you at the centre. Seva moves you to the side.

Temples in the US run on this kind of work: parking, cooking, cleaning, teaching the children. A practical guide to [volunteering at a Hindu temple](/blog/volunteering-at-a-hindu-temple) describes how to begin.

## Where does seva go wrong?

It goes wrong in three familiar ways.

The first is resentment: you keep serving, but the irritation builds until the service becomes a grievance. The second is keeping score: you remember every meal you cooked, every drive you gave, and you wait for the balance to be paid. The third is display: the seva that is photographed, posted, announced at the committee meeting.

The correction for all three is the same, and it is not "try harder". It is shrink it. Do less, and do it fully. One act done completely is seva. Ten acts done while counting are not.

Seva sits close to dana, the tradition's thinking on giving, set out in [dana in Hinduism](/blog/dana-in-hinduism). The two work together. Dana gives something away; seva gives your attention. Some families combine them on a birthday; there is a column on [giving on your birthday](/blog/giving-on-birthdays).

## Seva nobody thanks you for

Once a day, for seven days, do one act of seva that nobody will thank you for. Refill the water jug at the office. Clean the sink someone else left. Carry a neighbour's bin in. Do it completely, not half. Then take one minute afterward, just one, and notice how you feel. Not how you should feel. How you actually feel. By the end of the week, you may notice that the minute afterward is the most interesting part.
