Why no onion or garlic in Hindu pooja food? The short answer: many Hindu traditions class onion and garlic as rajasic or tamasic, foods said to stir up or dull the mind, and so they are left out of offerings made to the deity. The longer answer is more interesting. It involves the three gunas, a folk tale about the churning of the ocean, and a kitchen experiment you can run yourself. You do not need to accept any of it on faith. You can taste it.

## Why no onion or garlic in Hindu pooja food?

The main reason goes back to the three gunas, the qualities that the tradition sees in everything: sattva, clarity and balance; rajas, activity and restlessness; and tamas, inertia and heaviness. Food gets sorted the same way, and [the sattvic diet](/blog/sattvic-diet-explained) sets out the full scheme.

Onion and garlic are seen as strongly stimulating: pungent, heating, powerful in flavour and in their effect on the body. So they are often classed as rajasic, and sometimes as tamasic, especially when eaten in quantity or cooked heavily. Is onion tamasic? The honest answer is that traditions disagree about which box it goes in. What they agree on is that it is not sattvic.

That is why temple kitchens, Vaishnava homes and many families cooking naivedyam, the food offered to the deity before it is shared, leave them out. The idea is that food offered to God should be food that supports a clear, calm mind.

Customs vary widely, though. Some families never cook with onion and garlic at all. Many others eat them every day and skip them only on pooja days, fasting days, or during particular months. Neither is wrong.

## Is there a story behind it?

There is, and it is a good one, as long as you know what kind of story it is.

A popular folk tale ties onion and garlic to [the churning of the ocean](/blog/samudra-manthan-meaning), when gods and demons churned for amrita, the nectar of immortality. As Vishnu, disguised as Mohini, served it to the gods, a demon named Rahu slipped into the line and swallowed a mouthful before the Sun and Moon recognised him. Vishnu's discus cut off his head at once.

In the folk story of Rahu, Ketu and the onion, drops of the nectar mixed with blood fell to the earth from Rahu's severed throat, and from those drops grew onion and garlic. That is why, the tale says, they are potent, full of life-giving power, but not pure enough to offer to God.

The churning of the ocean is told in the Puranas. The part about onion and garlic is not. It is a story, not scripture, told to explain a practice that already existed. Stories like this are how grandmothers taught; they carry a rule in a form a child will remember.

## What does cooking without onion and garlic do to your attention?

This is where you can test it for yourself.

Cooking without onion and garlic is not cooking without flavour. South Indian temple cooking has been doing it for centuries. The flavour comes from other places: asafoetida, a pinch of which gives a savoury depth; ginger; curry leaves; mustard seeds and urad dal crackling in hot oil for the tempering; tamarind, jaggery, coconut, fresh coriander.

Many people who try it notice two things. The food feels lighter. And the kitchen smells different, less heavy, cleaner. Some people also notice they eat more slowly, because the flavours are subtler and invite attention. That attention is close to what the tradition is really asking for, and [eating with your hands](/blog/eating-with-your-hands) or [eating off a banana leaf](/blog/eating-on-a-banana-leaf) brings the body into the meal in the same way.

Jain practice avoids onion and garlic too, along with other root vegetables, but for a different reason: ahimsa, non-harm. Pulling a root from the ground kills the whole plant and the small creatures living in the soil around it. Same kitchen rule, different logic; [ahimsa in daily life](/blog/ahimsa-in-daily-life) follows that thread.

## A week of pooja-style dinners

Try one week of pooja-style cooking at dinner: no onion, no garlic. Use asafoetida, ginger, curry leaves and a good tempering instead. If lighter eating is new to you, read [fasting and focus](/blog/fasting-and-focus) alongside it.

At the end of each meal, notice not what you miss, but what you taste. Most people find there is more there than they expected.
