The Kolaru Pathigam is a Tamil hymn of about ten verses by the child saint Tirugnana Sambandar, sung when he was warned that the planets were against a journey. Its message is simple: for one who holds Shiva in the heart, every graha, every day, every star turns kind, graha being the tradition's word for a planet. Families still recite it when a hard planetary period is announced, less as a remedy than as a way of steadying the mind.

A few years ago our family astrologer in Madurai looked at my chart and mentioned, in that careful voice astrologers use, that a difficult period was coming. My aunt in Chennai began listing poojas. On our weekly video call, my father said nothing for a while and then began to sing, slowly, in the old Tamil, the Kolaru Pathigam. He did not explain it. He just sang two verses and asked how my son's school was going.

## What is the Kolaru Pathigam?

A pathigam is a set of roughly ten verses, the basic unit of the Tevaram, the great collection of Tamil hymns to Shiva by the Nayanmars, the Tamil Shaiva saints. Tirugnana Sambandar is the youngest and most beloved of those saints; the tradition says he was given milk by Parvati herself as a small child and began singing at once.

The story behind this hymn comes from his life. The queen of the Pandya kingdom, Mangaiyarkarasi, sent word asking him to come to Madurai, where the king had turned away from Shiva. The older saint Appar, who loved Sambandar dearly, warned him not to go. The day was unfavourable, Appar said; the planets were badly placed; the journey was dangerous. Sambandar answered him in song. The planets cannot harm those who belong to Shiva, he sang, and set off. This is Sambandar's Kolaru Pathigam, and in the tradition's telling, the journey went well.

## What does the Kolaru Pathigam say about the planets?

The Kolaru Pathigam's meaning is surprisingly direct. The verses name the Sun, the Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, Saturn, and the two shadow planets Rahu and Ketu, and declare that all of them are good, all of them are kind, to those who keep in their hearts the Lord who wears the crescent moon and the Ganga in his matted hair. The refrain insists: they are good, they are very good.

What I find moving is that the hymn does not deny the grahas. It does not say astrology is nonsense. It names every planet, one by one, the way you might name every person at a table you were nervous about joining. Then it says: you are welcome here, and I am not afraid of you. It is a Tamil hymn to the navagrahas that is really about trust. Many families who are anxious about [what Sade Sati is traditionally said to mean](/blog/shani-sade-sati-explained) keep this song close for exactly that reason.

## How do families recite it today?

Some recite it every day. Many recite it on Saturdays, the day associated with Shani, or through the years of Sade Sati or a difficult dasha, a planetary period in one's chart. Some light a lamp first. My father simply sings it after his morning coffee.

If you are new to it, listen first. The old Tamil of Sambandar is not easy even for Tamil speakers, and a good recording will teach you the rhythm before the words. Then read along with a meaning, a verse at a time. You do not have to memorise all of it; one verse, understood, is worth more than ten recited in a hurry. If chanting itself is unfamiliar, the [beginner's guide to chanting](/blog/chanting-for-beginners) covers posture and pace. And if you find that silence after the verse is the part that helps, [a short practice of silence](/blog/mouna-vratam-silence) sits well beside it. On Mondays my father adds [the Lingashtakam, eight short verses to Shiva](/blog/lingashtakam-meaning), which I have written about separately.

Frame it as the tradition frames it. Jyotish, the astrology of the tradition, describes tendencies and seasons; it does not dictate fate. The hymn takes the planets seriously enough to name them, and then refuses to be ruled by dread.

## Before the day you are dreading

Pick a day you are dreading this week, a hard meeting, a medical appointment, a conversation you have been avoiding. Before it, listen to the Kolaru Pathigam once, or recite one verse. Then notice, without judging, whether the dread gets smaller. It might not. But my father has sung it through a heart surgery and a knee operation, and he says the song never promised him anything except company.
