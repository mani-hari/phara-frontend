Namaste literally means "I bow to you". I saw how far that small phrase has travelled when a colleague ended our Monday team meeting with it, palms together, smiling at her camera. Afterwards she sent me a private message: had she done it right? The namaste meaning is simple enough to fit in one line, but her question stayed with me all week, because it was really a question about respect, and about who gets to use a gesture that came from somewhere else.

## Namaste meaning, word by word

The word is Sanskrit. Namah means bowing, reverence, a lowering of oneself. Te means to you. Put together, namaste is "I bow to you", or more gently, "my respects to you". The gesture that goes with it, palms pressed together at the chest with a slight bow of the head, is called anjali mudra, the gesture of offering.

You will often hear a longer reading: "the divine in me honours the divine in you." It is not a literal translation. The word itself says nothing about the divine. But it is a reasonable reading, and a beautiful one, drawn from the old Hindu idea that the same reality lives in every person. When you ask what namaste really means, the honest answer is both. It means "I bow to you", and in the tradition's larger view, the one you bow to is never only a person.

My grandfather, who ran a cloth shop in Kumbakonam, greeted every customer with folded hands, whether they bought a bolt of silk or only asked the price of a towel. He never explained it. I think now it was a way of reminding himself that the person across the counter was not a transaction.

## Is it okay for a colleague to say namaste?

This is the question my colleague was really asking, and I have heard versions of it at school pick-ups, at yoga studios and at office parties. My answer is yes, with warmth.

When someone who did not grow up with the gesture uses it sincerely, it usually lands as respect. She was not performing. She had learned it somewhere, liked what it meant, and wanted to offer it. What I told her after that meeting was simple: she had done it right, and the slight bow of the head was the best part. She seemed relieved. I think she had been worried she was taking something that was not hers.

There are times when it feels different. When namaste is printed on a joke mug or used as a punchline, it can sting a little, the way anything precious does when it is treated carelessly. But I would rather meet a hundred sincere, slightly awkward namastes than police one of them. People learn a tradition by touching it, and correction offered with a smile goes further than a lecture. [Explaining Hindu festivals to coworkers](/blog/explaining-hindu-festivals-to-coworkers) follows the same spirit, at more length.

## Namaste, namaskaram, vanakkam: how should you respond?

The gesture changes its name as it travels across India. Namaskaram is common in Kerala, Karnataka and among Telugu speakers. In Tamil homes like ours it is vanakkam, said with folded hands, and it is what my son says to his grandparents at the start of every video call, even when he is half asleep. In some families, children touch the feet of elders instead, or first, and receive a hand on the head in return.

The words differ. The gesture underneath them is the same: bowing the head over folded hands. It is the same gesture a devotee makes before the deity, and before [breaking a coconut at the temple](/blog/why-break-a-coconut). The only difference is who is in front of you.

If you are wondering how to respond to namaste, the simplest way is to return it. Fold your hands, bow your head slightly, and say namaste back. A smile is part of it. If your hands are full, a nod and the word are enough. Nobody is keeping score.

## One small thing for the week

This week, greet one person with your full attention. Palms together or not, it does not matter. Just stop what you are doing, look at them, and let the greeting be the only thing happening for those two seconds. Notice how the conversation that follows is different. The small rituals of respect, like the lines of a [kolam at the doorstep](/blog/kolam-and-rangoli-meaning) or the flame of [camphor at the end of an aarti](/blog/camphor-aarti-meaning), work because they slow us down for a moment. A greeting can do the same.
