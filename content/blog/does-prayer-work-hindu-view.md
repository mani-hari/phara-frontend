The night of my father's knee operation, I prayed from a New Jersey kitchen while my mother and sister waited in a Chennai hospital corridor. It was afternoon for me, late night for them, and I remember the light coming through the blinds onto the counter. Does prayer work? I still cannot tell you what that prayer did to the surgery. I can tell you what it did to me. That is the only honest answer I have, and I think it is the one the tradition itself gives.

## Does prayer work the way we hope?

Most of us, when we pray hardest, are asking for something specific: a clean scan, a job offer, a safe flight, a recovery. And the honest answer is that nobody can promise outcomes. Not a priest, not a temple, not a book. Anyone who promises that a particular prayer will produce a particular result is promising something that is not theirs to give.

The Hindu view on prayer has always held this more carefully than we sometimes remember. In the second chapter of the Bhagavad Gita, Krishna tells Arjuna that his right is to the action alone, never to its fruits. Prayer is an action. It is offered, not transacted. You do your part, with your whole heart, and you let go of your grip on what comes back. [Work without attachment](/blog/gita-on-work-without-attachment) stays with this verse longer than I can here.

It sounds cold when you first hear it. It is not. It is what makes prayer bearable when the answer is not the one you wanted.

## What happens when prayers are not answered?

My father came through that surgery. But I have sat with friends whose prayers were not answered, at least not in the way they asked. A cousin whose mother did not recover. A colleague whose visa was denied after months of prayer. What do you say about unanswered prayers? Hinduism does not offer a tidy explanation, and I have learned not to reach for one.

What it offers instead is sharanagati, surrender, the act of placing yourself in the care of something larger. Surrender is not giving up. It is closer to what you do in a hospital corridor at two in the morning when there is nothing left to arrange: you stop trying to control and you let yourself be held. Prayer and surrender, in this sense, are the same movement.

Tirumular, the Tamil saint, wrote in the Tirumantiram that the ignorant say love and Shiva are two, but love itself is Shiva, anbe sivam. I read that line again during the long week of my father's recovery, sitting up late with the phone beside me. It did not change the medical facts. It changed what I thought I was doing. I was not bargaining with a distant God. I was loving my father from eight thousand miles away, and the love itself was the prayer.

## What prayer does change

Here is what I can say with some confidence, from my own experience and from watching others.

Prayer changes attention. It gathers a scattered mind into one place, even for a minute. Prayer changes the ability to wait. The hours in a waiting room are the same length, but they feel different when you have something to hold. And prayer changes who we remember. The week of the surgery, I called my aunt in Madurai, whom I had not spoken to in months, because I had been praying for the whole family and she came to mind.

The next day, my family got on a video call and my father, weak but awake, asked us to chant together. My son joined from the sofa in his pyjamas. My mother held the phone. Two continents of family, one small chant, badly out of sync. I have no idea what it did for the healing. I know what it did for the family. For anyone in that place now, [praying for a sick parent](/blog/praying-for-a-sick-parent) may be kinder reading.

## One small thing for this week

Pray for one person without asking for anything specific. Not "let her get the job" or "let him recover". Just hold them in your mind for a minute, with love, and let that be the whole prayer. Then notice whether, afterwards, you pick up the phone and call them. Many people do. That, too, is an answer.
