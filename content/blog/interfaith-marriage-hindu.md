A friend in Houston told his parents in India about his fiancée on a video call. His mother was silent for a full minute and then asked whether she ate rice. He was hurt at first. He realised later that it was her way of asking how to feed her. That is often how an interfaith marriage begins in a Hindu family: not with theology, but with a mother working out where the new person will sit at the table. Most of what parents feel is fear of loss, and fear can be answered slowly.

## What are Hindu parents really worried about?

When the news lands, parents rarely argue about doctrine. Listen closely and the worries are usually these:

- **Continuity.** Will the lamp still be lit in this house? Will anyone know the prayers after us?
- **Grandchildren.** Will they know their Paati's language, their Thatha's stories, their own birth star?
- **What relatives will say.** This one is rarely spoken aloud and often the loudest.
- **Food and festivals.** Will there be Deepavali? Will there be a place for them in December, or at Easter?

It helps to name these fears calmly rather than argue them away. "You are worried the children won't know Pongal" is a sentence a parent can hear. "You are being old-fashioned" is not. When I have seen families come through this well, it was usually because someone, often the son or daughter, stopped defending and started listening.

It also helps to remember that the partner's family may have the same fears, in a different vocabulary.

## The wedding itself

Many Hindu priests, especially in America, will conduct a ceremony with a non-Hindu partner. Some ask for a short conversation first to explain the rituals. Some families hold a blended ceremony, with a Hindu rite and a church or civil blessing in one day. Others hold two ceremonies, a week or a month apart, one for each side.

The centre of a Hindu wedding is the saptapadi, the seven steps the couple take together around the sacred fire, each step a promise: to nourish each other, to grow strong together, to share joy and sorrow, to be friends. Read the meanings of the [seven steps of the saptapadi](/blog/saptapadi-seven-steps-meaning) and you will notice that nothing in them requires a particular creed. They are promises anyone can make sincerely.

Guests from the other side who have never attended one will find their bearings in [explaining a Hindu wedding](/blog/explaining-a-hindu-wedding) to friends and family.

## Making room over years, not days

Acceptance rarely arrives at the wedding. It arrives at the third Pongal, when the daughter-in-law remembers to draw the kolam without being asked. Or the fourth Deepavali, when the son-in-law is the one who stays up to help fry the murukku. My friend's wife, the one whose rice-eating was once in question, now sends his mother photographs of her rasam for approval.

There is a line in the Rig Veda that Hindus have quoted for three thousand years: ekam sat vipra bahudha vadanti, truth is one, the wise call it by many names. It is a large idea, and families have been living inside it far longer than they have been arguing about it. Parents who hold that line can also hold a daughter-in-law who prays in another name.

The practical rule is this: invite, do not test. Invite the new spouse to Pongal, to the temple, to the lamp. Do not quiz them on the names of the Navagrahas. Many partners grow into a tradition when they are welcomed into it, and [doing puja with a non-Hindu spouse](/blog/puja-with-a-non-hindu-spouse) is written for exactly that stage. When children come, the questions change again, and I have written about [raising interfaith kids with a Hindu thread](/blog/raising-interfaith-kids-hindu).

## One small thing to carry into the week

On the next video call with your parents, invite your partner to light the lamp with them. Your mother in her prayer room, your partner at your kitchen counter, both flames on the screen. Nobody needs to say a prayer. Let your parents see that the lamp is still being lit in your house, and by whom.
