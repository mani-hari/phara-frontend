The Bhagavad Gita opens with something close to a panic attack. Arjuna's limbs give way. His mouth goes dry. His skin burns, his hair stands on end, and his bow slips from his hand. He says his mind is reeling and he cannot stand. What Krishna offers next, over seventeen more chapters, is less a pep talk than a set of practices. That is why the Bhagavad Gita on anxiety is worth reading closely: it starts where anxiety actually starts, in the body.

## Bhagavad Gita on anxiety: what happens to Arjuna

Read chapter 1, Arjuna Vishada Yoga, the yoga of Arjuna's despair, and look at the list of symptoms. Trembling. A dry mouth. A burning feeling. Weakness in the legs. Confused thinking. Anyone who has had a bad night before a hard conversation will recognise it.

What is striking is that the text takes the body seriously before it offers any philosophy. It does not say Arjuna is weak or faithless. It simply describes what is happening to him, in detail, and lets him collapse onto the seat of the chariot. The teaching begins only after that. The scene itself is told as a story in [why the Gita begins with a breakdown](/blog/arjuna-vishada-yoga-meaning).

For anyone reading this with anxiety of their own: the first thing the Gita does is let the fear be seen. You can do the same.

## What did Krishna tell Arjuna to do?

Krishna's teachings on fear are spread across the text, but three threads stand out.

The first is in chapter 2, Sankhya Yoga. Krishna says that pleasure and pain, heat and cold, come and go; they are not permanent, so learn to bear them steadily. Many readers look for Gita verses for stress and land here, because it names the thing directly: sensations pass. Steadiness is not the absence of discomfort. It is the capacity to stay.

The second is verse 2.47: act, but do not cling to the outcome. A great deal of anxiety is attention racing ahead to results you cannot control. Bringing it back to the action in front of you is the practical centre of the Gita. The steady person Krishna describes at the end of that chapter, [the sthitaprajna](/blog/sthitaprajna-steady-mind), is someone who has made this a habit.

The third is in chapter 6, Dhyana Yoga, the chapter on meditation. Arjuna protests that the mind is restless, turbulent, as hard to hold as the wind. Krishna agrees. He does not argue. He says the mind can be held through abhyasa, steady practice, and vairagya, gentle non-attachment. Practice and letting go, together.

## Three practices to try

Anchor in the body and the breath. Chapter 6 describes the seat: firm, neither too high nor too low, the body, head and neck held upright and still, the gaze steady. You do not need a mountain cave. A chair at the kitchen table works. Sitting like this for a few minutes gives the nervous system something stable to organise around.

Act only on the next step. When the mind throws up a hundred future scenarios, choose one small action you can take now, and take it. Send the email. Make the call. Wash the cup. Action, done fully, pulls attention back into the present.

Offer the outcome. Before a stressful event, say to yourself, in whatever words fit, that you will do your part and let the result go. Some people find writing helps; [likhita japa, writing a mantra by hand](/blog/likhita-japa-writing-mantras), is one way to give an anxious mind something steady to do.

A word of honesty. These are practices, not treatment. If anxiety is heavy, persistent or disrupting your life, please talk to a doctor or a counsellor. The Gita can sit alongside professional help; it does not replace it. For small supports on hard days, try [rituals for anxious days](/blog/rituals-for-anxious-days).

## Five minutes before the phone

Each morning, before your phone, sit for five minutes the way chapter 6 describes. Spine upright, body still, eyes resting on one point or gently closed. Let the breath be whatever it is. When the mind runs, bring it back without scolding.

At the end of the five minutes, name one action for the day, something real and specific. Then, silently, let its result go. Do this for seven mornings and notice whether the day feels different, even slightly. That slight difference is where Arjuna started too.
