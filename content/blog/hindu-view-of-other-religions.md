A line in the Rig Veda says it plainly: ekam sat vipra bahudha vadanti, truth is one, the wise call it by many names. That is the short answer to the Hindu view of other religions. Truth is larger than any single name for it, and so other paths are not automatically wrong. The story of how Hindus have actually lived with that line, across thousands of years and a great many arguments, is more complicated, and more interesting.

## What is the Hindu view of other religions?

Start with the line itself. It comes from a hymn in the first book of the Rig Veda, where the poet lists the names of gods, Indra, Mitra, Varuna, Agni, and then says that these are many names for one reality. The poet is not saying all names are the same. The poet is saying that the reality is the same, and that the names multiply because people, and poets, do.

The tradition then built this into ordinary worship. A Hindu family has an ishta devata, a chosen form of God. One family is devoted to Murugan, the neighbours to Krishna, the aunt to the Goddess. Nobody thinks the others are worshipping a false god. Plurality is not something Hinduism tolerates from outside. It is normal inside the house. The same generosity works inside a single family through the [ishta devata, the chosen deity](/blog/ishta-devata-meaning).

So when a Hindu meets someone praying to Christ, or facing Mecca, or sitting in silence like a Buddhist monk, the instinct many were raised with is recognition, not alarm: another name, another road.

## Many paths, or many mountains?

In the fourth chapter of the Bhagavad Gita, Krishna says: in whatever way people approach me, in that way I receive them; everyone follows my path in every way. It is one of the most quoted lines on religious openness, and it rewards a slow reading. Krishna does not say all paths are identical. He says he receives people along all of them.

In the nineteenth century, Ramakrishna, the mystic of Dakshineswar near Kolkata, took this literally. The story says he practised Islam for a time, and later meditated on Christ, and reported reaching the same experience of the divine. He compared religions to many ghats leading down to the same water. The story is really about a man who trusted his experience more than labels.

The tradition also had a habit of arguing, fiercely and respectfully. Adi Shankara travelled the length of India debating scholars of other schools. The debates had rules: state the other side's view fairly before you refute it. The tradition had a name for this, shastrartha, and [debate as devotion](/blog/debate-as-devotion-shastrartha) is not too strong a way to describe it. Debate was a form of taking the other person seriously.

## Where the ideal meets the ground

It would be dishonest to pretend the ideal has always been lived. People are people, and every tradition has had its intolerant moments and its pious snobs.

And respect is not the same as saying everything is identical. A Hindu can honour a Christian friend's faith without pretending that the Trinity and the Upanishads say the same thing. Flattening real differences is its own form of disrespect.

What the tradition asks, at its best, is humility about one's own grasp of truth. If the reality is one and the names are many, then my name is also only a name. That is humbling, and useful. It makes it easier to sit beside a spouse of another faith, or answer a child's question at Christmas. Families living this at home may find [raising interfaith kids with a Hindu thread](/blog/raising-interfaith-kids-hindu) practical; the Vedic root of the idea is in [many paths, one truth](/blog/many-paths-one-truth).

## The size of truth

The story of the Rig Veda poet is really about the size of truth. It is larger than any one hymn, any one temple, any one book. The poet looked at a crowd of gods and did not pick a winner. He looked past them.

Try this. Take one prayer from another tradition, the Lord's Prayer, the opening of the Quran, a Buddhist metta verse, and read it slowly, once, the way you would read a sloka you love. Do not decide what you think. Just notice where it meets you.
