A friend in Ohio says he knows his mother has arrived from India by the smell of camphor at six the next morning. She finds the one east-facing window in the house and turns the shelf beneath it into a shrine before anyone else is up. For parents visiting the USA from India, prayer is not an extra. It is the thread that holds their day together in a strange country, and the kindest thing you can do is make room for it before they land.

## What do visiting parents need for their prayers?

Less than you think, and more than a spare bedroom.

- **A clean corner, facing east if possible.** A shelf, a side table, the top of a low bookcase. Many elders prefer to face east while praying, towards the rising sun. If your house cannot oblige, north is the traditional second choice. The ideas for [setting up a small puja corner in an apartment](/blog/small-puja-corner-apartment) work just as well for a guest room.
- **A lamp that will not trip the smoke alarm.** This is the single most common household crisis. Keep the lamp well away from the detector, use a small wick, and show your mother where the window opens. The basics of [how to light a diya](/blog/how-to-light-a-diya) safely indoors are worth a read before the visit.
- **Their own supplies.** Most parents pack kumkum, vibhuti, sandal paste and a small bottle of sesame oil from home, and they prefer their own. Let them. If something runs out, the Indian grocery is your friend.
- **Their own unhurried morning.** This is the one people forget. Your household runs on school drop-off and a seven-thirty train. Theirs ran on bath, prayer and coffee, in that order, for forty years. Give them the bathroom first, and do not rush them.

## Finding the temple, and the days in between

The nearest temple may be forty minutes away. For parents used to a temple at the end of the street, this is a real loss. One visit a week is realistic for most families: a Saturday morning, or a weekday evening if you can get home early. Some temples run shuttle services for seniors, or have regular groups of visiting parents who meet there. Ask at the office.

The harder problem is the long quiet weekdays. You are at work, the children are at school, the house is silent, and the neighbourhood has no one on the street. My father, on his visits, used to say New Jersey was very clean and very empty.

Help them fill the days. Find out whether the temple has a weekday sloka group or bhajan class. Many parents love chanting the [Vishnu Sahasranamam](/blog/vishnu-sahasranamam-for-beginners) together with others. A walk at the same hour each day, a library with Tamil or Telugu books, a daily call to Chennai at a time that suits both sides. Small structures matter.

## Knees, flights and pace

My father has had heart surgery and a knee operation, and both change what a visit looks like. A temple with forty steps at the entrance is not the same outing it was ten years ago.

- **Choose temples with seating.** Many American temples have chairs along the walls, and nobody minds if an elder sits through the aarti.
- **Go early, before the crowds.** A weekday morning or the first hour on Saturday is calmer and cooler.
- **Do not overpack the itinerary.** Parents who have flown twenty hours do not need to see four temples and two cousins in the first week. One visit, one rest day, one visit.

If they are coming for the first time, the practical notes on [a first visit to a Hindu temple in the US](/blog/first-visit-hindu-temple-usa) may help set their expectations too.

## One small thing to carry into the week

Before they land, set up the prayer corner yourself. A cloth on the shelf, a small lamp, a picture of the family deity, a few flowers from the supermarket. Let the first thing they see, when they walk into their room after that long flight, be a lamp waiting for them. It tells them, before you say a word, that their mornings will be safe in your house.
