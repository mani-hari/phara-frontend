My grandmother in Thanjavur used to say that the Big Temple's shadow never touches the ground. I believed her for years. She said it the way she said most things, firmly, while doing something else with her hands, and it did not occur to me to check. When I finally stood in the courtyard of the Thanjavur Brihadeeswarar temple at noon, as a grown man with a camera, I understood that she had not been telling me a fact. She had been telling me how the place makes you feel.

## What makes the Thanjavur Brihadeeswarar temple so special?

It was built by the Chola king Rajaraja in the early eleventh century, at the height of Chola power, and dedicated to Shiva as Brihadeeswarar, the great lord. Most South Indian temples you know have their tallest tower at the gateway, the gopuram, which grows smaller as you move inward. Here it is the other way round. The towering vimana, the tower over the sanctum, rises directly above the god, and the gateways are modest beside it. The eye goes straight to the centre.

Inside is one of the largest lingams in the country. Outside, facing it, sits a Nandi, Shiva's bull, carved from a single stone. The whole complex is a UNESCO World Heritage site, but it has never stopped being a living temple. Worship goes on every day, as it has since Rajaraja's priests first lit the lamps.

People call it a king's gratitude written in granite. Inscriptions on the walls list what Rajaraja, his sister and his queens gave to the temple, down to the names of the dancers, musicians and lamp-keepers who would serve it. It is less a monument to a king than a very long thank-you letter, with the staff list attached.

## Does its shadow really never fall?

The saying my grandmother repeated is famous across Tamil Nadu: the shadow of the Big Temple's vimana never falls on the ground. It is not literally true. The shadow does fall. But the tower is built in such proportion, broad at the base and tapering steeply, that at midday its shadow mostly falls on the structure itself rather than spreading across the courtyard.

Standing there, I realised she had been right about something else. The temple is so large and so balanced that it seems to hold itself in, to cast nothing unkind on anyone. A grandmother's exaggeration held a truth about proportion. Things built with care take up exactly the space they need.

## What a devotee experiences there

The first thing is the courtyard, wide and open, paved in stone that is warm under bare feet by afternoon. Then the Nandi mandapam, the pavilion where the great bull sits, patient, facing his lord. Then the tower, which makes a person feel small and, at the same time, held, as if the building had been made with you in mind.

If you have time, look at the walls. There are Chola frescoes in the passage around the sanctum, and dancers carved in stone, frozen mid-step in the poses of Bharatanatyam. The dancing Shiva of [the Chidambaram temple](/blog/chidambaram-nataraja-temple), not far away, was the Cholas' family deity, and you can see his rhythm everywhere here.

My Thatha had his cloth shop in Kumbakonam, less than an hour's drive away. He measured every length of cloth twice before cutting, and if a customer hurried him, he would say, "The Cholas did not hurry." I did not know then what he meant. I know now. You can meet him properly in [honest trade and Thatha's shop](/blog/honest-trade-thathas-shop).

## Carrying the Chola way into the week

From afar, a Monday or [pradosham](/blog/pradosham-meaning) abhishekam, a ritual bathing of the lingam, to Shiva in your name carries the same devotion across the ocean. Many families keep Monday as Shiva's day at home too; [the Monday fast for Shiva](/blog/somvar-vrat-monday-fast) explains the custom.

One small thing to carry into this week: finish one task properly, the Chola way. Pick something ordinary, a report, a repair, a letter to a parent. Measure twice. Do not hurry the last part. Then look at it, the way the king must have looked at his tower, and let it take up exactly the space it needs.
