Karma explained in one line: what you do, and why you do it, shapes who you become. It is not a cosmic scoreboard or a punishment handed down for old sins. The clearest account of it comes from a battlefield. In the Bhagavad Gita, the warrior-prince Arjuna stands between two armies, sees his cousins and teachers on the other side, and puts down his bow. Why act at all, he asks, if every action leads to harm? Krishna's answer, spread across eighteen chapters, is the most practical teaching on karma the tradition has.

## Karma explained: action, not punishment

The word karma comes from the Sanskrit root kri, to do. At its simplest it just means action. Over time it came to mean action together with its consequences, and especially the consequences for the one who acts.

Here is the idea underneath. Every action, word and intention leaves an impression on the doer, a groove in the mind called a samskara. Repeat an action and the groove deepens. Get angry often and you become an angry person; it gets easier each time. Practise patience and patience becomes your nature. What is karma in Hinduism, then? Less a judge and more a habit forming. The world responds to the person you are becoming, and you keep becoming that person through what you do.

The Gita says something blunt about this: no one can remain without acting, even for a moment. Breathing is action. Doing nothing is a choice too. So the question is never whether to act. It is how.

## Is karma a scorecard?

The popular version of karma sounds like revenge with a delay. Someone cuts you off in traffic and you mutter that karma will get them. A family suffers a tragedy and a distant relative wonders aloud what they must have done in a past life.

Karma is usually described as a law of consequence. At heart, it is an idea about responsibility for yourself, not a tool for judging others. Is karma punishment? Not in the texts that matter most. Many teachers warn specifically against using karma to explain away someone else's suffering; that turns a teaching about your own conduct into an excuse for cruelty.

The tradition also cares about intention and attachment. Two people can give the same gift, one to be seen and one simply to help. The act looks identical. The groove it leaves is not. This is why the old texts spend so little time on the size of an action and so much on the state of mind behind it. Feeding a hungry stranger, for example, is praised not for the quantity of rice but for the absence of self-importance, which is the point of [annadhanam, feeding as worship](/blog/annadhanam-feeding-as-worship).

## Karma yoga for ordinary people

Krishna's answer to Arjuna is the most famous verse on karma in the Bhagavad Gita, chapter two, verse forty-seven: you have a right to your action, but never to its fruits. Do not let the fruit be your motive, and do not cling to inaction either.

This is karma yoga, the discipline of action. It does not ask you to stop caring about outcomes; a doctor still wants the patient to recover. It asks you to put your whole attention into the doing and loosen your grip on the result. Anyone who has done good work knows the feeling. The best of it happens when you forget to check how it is landing.

Karma yoga is also the part of the tradition that suits people who are unsure about gods and rituals; it asks only that you act well. If that describes you, [spiritual but not religious in Hinduism](/blog/spiritual-but-not-religious-hinduism) shows how much room there is. And karma sits alongside its partner idea, [dharma in everyday life](/blog/dharma-meaning-in-everyday-life), which is about what to do; karma is about how.

## A reframe for the week

People often reach for karma when they want to know whether life is fixed. Astrology raises the same question, and the tradition's answer is the same: the past sets conditions, the present is still yours. [Astrology and free will](/blog/astrology-and-free-will) follows that thread through the old stories, and [is astrology real](/blog/is-astrology-real) takes an honest look at the chart itself.

For one week, choose one ordinary daily task. Washing up, a report, the school run. Do it fully, with your attention on the doing, and then let go of how it lands. Do not check who noticed. At the end of the week, see what has changed, not in the world, but in you. Nobody is keeping score. That is rather the point.
