The Katha Upanishad pictures the body as a chariot, the senses as its horses, the mind as the reins and the self as the one riding. A ritual that carries the word ratha, chariot, in its name is, in one reading, a thank-you to the chariot that has carried a life for seventy years. Bheemaratha Shanti is the Hindu ritual traditionally held around the seventieth birthday: a homam for long life, a bath for the couple, and a family gathered to say that the journey so far has been worth honouring.

## What is Bheemaratha Shanti?

It is one of a sequence of age rituals that some Hindu families, particularly in South India, mark across the later decades of life. The usual order runs like this: Ugraratha Shanti as the sixtieth year begins, Shashtiabdapoorthi at sixty, Bheemaratha Shanti around seventy, and Sathabhishekam past eighty, when a person has seen a thousand full moons.

The exact age for Bheemaratha Shanti varies. Many families hold it around seventy, some as the seventieth year begins and some on its completion; other traditions place it later in the seventies. Communities differ. The family priest or the family's own custom usually decides, and nobody should feel they have got it wrong by following their own elders. If your parents are still approaching sixty, the earlier milestone is described in [planning a shashtiabdapoorthi from abroad](/blog/shashtiabdapoorthi-60th-birthday).

Shanti means peace. In these rituals it has a particular sense: a peace-making at a threshold. The old texts treat certain ages as turning points, when a life moves from one stage to another, and the ritual asks that the crossing be gentle.

## What does the chariot in the name mean?

Explanations of the full name differ. Bheema means great, mighty, even fearsome. Ratha is the chariot. Some say the name recalls the strength needed to have come so far; others connect it with the sun's chariot and the passing of time. The priests themselves do not always agree, and that is fine.

The Katha Upanishad's image, from the same text in which a boy questions Death about [what moksha means](/blog/what-is-moksha), is the one I find most useful. The body carries us. The senses pull it, sometimes well, sometimes in several directions at once. The mind holds the reins, and the self sits in the back, watching. After seventy years the chariot has been through a great deal: births, deaths, moves across oceans, a knee that complains in winter. It is still moving.

The story says the ritual is for the planets, for long life, for peace. The story is really about gratitude for endurance. Seventy years of getting up in the morning deserves a ceremony.

## What happens, and how to join from afar

The ritual follows a familiar shape, shared with the other age rituals.

It begins with a Ganapati puja and the sankalpam, the statement of intention naming the person, their lineage and their birth star. Then comes the Ayushya homam, a fire ritual praying for a long and healthy life; there is more on it in [the Ayushya homam and the star birthday](/blog/ayushya-homam-star-birthday). Many families add Mrityunjaya japa, the repeated chanting of the great mantra to Shiva as the conqueror of death, explained in [the meaning of the Mahamrityunjaya mantra](/blog/mahamrityunjaya-mantra-meaning).

Then the kalasa abhishekam: the couple sit together while water from the sanctified pots is poured over them. Blessings are given and received. The elders bless the children; the children, some now grandparents themselves, bow to the elders.

For children living abroad, there are two ways in. You can fly home, which is best if you can. Or you can sponsor the homam at a temple and join the ritual live, watching the fire in Chennai or Bengaluru at a strange hour of your own night. The practical side of that is covered in [how online puja from the USA works](/blog/online-puja-from-usa-explained). Neither is lesser. The point is that the person turning seventy knows their family is there.

## Whose story gets told?

Every family has its stories: the move to the city, the first job, the flood, the wedding that nearly did not happen. At seventy, a person has a whole shelf of them, and very few get asked for.

So, before the ritual, ask the person turning seventy which story they want told on the day. Then make sure someone tells it, out loud, after the homam and before lunch. The fire honours the chariot. The story honours the rider.
