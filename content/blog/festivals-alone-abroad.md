A friend in Seattle told me that his first Deepavali in America was a tea-light on top of the microwave and a box of sweets from the Indian store, eaten standing up in a studio apartment. He said it was enough, because he did it on purpose. I have thought about that phrase many times. Celebrating festivals alone abroad is rarely about recreating the festival you grew up with. It is about choosing one thing and doing it deliberately.

## Why do festivals feel lonelier abroad?

Because of what is missing, mostly. The noise, first. A festival in Chennai is loud: crackers from four in the morning, the neighbour's radio, the milkman wishing you, the temple loudspeaker. Here, the street is quiet and the neighbours are at work. Then the people: a grandmother directing the kitchen, cousins everywhere, an uncle who arrives unannounced. And the day itself. Deepavali or Pongal falls on a Tuesday, and Tuesday does not stop for you. Your manager still has a meeting at ten, and if you have children, someone has to [ask the school for a day off](/blog/school-day-off-for-diwali).

In the Tiruppavai, Andal goes from door to door before dawn in the month of Margazhi, calling each friend to wake and come to bathe in the river. "Are you still asleep?" she sings, a little teasing. It is a song for a whole street. I used to hum it on dark winter mornings in New Jersey, in a street that did not know it was being called. That is the loneliness in one image: the call goes out, and the street does not answer. I have written about [the month of Margazhi](/blog/margazhi-month-meaning) and the radio that came on before six in our Chennai house.

It is normal to feel homesick during festivals. Many people who are perfectly settled abroad feel it hardest on exactly these days.

## Celebrating festivals alone abroad, kindly

Do not try to recreate the whole day. You will fall short, and falling short will feel worse than doing nothing. Instead:

- **Keep the one ritual that matters most to you.** For some it is the oil bath; for some, the lamp at dusk; for a young man who has had [his sacred thread ceremony](/blog/upanayanam-in-the-usa), perhaps the Gayatri at dawn.
- **Cook one dish.** Not the feast. One thing your mother made, even if it comes out wrong.
- **Wear something new.** Even a new shirt under a work jacket counts.
- **Call home at their morning.** India is ahead, so your evening is their early morning. Catch them while the house is still busy.

If you need lamps, kumkum or a small idol from home, it helps to know what travels well, which is the subject of [flying with puja items](/blog/flying-with-puja-items).

## Finding company

The temple on the weekend, first. Most US temples hold festival celebrations on the nearest Saturday or Sunday, and walking into a hall full of people doing the same thing is a relief. If you want a home puja and do not know anyone, the note on [finding a Hindu priest in the USA](/blog/finding-a-hindu-priest-usa) starts with the temple office.

Then, the coworker who has never seen a diya. Bringing a box of sweets to the office and explaining in two sentences what the day means often turns into a longer conversation than you expect, and [explaining Hindu festivals to coworkers](/blog/explaining-hindu-festivals-to-coworkers) has the two sentences. And then there are the other people also far from home: students, new arrivals, people whose families are in another city or another country.

Our own festival evenings in Edison, with my wife and son, still begin with my mother's lamp on the video call, a small gold point on a phone propped against the sugar tin. It is not the same as being there. It is ours.

## One small thing to carry

Invite one person who is also alone. A student from the temple, a new colleague, a neighbour whose family is overseas. One more plate. A festival shared with a stranger is still a festival, and sometimes it is the one you remember best.
