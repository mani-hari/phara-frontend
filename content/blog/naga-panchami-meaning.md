The Naga Panchami significance is easiest to see in a story. King Janamejaya lit a fire to burn every snake in the world. His father, King Parikshit, had died of a snakebite, and the grief had hardened into a plan. Priests chanted, and serpents fell out of the sky into the flames by the thousand. Then a boy named Astika walked into the sacrifice, praised the king so well that he was granted a wish, and asked for the fire to stop. The Mahabharata tells this near its very beginning, in the Adi Parva, and folk tradition adds that the snakes were saved on a panchami, a fifth lunar day. The story says the snakes were rescued. The story is really about a king learning that grief is not a reason to destroy. That, more than any ritual, is what the day keeps: honouring what we fear instead of killing it.

## Why does Naga Panchami matter?

Naga Panchami falls on the fifth day of the bright half of Shravana, usually in July or August, in the thick of the monsoon. That is not an accident of the calendar. The rains flood burrows and drive snakes into fields, courtyards and paths. A farming household met more snakes in that season than in any other. The festival places a day of reverence exactly where fear would be sharpest.

The name and the day shift across India. In Andhra Pradesh and Telangana many keep Nagula Chavithi, on the fourth day after the Deepavali new moon; in parts of Tamil Nadu and Karnataka there is Naga Chathurthi, a day before panchami. The details differ; the gesture does not. In the Tamil calendar this is also the season of [Aadi Perukku, when the rivers rise](/blog/aadi-perukku-meaning), and water and serpent have always travelled together in the Indian imagination.

## Why are snakes worshipped in Hinduism?

Look at where snakes turn up in the stories. Vishnu sleeps on Shesha, the many-headed serpent whose name means "what remains", the one left over when everything else dissolves. Shesha is time itself, coiled, holding the god who holds the world. Vasuki hangs around Shiva's neck, and in [the churning of the ocean](/blog/samudra-manthan-meaning) Vasuki becomes the rope wound around Mount Mandara, pulled by gods and demons alike. And the child Krishna dances on the hoods of Kaliya, the serpent who poisoned the Yamuna, but does not kill him. He sends him away to the sea.

The snake is many things at once. It lives underground, near hidden water, so it guards the earth's fertility. It sheds its skin, so it knows about renewal. It coils, and yogic traditions borrowed that image for kundalini, the energy imagined sleeping at the base of the spine. And it is dangerous, which is exactly why the stories keep insisting on sparing it. Astrology borrowed the serpent too, in Rahu and Ketu; [kala sarpa dosha](/blog/kala-sarpa-dosha-meaning) is where most people meet that idea today.

## Anthills, stone nagas and milk

On the day, women across South India go early to an anthill, the traditional home of a cobra, or to the nagakal, carved stones of intertwined serpents set under peepal and neem trees near temples. They smear turmeric and kumkum, lay flowers, and pour milk. Some tie a thread; some keep a fast until the offering is done.

Notice that the offering goes to the stone, not to a live snake. The tradition is wiser than it looks. A snake has no use for milk, and handling one for a festival helps nobody. The stone carries the reverence so that the animal can be left alone. Many families now pour only a few symbolic drops, or offer the milk at the stone and take the rest home as prasadam, which is closer to the spirit than a bowl left out in the heat. For many households, [the month of Aadi](/blog/aadi-month-significance) has already set the tone for this season: small offerings, repeated, in a time of water.

## The frightening thing, left alive

Go back through the stories. Janamejaya's fire stops. Kaliya is sent away, not slain. Vasuki is pulled and strained, but the gods need him whole. Shesha is never conquered at all; he is the bed.

In almost every one of these stories, the snake is spared. Perhaps that is the festival's quiet instruction. Leave the frightening thing alive, and learn to live beside it. Most of our serpents are not in the garden. They are the worries that live under the house, and the answer, the story suggests, is rarely a fire.
