The first mottai I remember was a cousin's son at Palani, before dawn, howling in his grandmother's lap while the barber worked quickly. What stayed with me was not the crying. It was the sandalwood paste afterwards, cool and yellow on that small shaved head, and how quickly he stopped. The mundan ceremony, a child's first ritual haircut, is usually over in minutes. Families in the US often ask whether it has to happen at a temple in India or whether home will do. Both are done, and both count.

## Why is the mundan ceremony done?

In the Sanskrit texts it is chudakarana, one of the samskaras, the rites that mark a life from before birth to after death. The overview of [the sixteen samskaras](/blog/sixteen-samskaras-explained) places it among the early childhood rites, after the naming and the first rice. In the north it is called mundan; in Tamil homes it is simply mottai, the shaved head.

My aunt in Chennai puts it plainly: the hair is the first thing a child can give. A baby owns nothing else. For many families it is also a vow being fulfilled. A mother who prayed at a particular temple for a safe birth promises the child's first hair to that deity, and the mottai is the family keeping its word. I find that moving, the idea that a child's first offering is made on behalf of a promise someone made while waiting for them to arrive.

## Temple or home?

Offering hair is a long devotional custom at a few temples in particular. At Palani, the hill temple of Murugan and one of [the six abodes of Murugan](/blog/six-abodes-of-murugan), families climb the steps or take the winch up the hill with freshly shaved children, and the smell of sandalwood is everywhere. At Tirupati, the hall where hair is offered is busy from early morning. Many Tamil families go instead to their kula deivam, the family deity, whose temple may be a small shrine in a village the family left generations ago. The choice is usually made by elders, so ask rather than assume.

Home is also fine, and common. A priest does a short prayer, Ganesha first, then a sankalpam, the statement of intention, in the child's name and star. A barber, or sometimes the maternal uncle, cuts the first locks before the rest is shaved. At a US temple, some priests can arrange this on a weekday, and the note on [finding a Hindu priest in the USA](/blog/finding-a-hindu-priest-usa) covers what to ask; ask the temple office whether they have a space and whether a barber can come. The hair is gathered and later offered at a temple or into flowing water, according to family custom.

## Planning the mundan from the US

Timing varies by family. Many do it in the first or third year; some insist on an odd-numbered year. For families abroad, the practical answer is often a summer trip to India, so the grandparents can hold the child and the family temple can be visited. If you go that way, plan the temple morning early in the trip, while everyone still has the patience of the newly arrived.

In many Tamil homes, the ear-piercing, kaadhu kuthu, is done on the same day, the child sitting on the maternal uncle's lap. That rite has its own customs, set out in [the karnavedha ear-piercing ceremony](/blog/karnavedha-ear-piercing).

Know, too, what the grandparents will expect: new clothes for the child, sweets for whoever is watching, a visit to the sanctum afterwards. If you are doing it in the US instead, tell them honestly, and include them on a video call. My father would rather watch on a phone, holding it too close to his face, than be told about it afterwards. If the mundan is also the child's first time at a temple, the note on [a baby's first temple visit](/blog/babys-first-temple-visit) has ideas for keeping the morning calm.

A word on the crying: it happens, and it passes. Hold the child close, keep the room quiet, and have the sandalwood paste ready. The paste is part of the ritual, and it is also the part the child seems to like best.

## One small thing to keep

Keep a lock of the hair in a small cloth pouch, tucked into a drawer with the birth certificate. It feels a little odd at the time. It is exactly the kind of thing your child will want to see at eleven, when they start asking what they were like before they can remember. My son is about eleven now, and he asks exactly these questions. The answers are easier when you have something to put in his hand.
