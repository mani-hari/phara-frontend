The most honest prayer I have said at our temple in New Jersey was over a pot of sambar for four hundred people, stirring with a paddle the size of an oar. Volunteering at Hindu temples in America is mostly this: unglamorous, useful work done alongside people you would never otherwise meet. You do not need special skills. You need to turn up on time, do what is needed, and go home a little tired. The tradition calls it seva, selfless service, and it is one of the oldest forms of worship there is.

## What does volunteering at Hindu temples look like?

More varied than most people expect. On any festival weekend, a temple here runs on volunteers:

- **The kitchen and the annadanam line**, where food is cooked and served free to everyone who comes. [Annadanam as a form of worship](/blog/annadhanam-feeding-as-worship) explains why feeding people sits so close to the centre of temple life.
- **Parking and shoe racks.** Someone has to wave cars into a field and keep a thousand pairs of sandals in order.
- **Cleaning brass lamps**, a job for a quiet Saturday with tamarind and a scrubbing pad. The method is the same as at home; see [cleaning brass puja items](/blog/cleaning-brass-puja-items).
- **Stringing flowers** for the deities' garlands, usually done by a group of women who have been doing it together for years and who will teach you if you ask.
- **Teaching Sunday classes**, where children learn slokas and stories. Our son spent years in [Bala Vihar, the temple Sunday school](/blog/bala-vihar-temple-sunday-school), and the teachers were all volunteers.
- **Helping elders** to the shrine, finding them a chair, walking them to the canteen.

None of this looks like prayer from the outside. From the inside, it often feels more like prayer than standing in front of the sanctum.

## How do you start?

Ask at the temple office. Most temples keep a volunteer list or a sign-up sheet for festivals, and the person at the desk will be glad you asked. Sign up for one festival shift, not five.

Then turn up on time. It sounds obvious, but a volunteer who arrives when promised is worth three who arrive when convenient. Do what is needed rather than what is visible. The person who carries the rubbish bags out at ten at night is doing seva as surely as the one garlanding the deity. [What seva really means](/blog/seva-meaning) says it at length; the short version is this: the work is the offering.

Bring your son or daughter. Children can hand out prasadam, fold programmes, carry water to the kitchen. Our son's first job was handing out laddoos at the exit, and he took it more seriously than anything he did at school that week.

## What if you only have two hours a month?

Then give two hours a month. Temples need regular small help more than occasional heroics. Some roles fit into a busy life:

- The flower-garland team that meets once a month.
- The monthly lamp-cleaning group.
- Driving an elder who no longer drives to the big festival, and bringing them home.

Consistency matters more than size. The aunty who has strung the same garland every first Friday for fifteen years is a pillar of that temple.

And say no without guilt. When a festival committee asks for more than you can give, it is all right to decline. Seva that breeds resentment at home is not much of an offering. Two cheerful hours are better than ten grudging ones. When the big festival comes around, the [Brahmotsavam and other temple festivals in America](/blog/temple-festivals-in-america) will need hands, and you can choose one evening.

## One small thing to carry into the month

Pick one shift at your temple's next festival, and do it without telling anyone. Not your colleagues, not a group chat, not your mother on the next video call. Just go, stir the pot or park the cars, and come home.

Then notice how the temple feels the week after. For me, it stopped being a place I visited and became a place I belonged to. The sambar had something to do with it.
