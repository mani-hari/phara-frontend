The first time I brought a box of kaju katli, the diamond-shaped cashew sweet, to a stand-up meeting, three people asked what Diwali was, and one asked if she could light a lamp too. That is most of what you need to know about Diwali at work. People are curious, not suspicious. A box of sweets and one warm sentence, it is the festival of lights and it celebrates light over darkness, will do more than any presentation. The rest is planning: telling your manager early, and protecting one evening for the lamp at home.

## How do you explain Diwali at work in one minute?

I used to over-explain. A colleague would ask a simple question and I would start with the Ramayana, detour through regional calendars and end somewhere near the five days of the festival, by which time the coffee had gone cold. Now I say something like this: Diwali is the Hindu festival of lights. Families clean their homes, light small oil lamps, pray for a good year and share sweets. It celebrates light winning over darkness. It feels a little like New Year and Thanksgiving at once.

That is enough. If someone wants more, they will ask, and then you can tell them one story, perhaps Rama coming home to Ayodhya after fourteen years, with the whole city lighting lamps to welcome him. There is no theology exam at the water cooler. If you want a wider set of one-line answers for other festivals, I have collected some for [explaining Hindu festivals to coworkers](/blog/explaining-hindu-festivals-to-coworkers), and the stories in [explaining Diwali to kids](/blog/explaining-diwali-to-kids) work just as well for adults.

## What to bring and share

Sweets are the custom, and they are also the easiest bridge. Choose ones that travel and keep: kaju katli, besan ladoo made from roasted gram flour, soan papdi, a few dry fruit bites. Put a small card on the box listing ingredients, because many sweets carry nuts, milk and ghee, the clarified butter used in so much festival cooking. A colleague with an allergy should not have to guess.

If your office allows it, a single diya, the small clay lamp of the festival, on your desk for the day is lovely. Many workplaces do not allow open flames, so a battery tea light in a clay holder gives the same warmth without troubling the facilities team. Some teams like a short shared moment, five minutes in the kitchen with the sweets and a word of explanation. Offer it; do not insist on it. The Katha Upanishad says the Self is not reached by much talking. Neither, I have found, is a colleague.

## Asking for time off or a lighter day

Treat Diwali the way your colleagues treat their own major holidays. You would not apologise for asking for Christmas Day, so do not apologise for this. The practical difference is that the date moves each autumn, because the festival follows the new moon. Check the panchangam, the traditional almanac, as soon as the season approaches, and tell your manager weeks ahead. It also helps to know [why the Diwali date moves in the USA](/blog/when-is-diwali-in-the-usa), since the US evening can land a day apart from India's.

If a full day off is not possible, ask for a lighter one. No late meetings, leave on time, home by dusk. The evening is what matters: a lamp, a short prayer, a few quiet minutes. There is a separate piece on [Lakshmi puja at home when you live abroad](/blog/lakshmi-puja-at-home-abroad), and all of it fits after an ordinary working day.

One more thing I learned the slow way: if your team spans India and the US, remember that your Indian colleagues will likely be off on the day itself. Plan handovers before, not during.

## One small thing to carry into the week

Last autumn, a colleague I sit near asked if she could come to our home on the Saturday after Diwali. She brought her two children, who drew a lopsided rangoli on our front step and would not stop talking about it. That rangoli did more for the festival in our office than any sweet box ever did.

So this is the one thing: invite one colleague home. Not the whole team, just one person who was curious. A festival grows when someone outside the family sees it from the inside, lamp in hand.
