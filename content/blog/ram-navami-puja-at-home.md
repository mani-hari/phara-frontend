Rama is said to have been born at midday. That one detail makes Ram Navami puja at home unusually easy to keep, even on a working day: it belongs to the lunch hour. Twenty minutes, a lamp, a picture of Rama and a glass of panakam, and you have done what millions of households do at the same hour. You do not need a priest, a day off or a large altar. You need to be present at noon.

## Why is Ram Navami celebrated at noon?

The Valmiki Ramayana, in its first book, the Bala Kanda, places Rama's birth on the ninth day of the bright fortnight of Chaitra, *navami*, when the moon was in the star Punarvasu. Tradition adds that it was midday, the hour when the sun stands highest. So the heart of the festival is the *madhyahna*, the midday window, roughly the two or three hours around local noon. That is when the main puja is done in temples and homes. Some families fast until then and break the fast with the offering.

Ram Navami usually falls in March or April, a week or so after the lunar new year that the Deccan celebrates as Ugadi; there is a lovely piece on [Ugadi's significance and its bitter first taste](/blog/ugadi-significance). For the exact day, check the current year's panchangam. Noon means your local noon. If you are in California, you do not need to match India's clock.

Why noon? Try noticing it for yourself. Midday is the peak of the day's energy, when the body is most alert and the light is least forgiving. A ritual placed there asks you to stop at the busiest point, not the quietest. That is a different discipline from a dawn practice, and in some ways a harder one.

## Ram Navami puja at home in six steps

Keep it simple. This is a Ram Navami puja vidhi, a method, that fits in a lunch break.

1. Clean the space. Wipe the shelf or table where you will set up. Wash your hands and face. Take one breath at the threshold before you begin.
2. Set up the image. A picture of Rama with Sita, Lakshmana and Hanuman, together as the family is usually shown, on a clean cloth.
3. Light the lamp and offer flowers. A ghee or sesame oil lamp, and any fresh flowers you have. Yellow and white are traditional, but whatever is in the house is fine.
4. Say a short sankalpam, the spoken statement of intention. Something like: "On this Ram Navami, I offer this puja with gratitude." Your own words are enough.
5. Offer tulsi leaves one by one. With each leaf, chant *Sri Rama Jayam*, victory to Rama. Eleven or twenty-one leaves works well. If you have no tulsi, offer the chant alone.
6. Aarti. Wave the lamp in slow circles before the image, then bring your palms over the flame and touch them to your eyes.

That is it. The whole thing takes fifteen to twenty minutes.

## Panakam, neer mor and a reading

The offerings for Ram Navami are summer drinks, because it falls in the heat. *Panakam* is jaggery dissolved in water with dry ginger, cardamom and a squeeze of lemon. *Neer mor* is thin spiced buttermilk with curry leaves, ginger and green chilli. *Kosambari*, a salad of soaked moong dal, cucumber and grated carrot, often sits beside them. The full method is in the guide to [panakam and neer mor](/blog/panakam-and-neer-mor). Offer them first, then drink them. Notice how the body responds to something cool and sweet in the heat of noon.

If you have ten more minutes, read aloud. The birth chapter of the Bala Kanda, or a few verses of the Ram Raksha Stotra, a protective hymn to Rama. Reading aloud is different from reading silently; the breath has to slow down to carry the words.

If colleagues ask why you stepped away at noon, the honest answer is short: "It is a festival for Rama, who was born at midday, so I did a small prayer at lunch." ## Rama's name at noon

After the festival, keep one piece of it. For seven days, at noon, wherever you are, at your desk, in the car, in a queue, chant Rama's name eleven times under your breath. Eleven names fit inside half a minute. Then notice what happens to your afternoon. Many people find the post-lunch slump arrives more gently. You may find something else. Either way, you will have given the busiest hour of your day a small still point.
