The first thing many Telugu and Kannada families taste on the new year is a spoonful of neem flower, bitter enough to make a child wince. Ugadi significance starts there, on the tongue. The story says Brahma began creation on this morning; the pachadi, the sweet-sour-bitter relish eaten before anything else, says the world he made will not be all sweet, and asks you to accept it anyway.

## Ugadi significance: the day time begins again

Ugadi is the lunar new year for much of the Deccan: Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Karnataka. It falls on Chaitra Shukla Pratipada, the first day of the bright fortnight in the month of Chaitra, which usually lands in March or April. The name is usually read as *yuga* plus *adi*, the beginning of an age. In Maharashtra the same morning is Gudi Padwa, when a cloth-wrapped pole topped with an upturned pot is raised at the doorway, and Sindhi families keep Cheti Chand at almost the same moment. One season, several names.

Why is Ugadi celebrated on this particular day? The tradition, preserved in retellings attributed to the Brahma Purana, holds that this was the day Brahma began the work of creation. Before it, there was no time to measure. On it, the clock started. Families do what you might expect for such a morning: an oil bath before dawn, mango leaves strung over the door, fresh clothes, a kolam or rangoli at the threshold, a visit to the temple.

The story says a god began making the world. The story is really about starting again. Every culture needs a morning when the ledger is wiped and the calendar feels new. Ugadi puts that morning at the edge of spring, when the neem trees flower and the first raw mangoes appear, so the season itself seems to agree.

## Why does the new year start with something bitter?

Here is the part I find most interesting. On a morning of celebration, the first food is not a sweet. It is the Ugadi pachadi: neem flowers for bitterness, jaggery for sweetness, tamarind for sourness, raw mango for tang, salt, and pepper or green chilli for heat. Six tastes in one spoon. There is a full guide to [making Ugadi pachadi with its six tastes](/blog/ugadi-pachadi-six-tastes) if you want to try it.

Grandmothers explain it simply: life will bring all of these, so taste them all today. The symbol goes further. You are not being asked to hope the year will be sweet. You are being asked to agree in advance to the whole year, the bitter parts included. It is a small contract signed with the tongue. Nobody pretends the neem is pleasant. They eat it anyway, in company, and then they eat the sweets.

This is a very different idea of a new year from resolutions and fireworks. It does not promise improvement. It promises presence. Whatever comes, you have already tasted it once, on a good morning, with your family around you.

## Listening to the year: the panchangam reading

In the afternoon or evening comes *panchanga sravanam*, the listening to the almanac. A priest or an elder reads out the forecast for the new year from the panchangam, the traditional Hindu calendar: the rains, the harvests, which planets rule, what the year holds for each rasi. In temples a crowd gathers; at home the family sits together while someone reads.

The predictions are the least of it. What matters is the listening. The family sits still, together, and hears the year described before it arrives. It is a way of saying: we will face this together, whatever the book says. Neighbouring traditions do something similar in their own way; the Tamil [Puthandu, kept far from home in the USA](/blog/puthandu-tamil-new-year-usa), and Kerala's [Vishu kani, the first sight of the new year](/blog/vishu-kani-meaning), both fall a few weeks later by the sun's calendar rather than the moon's.

When is Ugadi celebrated in any given year? Because it follows the moon, the date moves; check the current year's panchangam, or a [Hindu festival calendar for the USA](/blog/hindu-festival-calendar-usa). A few weeks later comes [Akshaya Tritiya, the day of what never runs out](/blog/akshaya-tritiya-significance), another good day for beginnings.

## Eat the bitter part first

If you keep Ugadi this spring, try this. When the pachadi is served, find the neem flower and eat that part first, slowly, without rushing to the jaggery. Then name one thing you are willing to receive in the coming months, even if it does not taste good: a hard conversation, a slower season, a lesson you did not ask for. The story says the world began today. It began, apparently, with a little bitterness. Perhaps that is why it has lasted.
