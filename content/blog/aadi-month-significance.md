In the Tamil month of Aadi, loudspeakers at the neighbourhood Amman temples begin playing devotional songs before dawn, and women carry pots of *koozh*, a thin ragi porridge, through the streets to offer the Goddess. It is also the month in which families will not fix a wedding, move house or open a shop. Aadi month significance lives in that contrast. The story of why tells you what the month is for: it belongs to the Mother, not to us.

## What gives the Aadi month its significance?

Aadi is the fourth month of the Tamil calendar, running roughly from mid-July to mid-August, when the sun moves into Karka, the sign of Cancer. That movement marks the start of *Dakshinayana*, the sun's southward half of the year, which the tradition describes as the night of the gods, a season turned inward. It is also the month of the strong monsoon wind in Tamil Nadu, the *Aadi kaatru*, which a proverb says is strong enough to blow a grinding stone away.

Into this season the tradition sets the Goddess. Aadi is said to be the month when Amman, the Mother, comes closest to the village. The small shrines to Mariamman, Kaliamman and the village goddesses, often quiet the rest of the year, become the centre of life. Neem leaves are hung at doorways. Pongal is cooked in the temple courtyard. The month is loud, crowded and fervent, in a very particular way: it is the people's festival, more than the priests'.

## Why do families avoid weddings in Aadi?

Why is Aadi month not auspicious for marriage? Ask five families and you may get five answers. Most of them are true in their own way.

The practical answer comes first. Aadi is the planting season in the Kaveri delta and much of Tamil Nadu. The first rains have come, the fields must be sown, and nobody in a farming village has time or money for a wedding in the middle of it. Aadi Perukku, when the river rises, is the day of sowing; there is a lovely piece on [Aadi Perukku and thanking the river](/blog/aadi-perukku-meaning).

Then there is the family custom. In many Tamil households, a bride married in the months before is brought back to her mother's house for Aadi and kept there for the month. The couple is separated, gently, for a season. Some say this was so that no child would be conceived to be born in the fierce heat of Chithirai. Others simply say it gave a young woman a month at home.

And then the symbolic answer. This month's devotion belongs to the Goddess. It is her season, not the season of household beginnings. Weddings, housewarmings and new ventures wait, not because Aadi is bad, but because it is already spoken for. Explanations vary from family to family, and it is worth asking your own elders what theirs was.

## Fridays, Pooram, Amavasya and Perukku

Aadi is dense with sacred days.

Every Friday of the month is *Aadi Velli*, when women light lamps for Amman and Lakshmi, often with turmeric, kumkum and lime-garland offerings. Those Friday lamps flow directly into the great Friday observance of Varalakshmi, which often falls in or just after Aadi; there is a gentle guide to [Varalakshmi Vratham at home](/blog/varalakshmi-vratham-at-home), and another on [Lakshmi puja when you live abroad](/blog/lakshmi-puja-at-home-abroad).

*Aadi Pooram* is the day of the Pooram star in Aadi, remembered as the birth star of Andal, the woman saint who sang herself into marriage with Vishnu; the story of [Andal and what she means](/blog/andal-story-meaning) is one of the loveliest in Tamil devotion. On this day, temples perform a *valaikappu* for the Goddess, adorning her with glass bangles as if she were an expectant mother, and the bangles are given away to women afterwards.

*Aadi Amavasya*, the new moon, is one of the three great days for remembering the ancestors, when crowds gather at the sea at Rameswaram and on riverbanks; there is more on [Amavasya and the ancestors](/blog/amavasya-and-ancestors). And *Aadi Perukku*, on the eighteenth day of the month, is the thanksgiving to the river.

## A month turned toward the Mother

The story says Aadi is inauspicious for our own plans. The story is really about attention. For one month, the calendar sets aside our weddings, our housewarmings and our ambitions, and turns us toward the Mother, the rain and the ancestors. It is a month of receiving rather than beginning.

So if you keep one thing from Aadi, keep one Friday lamp for her. No elaborate puja. A lamp, a flower, a few minutes. Your plans can wait a month. They always could.
