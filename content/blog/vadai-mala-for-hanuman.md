A garland is usually made of flowers. For Hanuman in Tamil temples, it is often made of vadas, dozens of them, threaded like beads and hung from his shoulders to his knees. The question is why a god gets a garland you can eat. The answer behind the vadai mala for Hanuman is a folk story, told differently in different places, and like most good folk stories it is about something other than what it seems.

## Why a vadai mala for Hanuman?

One popular telling goes like this. When Hanuman was a child, he woke up hungry and saw the rising sun. It looked like a ripe fruit, so he leapt into the sky to eat it. But someone else was also chasing the sun that day: Rahu, the shadow planet who, in the old stories, swallows the sun and moon to cause eclipses.

The child and the shadow met in the sky. Rahu, the story goes, was no match for Hanuman, and he came away humbled. He is said to have made a promise: those who offer urad dal, the black gram that tradition links to Rahu, to Hanuman would be spared Rahu's trouble.

This is a folk story, not a scene from a single scripture. The leap at the sun is old and widely told; the promise about urad dal belongs to temple and household tradition, and you will hear it with variations from one town to the next.

## What is the story really about?

The story says Rahu made a promise. The story is really about a fear turned into food.

In Jyotish, the tradition of Indian astrology, Rahu is the planet people worry about. He is associated with confusion, obsession and sudden reversals. And here is what the tradition does with that worry: it takes the grain linked to Rahu, soaks it, grinds it, salts it, fries it until it is crisp and golden, and hangs it around the neck of the one being who was never afraid of anything. The shadow becomes an ornament.

That is a clever move. You do not banish the fear. You cook it and offer it to courage.

In north India the same devotion takes a different shape. There, Hanuman is garlanded with jalebis or laddus. The ingredient changes; the instinct does not. You give the strong one something sweet, or something salty, and in the giving you borrow a little of his steadiness.

## How to make the vadas and thread the mala

**Ingredients (about 54 small vadas)**

- 200 g whole or split urad dal (1 cup), soaked for two hours
- 1 teaspoon salt
- 1 teaspoon coarsely crushed black pepper
- Oil for deep frying
- Clean cotton thread and a large needle

**Method**

1. Drain the dal and grind it with very little water to a thick, stiff batter. It must hold its shape.
2. Stir in salt and pepper. No onion, since the vadas are an offering.
3. Wet your fingers, take a small portion and flatten it on a banana leaf or oiled plastic into a thin ring with a hole in the middle.
4. Fry on medium heat until deep golden and crisp all the way through.
5. Drain and cool completely so the vadas harden and hold on the thread.
6. Thread them on cotton string in the count your family uses: 54 and 108 are common, and temples often go far beyond.

The mala is offered on Saturdays, and on Hanuman Jayanthi. In Tamil Nadu that falls in the month of Margazhi, on the new moon day, traditionally linked with the Moola star, and [the significance of Hanuman Jayanti](/blog/hanuman-jayanti-significance) explains the day itself. After the pooja, the vadas are shared as prasadam, and [prasad and gratitude](/blog/prasad-and-gratitude) explains why that part matters as much as the offering.

## Eleven vadas and a line of Rama's name

A full mala is a lot of frying. If it is too much, fry eleven small vadas, thread them on a string, and offer them on a Saturday evening with [the Hanuman Chalisa](/blog/hanuman-chalisa-on-saturdays), or even a single line of Rama's name. Hanuman, the stories agree, was never impressed by size. He tore open his own chest to show Rama sitting there. Eleven vadas is plenty.
