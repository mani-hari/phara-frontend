At my father's Mrityunjaya homam, a fire ritual for health and long life, arranged the week before his heart surgery, I sat on a mat in the temple hall in Madurai and watched the priest pour ghee into the fire. One spoon, a mantra, "svaha", and it was gone. Another spoon. Gone. Somewhere around the fortieth offering I realised the ritual was teaching me something I badly needed: how to let go of what I could not hold. People ask about homam benefits, and the stated ones are real to many families: health, protection, a good start. But the benefit I carried out of that hall was quieter. It was release.

## What are the benefits of a homam, really?

Every homam begins with a stated intention. The sankalpam, the spoken statement of purpose, names the person and what the family is praying for. A Mrityunjaya homam for health and long life. A Ganapathy homam for a new beginning. A Sudarshana homam for protection when things feel threatening; [the meaning of the Sudarshana homam](/blog/sudarshana-homam-meaning) explains why that one is chosen. If you are new to all this, [what a homam is](/blog/what-is-a-homam) lays out the steps.

Those are the benefits of attending a homam as tradition names them. Families trust them, and I do not want to diminish that.

But ask anyone who has sat through one, and they will usually mention something else. The heat on the face. The drone of the chant. The way an anxious family, who have been talking over each other for days, falls silent together for an hour. My mother, who had not slept properly in a week, dozed off against a pillar halfway through. The priest did not seem to mind. I think he had seen it many times.

## Why do offerings go into the fire?

There is a phrase priests often say after an offering: "idam na mama". It means "this is not mine".

I had heard it all my life without listening. That morning, I listened. The ghee is given, and it is not mine anymore. The rice is given, and it is not mine anymore. You do not get it back. You do not get to supervise what the fire does with it. The word svaha marks the moment the gift is complete.

That is the spiritual meaning of the homam fire, at least as I understood it that day. The offerings are not bargaining chips. They are practice. Every spoonful is a small rehearsal of the one thing we find hardest to do: hand something over and walk away.

My father's heart was not mine to fix. It belonged to him, and to the surgeon, and to whatever larger order holds all of us. I was not going to stop worrying. But for the first time that week, I could see the worry for what it was: something I kept trying to hold that was not mine to hold.

## Carrying the fire into an ordinary week

The Bhagavad Gita, in its ninth chapter, has Krishna say: whatever you do, whatever you eat, whatever you offer or give, do it as an offering to me. I used to read that as a demand for more religion. Now I read it as an invitation to treat ordinary acts like spoons of ghee: do them fully, then let them go.

It helps in hospital corridors, where the outcome is out of your hands. It helps when a job hangs in the balance and the decision is someone else's. It helps when a child leaves for college in another city and stops calling as often as you would like. You do your part. Then you say, quietly, "not mine".

A homam is one way of practising that. You can arrange one at a temple and watch from abroad, as friends who [book pujas for their parents in India](/blog/booking-a-puja-for-parents) often do. You can sit through one in person. Either way, the fire does the teaching. And if you are wondering how a homam differs from the lamp in your own puja corner, [homam vs puja](/blog/homam-vs-puja) explains it well.

## One small thing for the week

When something is out of your hands this week, a test result, an email you are waiting on, a decision someone else will make, pause for a breath. Then say, quietly, "idam na mama. Not mine." See whether it loosens its grip, even a little.

My father came through his surgery, thanks to a very good team of doctors. I still say the phrase, most weeks, about something.
