When the pooja is in twenty minutes and nothing is cooked, rava kesari is the answer. Semolina, ghee, sugar, water and your full attention for one short stretch. A rava kesari naivedyam, the sweet offered to the deity before anyone eats, is proof that an offering does not need a whole morning. It needs a small pan, a few pantry staples, and you standing at the stove without drifting away.

## Rava kesari naivedyam, step by step

The ratio to remember is roughly one part rava, two and a half parts water, one part sugar, by volume.

**Ingredients (serves four)**

- 100 g fine rava, also called sooji or semolina (½ cup)
- 300 ml water (1¼ cups)
- 100 g sugar (½ cup)
- 4 tablespoons ghee, used in stages
- 10 cashews and a few raisins
- A generous pinch of saffron
- 3 cardamom pods, powdered

**Method**

1. Soak the saffron in two tablespoons of warm water. It gives the kesari its colour, so there is no need for food colouring.
2. Warm a tablespoon of ghee, fry the cashews golden and the raisins until they puff, and lift them out.
3. In the same pan, add another spoon of ghee and roast the rava on low heat, stirring, until it smells fragrant and toasty. It should stay pale, not brown. This takes about five minutes.
4. Meanwhile, bring the water to a boil with the saffron water.
5. Lower the heat and pour the boiling water into the rava carefully, stirring constantly. It will splutter and swell quickly.
6. When the rava has absorbed the water, add the sugar. It will melt and loosen the mixture again. Keep stirring.
7. Add the remaining ghee a spoon at a time, stirring until the kesari leaves the sides of the pan.
8. Stir in cardamom, cashews and raisins.

To serve it the temple way, press it into a small ghee-lined bowl, then turn it out onto the plate as a neat dome.

## When is kesari offered?

Kesari is a favourite for Ganesha. Many families make it on Vinayaka Chaturthi and on Sankatahara Chaturthi, the monthly fourth day after the full moon, and it makes a natural sweet for ending [the Sankatahara Chaturthi fast](/blog/sankatahara-chaturthi-fast) after moonrise. It is common at Friday lamps, and in many homes the same dish, called sheera, is the prasadam of a Satyanarayana pooja, the worship of Vishnu as the lord of truth.

It also turns up on Tuesdays in homes that keep the day for Murugan or Durga, a custom explained in [Tuesday worship of Murugan and Durga](/blog/tuesday-worship-murugan-durga). And it appears on any day the pooja is short on time, which, for most people with a job, is most days.

## Why does twenty minutes count as an offering?

People sometimes feel that a quick sweet is somehow a lesser offering than something that took all morning. The tradition does not really support that. What it asks for is attention, not elaborateness.

Try this: for these twenty minutes, stand at the stove and stay there. Do not taste. Watch the rava change colour. Listen for the splutter when the water goes in. Keep the first spoon aside for the lamp before anything else happens. You will notice that twenty minutes of complete attention feel longer, and fuller, than an hour of cooking while you answer messages.

That is the principle behind every offering, from a single spoon of kesari to a [nei appam made for pooja](/blog/nei-appam-for-pooja) or a [vadai mala strung for Hanuman](/blog/vadai-mala-for-hanuman). And once the offering is made, the second half of the practice is giving it out, which is [why prasadam is shared](/blog/why-prasadam-is-shared).

## A jar of roasted rava in the pantry

Here is a small habit that removes the last excuse. Most Indian stores in the US stock both fine and coarse sooji; pick the fine one. Roast a larger batch of rava in a spoon of ghee on the weekend, cool it completely, and keep it in a glass jar. On a weeknight when the evening runs late, you are already halfway there.

Next time you come home at eight on a school night and the lamp is still unlit, make kesari anyway. Offer it warm, sit for a minute, and then share it with whoever is home.
