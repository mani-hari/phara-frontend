Here is how to light a diya at home: place a cotton wick in oil or ghee, let it soak for a minute, light the tip, and set the lamp on a stable plate away from anything that can catch. That takes ten seconds. Lighting it with attention changes the next ten minutes. Below is how to do both, with the practical details that nobody writes down because everyone's grandmother just knew.

## How to light a diya: what you need

A lamp. Brass is the most common in South Indian homes, and a traditional kuthuvilakku, the tall standing lamp, is lovely if you have room. Silver is used in some families for the altar. Clay diyas are simple, easy to replace and traditional for festivals. Any of these is fine. Choose one with a wide, heavy base.

Fuel. Sesame oil is the everyday choice in many homes; ghee is often kept for special days. Coconut oil and mustard oil are common in other regions. The differences, and what tradition says about each, are covered in [ghee lamp or oil lamp](/blog/ghee-lamp-or-oil-lamp).

Wicks. Cotton, either bought ready-made or rolled by hand. How to make cotton wicks for a diya: take a small tuft of clean cotton and roll it between your palms, or along your thigh as older women do, until it forms a thin, tight strand, a little thicker at one end. Soak it in oil or ghee before placing it so it lights evenly.

A plate. A small brass or steel plate under the lamp catches drips and makes it harder to knock over. Add a few grains of rice or a flower on the plate if you like.

## Which direction should the diya face, and how many wicks?

Which direction should a diya face? Many traditions favour facing the flame east, toward the rising sun, or north, which is associated with prosperity. South is often avoided for daily lamps because it is linked with ancestors and is used for rites connected to them. West is treated differently by different families. These are customs, not laws, and practices vary between regions.

How many wicks in a diya? Customs differ here too. Some families light a single wick every day. Others insist on two wicks twisted together or placed side by side, and some avoid a single wick for worship altogether. On festival days, a five-faced lamp may be lit with a wick in each face. If your family has a custom, follow it. If it does not, two wicks side by side is a common, simple choice.

The honest answer to most of these questions is: ask your elders what your household does. The variety is not a problem to be solved. It is what a living tradition looks like.

## Lighting it with attention

Now the part that matters most. Before you strike the match, stop. Take one full breath. Notice your hand holding the matchbox.

Strike it. Bring the flame to the wick and hold it there until the wick catches properly. Then put the match down and watch. The flame will flicker, stretch, shrink and then find its shape. That settling takes a few seconds. Let your eyes stay with it until it is steady. Many families say a short prayer at this point; others simply fold their hands. [Why Hindus light lamps](/blog/why-do-hindus-light-lamps) explains what the flame is traditionally understood to mean.

You will notice something in the body while you do this. The eyes rest. The breath slows slightly. A steady flame is one of the few things the eye can hold without being tugged elsewhere, which is part of why the lamp sits at the centre of so much practice.

Safety in apartments deserves its own line. Keep lamps away from curtains, paper and hanging decorations. Do not place them directly under a smoke detector. Never leave a lamp burning in an empty room or while you sleep. A tray of water or sand under the plate is a sensible extra for small spaces; [a small puja corner in an apartment](/blog/small-puja-corner-apartment) has more ideas. Oil lamps also play a part in festival mornings, such as the pre-dawn ritual described in [the Naraka Chaturdashi oil bath](/blog/naraka-chaturdashi-oil-bath).

## Try this tonight

Tonight, roll one wick by hand. It will be uneven; that is fine. Soak it, place it, and light it slowly. Watch the flame for three full breaths before you walk away. If you want to make it a daily habit, the [dusk lamp ritual](/blog/the-dusk-lamp-ritual) shows how to fit it into two minutes each evening.
