Every November our tulsi comes indoors from the balcony in New Jersey and sits by the kitchen window, a little sulky about the cold. One evening that month, my wife dresses it for a wedding. Tulsi vivah is exactly that: the ceremonial marriage of the tulsi plant, the holy basil, to Vishnu. It is one of the oddest and loveliest customs in the Hindu year, a real wedding with a plant as the bride, and in many regions it opens the season of human weddings too. My son, when he was younger, wanted to know who paid for the catering.

## What is Tulsi vivah?

Tulsi vivah is kept in the bright half of the month of Kartika, usually November, at any point from Prabodhini Ekadashi, when Vishnu is said to wake from his four-month sleep, to the full moon; the full moon itself is [Kartik Purnima, the night of lamps](/blog/kartik-purnima-significance). The groom is Vishnu, as Krishna, or in the form of a shaligram, a smooth black fossil stone from the Gandaki river that is worshipped as Vishnu himself.

South Indian families keep a close cousin on Ksheerabdi Dwadashi, the day after the Ekadashi, when a branch of amla, the Indian gooseberry, is set beside the tulsi to stand for Vishnu. My mother in Chennai does it this way, and on the video call I can see the little amla branch leaning against the pot.

In many parts of India, no family weddings are held until the tulsi has been married. She goes first.

## Who was Vrinda?

Versions of the story appear in the Puranas, commonly the Shiva Purana and the Padma Purana, and in many folk tellings, and the details differ between them. Vrinda was the devoted wife of Jalandhara, a powerful demon king. Her faithfulness was so complete that it made her husband invincible; while she stayed true to him, no one could kill him. Jalandhara grew arrogant and made war on the gods, and even Shiva could not defeat him.

So Vishnu took Jalandhara's form and came to Vrinda. She took him for her husband. Her vow was broken, Jalandhara fell in battle, and Vrinda, discovering the deception, cursed Vishnu to become a stone. He became the shaligram. Then, in grief, she gave up her life, and from where she died grew the tulsi plant. Vishnu, moved and ashamed, promised to marry her every year and to accept no offering without a tulsi leaf.

It is not a comfortable story. The god does something wrong, the woman who is wronged is the one who suffers, and the resolution is partial. I have come to like that the tradition tells it at a wedding anyway. Marriages are not always tidy. The ceremony does not pretend otherwise; it honours the one who was faithful, and asks the one who erred to keep his promise, year after year.

## A small wedding in a US kitchen

Ours is not elaborate. My wife ties a red cloth around the pot like a sari and slips a few glass bangles over the rim. Four sugarcane stalks, from the Indian grocer, are tied together at the top over the pot to make a mandap, a wedding canopy. A small picture of Krishna, and sometimes a shaligram borrowed from a friend, is placed beside the tulsi. We add an amla branch, the way my mother does, light a lamp, offer a sweet, and my son rings the bell with too much enthusiasm.

We invite two or three neighbours. Some are Hindu; some just like the idea of a plant getting married. If you keep a tulsi indoors in a cold climate, there is practical advice in [keeping a tulsi plant indoors](/blog/tulsi-plant-indoors). The same season brings [the lamps of Karthigai Deepam](/blog/karthigai-deepam-significance), and our kitchen windowsill is rarely without a flame that month. My son once asked whether the tulsi counts as a teacher, since she is always first; I told him to ask on [Guru Purnima](/blog/guru-purnima-meaning), the day for teachers.

## One small thing to carry into the week

Andal, the Tamil poet-saint, wished to marry the Lord himself, and in the tradition she did, as [Andal's story and what it means](/blog/andal-story-meaning) tells. The tulsi, in her own way, gets the same wish granted every year.

This week, water the tulsi each morning, or any plant you keep, and say one line to Vishnu while you do it. It can be his name. It can be a thank-you. The plant will not mind which, and you will have started the day with something green and alive in your hands.
