Puliyodarai was the food of long train journeys out of Chennai, wrapped in a banana leaf and still good hours later somewhere past Villupuram. In New Jersey, a jar of puli kaichal, the tamarind paste, in the fridge does the same job for a Saturday offering. This puliyodarai recipe makes the paste first, because that is where all the flavour lives; the rice is mixed in minutes whenever you need it.

## Puliyodarai recipe: make the paste first

**For the puli kaichal (enough for three or four batches of rice)**

- 60 g tamarind (a lemon-sized ball), soaked in 500 ml hot water (2 cups)
- 100 ml gingelly (sesame) oil (a scant ½ cup)
- 1 teaspoon mustard seeds
- 1 tablespoon each chana dal and urad dal
- 2 tablespoons raw peanuts
- 6 dried red chillies, broken
- 2 sprigs curry leaves
- ½ teaspoon turmeric and ½ teaspoon asafoetida
- 1 tablespoon grated jaggery
- Salt to taste

**For the roasted powder**

- 2 tablespoons white sesame, 1 tablespoon coriander seeds, 1 teaspoon fenugreek seeds, 3 dried red chillies, dry-roasted and ground fine

**Method**

1. Squeeze the soaked tamarind and strain the extract, discarding the fibre.
2. Heat the oil in a heavy pan. Add mustard; when it pops, add the dals and peanuts and fry until golden.
3. Add the chillies, curry leaves, turmeric and asafoetida.
4. Pour in the tamarind extract with salt and jaggery. Simmer on low, stirring now and then, for twenty-five to thirty minutes.
5. When the paste thickens and the oil separates and floats at the edges, stir in most of the roasted powder. Cool and store in a clean glass jar.
6. To serve, cook 200 g raw rice (1 cup) so the grains stay separate, spread it out to cool, then mix in three or four tablespoons of paste, a spoon of raw sesame oil and the remaining powder.

## Why does temple puliyodarai taste different?

In Vishnu temples in Tamil Nadu, especially in the Srivaishnava tradition, the Tamil community of devotion to Vishnu and Lakshmi, puliyodarai is one of the great prasadams, food offered to Perumal, as Vishnu is called in Tamil, and then given to devotees. Anyone who has queued for it knows the taste: darker, deeper, more sesame than most home versions.

Three things explain most of it. The paste is cooked a long time, until the tamarind loses its raw edge entirely. Sesame oil is used generously, and sesame powder is added in larger measure. And there is no onion or garlic, for the reasons in [why many Hindu kitchens skip onion and garlic](/blog/why-no-onion-garlic). But the fourth reason is harder to put in a recipe. It is cooked to be offered first. The cooks are not making lunch.

My Thatha kept a cloth shop in Kumbakonam, and on busy festival days, he said, temple puliyodarai was what the whole shop ate standing up. He never tried to make it at home. He said it would not taste right unless it had been to the temple first.

## When is it offered?

Puliyodarai turns up at particular moments in the year:

- **Saturdays in Purattasi**, the Tamil month many families dedicate to Perumal.
- **The day after Vaikunta Ekadashi**, when the fast is broken with a feast.
- **Aadi Perukku**, when families carry mixed rices to the riverbank; I have written about [what Aadi Perukku means](/blog/aadi-perukku-meaning).
- **Journeys**, because it keeps.

To offer it at home, press a small cone of the rice onto a leaf or plate, set it before the lamp, and complete the pooja before anyone serves themselves. If you need a simple sequence for that, [puja at home, step by step](/blog/puja-at-home-step-by-step) is a good guide. On a festival plate it sits well with [daddojanam, the temple curd rice](/blog/daddojanam-curd-rice-prasadam), and a spoon of [quick rava kesari](/blog/rava-kesari-naivedyam) for sweetness.

In the Tiruppavai, Andal imagines the devotees finally sitting down together to rice so full of ghee that it runs to their elbows. I like that the poem ends its longing with a shared meal.

## One small thing to carry into the week

Make one jar of paste on a weekend. Then, this Saturday, mix a small plate before breakfast, offer it at the lamp, and pack the rest in your lunch box. When a colleague asks what smells so good, tell them it went to God first.
