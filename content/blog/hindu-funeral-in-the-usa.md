A Hindu funeral in the USA is usually simpler than families fear. There is a funeral home, a priest, a few old rites done carefully, and a cremation that the family can often witness. I learned this at a friend's father's funeral in New Jersey, where nobody knew what to do for the first hour, and then the priest arrived and, quietly, the rites took over. They are old for a reason. They hold people up when people cannot hold themselves.

## What happens in the first hours?

The first call is to a funeral home; often the hospital or hospice will ask which one. Ask directly whether they have served Hindu families before. Many have, especially near large communities, and they will already know that the family may want to wash and dress the body, that cremation is the norm, and that it is usually wanted soon.

At the funeral home, the body is washed and dressed in new cloth: a white or cream veshti, a plain sari, sometimes the person's own favourite. Family members may be allowed to help; ask. A lamp is lit at the head. A little Ganga water, if the family has kept some, and a tulsi leaf are placed at the lips. Vibhuti or a namam, depending on the family's tradition, goes on the forehead. None of this needs to be elaborate.

If your parent died in India while you were here, the situation is different; [when a parent dies abroad](/blog/when-a-parent-dies-abroad) is written for that.

## Who performs the last rites?

The last rites are called antyeshti, the "final offering". The person who performs them is the karta, traditionally the eldest son. In practice, any child may be the karta: a younger son, a daughter, a grandchild. Many priests in the US are comfortable with a daughter performing the rites; ask when you call.

The priest leads and the karta follows. There are offerings of rice and black sesame, water poured, mantras said. At a riverbank in India, the karta would circle the body with a clay pot of water; in a funeral home chapel, this is done in the room, and at the end the pot is broken or set down. Much of what happens on a cremation ground in India can be done in a chapel, and a good priest knows how to adapt.

For a funeral, ask the temple first, since most keep a list of priests who do last rites; the note on [finding a Hindu priest in the USA](/blog/finding-a-hindu-priest-usa) has other questions worth asking. Check local rules on timing, permits and paperwork early. The funeral home will guide you. They do this every day, even when you never have.

## The crematorium and the days after

Many crematoriums allow the family to be present when cremation begins, and some let the karta start it. Ask early, because it has to be arranged. It is a hard moment and a strangely important one; friends have told me afterwards that they were glad they stood there. In the second chapter of the Bhagavad Gita, Krishna says the self is not slain when the body is slain. I did not find that comforting in the chapel. I found it comforting weeks later.

The ashes are collected a day or a few days later. Most families keep them at home until they can be immersed, often in India, which raises its own questions; [immersing ashes in India from abroad](/blog/immersing-ashes-from-abroad) walks through them. At home, a lamp is kept burning, often through the ten to thirteen days of mourning. Food is simple, sometimes cooked by neighbours rather than the family. Visitors come and sit, and the house is quiet. Those days end with a ceremony of return, explained in [the thirteenth day ceremony](/blog/thirteenth-day-ceremony-meaning).

## One small thing to carry

Write three numbers on a card and keep it somewhere you can find it: a funeral home that has served Hindu families, a priest who does last rites, and one friend who will bring food without being asked. It feels morbid to do this when everyone is well. It is not. The rites hold the family so that no one has to invent them in the worst week of their life. The card does the same for the first hour.
