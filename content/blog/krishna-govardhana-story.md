A boy asks his elders a rude question. Why do we worship a god in the sky, when it is this hill that feeds our cows? The elders have no good answer, so they follow the boy, and the sky answers with seven days of rain. The Govardhan story meaning starts with that question, and it is a better question than it first appears: before you thank anything distant, thank what is actually holding you up.

## The Govardhan story in the Bhagavata Purana

The tenth book of the Bhagavata Purana, the book of Krishna's life, tells it in a few short chapters. Krishna is still a child in Vraja, the land of cowherds along the Yamuna. One day he sees the whole village busy with preparations: grain, milk, sweets, ghee, all being gathered for a great yearly offering.

He asks his foster father Nanda what it is for. Nanda explains that it is for Indra, king of the gods and lord of rain. Indra sends the clouds, the clouds bring grass, the grass feeds the cows, and the cows are the village's life. So each year they honour him.

Krishna is not satisfied. We are cowherds, he says. We do not live in cities or rule kingdoms. Our life is the forest, the hill and the cows. It is Govardhana, the hill right here, that gives our cattle grass and shelter and water. Why not honour the hill, the cows and the brahmins and the poor of our own village instead of a distant king?

The villagers, a little uncertain, agree. The offering is moved. The food is carried to the foot of Govardhana, the hill is worshipped, the cows are decorated and fed, and everyone eats together. It is, in its way, a small revolution: worship turned from the sky to the ground under one's feet.

## Why did Indra send the storm?

Indra noticed. The king of the gods, wounded in his pride, did what proud people with power usually do. He sent the storm clouds to Vraja and ordered them to pour until the village was washed away.

The rain came in torrents. Wind flattened the trees. The cowherds, their families and their cattle were terrified and ran to Krishna. It was your idea, they said, in effect. Save us.

Krishna walked to Govardhana, put his hand beneath it, and lifted the whole hill into the air, balancing it on the little finger of his left hand like a child holding up an umbrella. He called everyone underneath: cowherds, women, children, cows and calves. For seven days and seven nights the rain fell, and the village sheltered beneath the hill, dry, while the small boy held it up without moving.

In the story's own terms, that is the whole reason he lifted it: to protect the people who had trusted him. Some folk tellings add a lovely detail. Each cowherd raised his own stick to help hold up the hill, and though Krishna was doing all the work, he let them feel they were sharing it.

## Govardhan story meaning: worship what sustains you

At the end of seven days, Indra understood what he was up against. He called off the clouds, came down, and bowed to Krishna. The story of Indra and Krishna ends not with a battle but with an apology. Surabhi, the heavenly cow, came with him, and together they bathed Krishna and gave him a new name: Govinda, the one who protects the cows, or in another reading, the one who knows and delights the senses.

There are several ideas folded into this. One is about pride: the god of the sky, used to being worshipped, cannot bear to be overlooked, and the storm he sends is really a tantrum. Another is about protection: the divine holds up the thing that might have crushed you and makes it a shelter.

But the one I keep returning to is Krishna's first question. The hill, the cows, the grass and the neighbours were holding the village up long before anyone thought to thank them. The same instinct runs through [why Hindus honour the cow](/blog/go-puja-cow-worship-meaning). Many families still keep Govardhan Puja on the day after Deepavali, as part of the [five days of Diwali](/blog/five-days-of-diwali), piling food into a small mountain. The story also sits comfortably beside the old Rig Vedic idea of [many paths to one truth](/blog/many-paths-one-truth): the divine can be honoured in a hill as surely as in the sky.

## Thank what holds you up

This week, before you thank anything grand, thank one ordinary thing that holds your life up. The market that has food in it. The neighbour who takes in your parcels. The water that comes out of the tap. The bus driver. It will feel slightly foolish. So did Krishna's question, at first.
