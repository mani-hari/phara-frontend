Shivaratri landed on a Thursday. I got home at eight, too tired to fast properly and too stubborn to skip it, and sat down on the living room floor in the dark anyway. That evening taught me something about Shivaratri fasting for working people: what the night asks for is intention, one offering and some wakefulness. A full fast and all four watches are beautiful, but they can bend. You can keep the night honestly even after a long week at a desk.

## Shivaratri fasting for working people

My grandmother kept Shivaratri the full way. No food from morning, the temple at dusk, four rounds of abhishekam through the night, a bath at dawn. I grew up thinking that was the only version that counted. Then I got a job with a commute and a manager in another time zone, and the only version that counted became the one I kept skipping.

So I started asking what is essential. Three things, I think. First, intention: a quiet moment in the morning where you decide that today is Shivaratri for you, even if nobody at work knows. Second, one offering: water poured over a small linga, or a single lamp in front of a picture of Shiva. Third, some wakefulness: a stretch of time in the evening, with the lights off, when you are awake on purpose.

What can bend? The full fast, if your work or health needs food. Eat simply, fruit and milk and one light meal, and let that be your fast. And all four praharas, the three-hour watches of the traditional vigil. If you want to try them, there is a good guide to [keeping the Shivaratri vigil at home](/blog/shivaratri-vigil-at-home). If you cannot, one watch is still a watch.

## Can a tired mind still keep vigil?

That Thursday I sat in the dark and could not stop thinking about a spreadsheet. I felt like a fraud. I remember wondering whether a tired, distracted mind could keep vigil at all.

Appar, the Tamil saint whose hymns are gathered in the Thevaram, has a line I love. He says the shade of the Lord's feet is like a faultless veena, like the evening moon, like a southern breeze, like a pond humming with bees. None of those images is dramatic. They are the ordinary sweetness of a quiet evening. I read that line again recently, and it occurred to me that Appar was not describing an ecstatic devotee. He was describing rest.

Tiredness, then, is not a failure of devotion. It can be an honest offering. You bring what you have. The story at the heart of the night is about a hunter who did not even know he was praying, and the Shiva Purana still counted his sleepless fear as worship; you can read it in [the meaning of Maha Shivaratri](/blog/maha-shivaratri-meaning). A tired office worker sitting in the dark with Om Namah Shivaya is not doing worse than that hunter.

## A small Shivaratri after a long week

Here is what I do now, and what I would suggest if you are asking how to observe Shivaratri while working, especially when it falls on a weekday.

When you get home, wash your face and hands and change into something loose. Pour a little water slowly over a linga, or into a bowl before a picture. Light one lamp. Turn off every other light. Put your phone in another room, not face down beside you, actually in another room. Then sit and chant Om Namah Shivaya for thirty minutes. Out loud if you live alone, in a whisper if you do not.

If thirty minutes of chanting feels long, start with ten and let the rest be silence. The notes on [chanting for beginners](/blog/chanting-for-beginners) helped me stop worrying about getting it right. And if even that feels like too much on a weeknight, the twice-monthly [Pradosham evening](/blog/pradosham-meaning) is a gentler way to build the habit before the big night comes round again.

Then go to bed. It is all right to sleep on Shivaratri if your body needs it. The night will not hold it against you.

## One thing to carry into next week

The morning after that Thursday, I felt oddly clear. Not because I had achieved anything, but because I had stopped for half an hour in the dark and nothing had been asked of me.

So here is the small thing to carry into the coming week: five minutes of sitting in the dark before sleep. Lights off, phone away, a few rounds of Om Namah Shivaya or just your own breathing. It is a tiny Shivaratri, every night, and it fits after even the longest day. If you are also trying to [fast with a desk job](/blog/fasting-with-a-desk-job) on other days, you will find the two practices keep each other company.
