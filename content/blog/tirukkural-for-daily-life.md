The easiest way into the Tirukkural is one couplet a day. I started reading one on the train out of Edison, and the two lines lasted longer than the ride. That is the whole method behind the Thirukkural for daily life: read a single kural in the morning, in Tamil and in an English rendering, carry one phrase with you, and notice where it turns up. You do not need to read the book cover to cover, and you do not need to be a Tamil scholar. You need two lines and a little attention.

## What is in the Kural, in one minute?

The Tirukkural, often shortened to the Kural, was written by the poet Valluvar (little is known about him, and [who Thiruvalluvar was](/blog/who-was-thiruvalluvar) sets out what there is). It has 133 chapters of ten couplets each, 1,330 in all. Each couplet is two short lines.

The chapters are grouped into three books. Aram is about virtue: kindness, truthfulness, gratitude, self-control, the life of the household. Porul is about wealth and public life: work, statecraft, friendship, the conduct of ministers and kings. Inbam is about love, in a series of tender scenes between lovers.

It is not a book of rituals and it does not name a particular god. For that reason it is easy to share with anyone, whatever their faith or lack of one.

## Thirukkural for daily life: a one-couplet method

Here is how I read it, and how many friends who have asked me how to read the Thirukkural have started.

1. Choose one chapter. Ten couplets, so ten days, or ten mornings on the train.
2. Each morning, read one kural in Tamil, even if slowly, and then in an English translation. There are several good Thirukkural in English renderings; any clear one will do.
3. Pick one phrase from it, a few words, and carry it through the day.
4. In the evening, notice where it turned up: a meeting, a queue at the pharmacy, a call home to Chennai.

For Thirukkural beginners, two chapters work particularly well. Iniyavai Kooral, on pleasant words, has a couplet I think of every week, Kural 100:

*Iniya ulavaaga innaadha kooral*\
*kaniyiruppak kaai kavarndhatru.*

To speak harsh words when sweet ones are at hand is like picking unripe fruit when ripe fruit is there. Valluvar gives a whole chapter to this: [the Tirukkural on sweet speech](/blog/tirukkural-on-sweet-speech).

The other is Seynandri Arithal, on gratitude. Kural 102 says that help given at the right moment, however small, is greater than the world. I read that one on a morning when a colleague had quietly covered for me in a meeting the day before, and I sent her a thank-you note before I got off the train. There is more on this chapter in [the Tirukkural on gratitude](/blog/tirukkural-on-gratitude).

What surprised me was not the wisdom of the lines. It was how often they came back without my calling them. Two lines are the right size to be remembered.

## Reading it with a child in America

A few months after I started, my son wanted to know what I was reading on my phone. He is around eleven, and his Tamil is better at listening than reading, so we read the English together and I read the Tamil aloud.

We chose a chapter together. He argued with Valluvar almost at once. Why should you not get angry if someone is unfair? Is it really better to be quiet? I let him argue. The Kural can take it, and a child who argues with a text has at least read it. We have gone back to [the Tirukkural on anger](/blog/tirukkural-on-anger) more than once since.

Because the Kural is so plainly about how to live, rather than about any one god, it has been easy to share with his friends too, and it has become one small part of [raising Hindu kids in America](/blog/raising-hindu-kids-in-america) in our house. One of them, whose family is from Ohio, asked if he could borrow the chapter on friendship.

## One small thing for the week

The early train is not a holy place. It smells of coffee and wet umbrellas, and half the carriage is asleep. But it gives me a quiet ten minutes, and the Kural fits into it.

One small thing for the week: pick one chapter. Read one couplet each morning for seven days. At dinner, say one line aloud and see what the family makes of it. [Karma explained simply](/blog/karma-explained-simply) makes the case that small daily actions matter more than grand intentions, and a single kural is about as small as it gets.
