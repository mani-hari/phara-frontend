The evil eye in Hinduism, called drishti in the south and nazar in the north, is the old belief that an envious or over-admiring gaze can harm what it looks at, especially a baby, a new house or a sudden success. Watch a grandmother dab a black dot behind a newborn's ear and ask her why. She will say the child is too beautiful, and someone might look too long. The custom looks like it is about envy. It is just as much about how we protect what we love when we know we cannot control who is watching.

## The evil eye in Hinduism: what drishti means

Drishti simply means sight. In everyday use it has come to mean a sight that wounds: the neighbour's glance at your new car, the aunt who praises your son's marks a little too warmly. The belief is far older than any one religion; the Mediterranean has its blue glass eye, the Middle East its hand-shaped charm. India gave it faces.

Walk past any building under construction in Tamil Nadu and you will see one: the drishti bommai, a grimacing painted demon with bulging eyes and a lolling tongue, tied to the scaffolding. Shops hang a lemon threaded with seven green chillies over the door. Trucks carry a small black doll on the bumper. The logic is charming in its cunning. Give the envious eye something uglier or sharper to land on, and it will spend itself there.

The face has a grander cousin. The Skanda Purana tells of Kirtimukha, a ferocious being created by Shiva who, having nothing left to devour, ate his own body until only a face remained. Pleased, Shiva placed that face above the doors of his temples. Look up at almost any temple gateway and he is still there, mouth open. What the story is doing is taking a hunger that consumes everything and turning it into a guardian.

## Why do people circle salt, chillies or camphor?

The most familiar household custom is drishti suthi, the Tamil phrase for circling away the eye. A handful of salt, dried red chillies or a flame of camphor is waved around a person three times, then dissolved in water or dropped into the fire. Children returning from a wedding, a bride after the ceremony, a relative back from hospital: all get circled. The black kajal dot works differently. It deliberately makes the child look a little imperfect, so no gaze lingers.

Then there are the protectors. Many homes keep an image of Ganesha facing the front door, and there is a lovely reason for his watchfulness in the telling of his own birth; this post on [why Ganesha comes first](/blog/why-ganesha-comes-first) tells it. In one version from the Brahmavaivarta Purana, it is Shani's gaze falling on the infant Ganesha that costs him his first head. The boy who suffered the worst drishti in the stories becomes the one we ask to guard the door. Hanuman, too, is a favourite, and many families pair a protective image with [the Hanuman Chalisa on Saturdays](/blog/hanuman-chalisa-on-saturdays).

## How to read the custom today

If you are sceptical, you are in good company, and there is no scientific evidence that a look can make anyone ill. But envy itself is real. Everyone has felt its heat, from both sides. The drishti rituals may be less a defence against a magical beam than an acknowledgement: people will compare, people will want what you have, and it is wise to be a little modest with your good fortune. The same instinct shows up in the question of whether [astrology is real](/blog/is-astrology-real): the tradition often names a real human pressure through a cosmic image.

What the custom should never become is fear or blame. The moment a family starts pointing at a particular aunt as the source of the eye, the ritual has turned into the very envy it was meant to deflect. The older antidote was always kindness, generosity and not boasting, which is closer to [karma, explained simply](/blog/karma-explained-simply), than to any charm.

## Keeping a drishti custom with a smile

If your family circles salt after a crowded function, keep doing it, and do it with a smile and a short prayer rather than a frown. If you have moved away from such customs, as many people who call themselves [spiritual but not religious](/blog/spiritual-but-not-religious-hinduism) have, you can still borrow the insight. Pair it with one new habit: praise others generously and specifically, without a trace of comparison. An eye that admires without wanting is the one gaze nobody needs protection from.
