A fast is not a punishment for the body. For nine nights, it is a way of making the body lighter so that attention can move somewhere else. The common Navratri fasting rules are simple: no grains, no onion or garlic, no meat, eggs or alcohol; instead fruit, milk, nuts, a few specific flours and rock salt, often as one meal a day. Everything beyond that varies by family. What matters more than the list is why you are doing it, and whether you can keep it kindly for all nine days.

## Navratri fasting rules, the common version

Most households follow some form of this pattern:

- **Avoid:** rice, wheat and other everyday grains; lentils in many homes; onion and garlic; meat, fish, eggs and alcohol; regular table salt.
- **Eat:** fruit, milk, curd, paneer and ghee; nuts; potatoes, sweet potatoes and other root vegetables; sabudana, the small pearls made from tapioca; kuttu, buckwheat flour, and singhara, water chestnut flour; samak rice, a small millet that is treated as a fasting food.
- **Salt:** sendha namak, rock salt, in place of ordinary salt.

Some people eat one full meal after sunset and nothing before. Others keep phalahar, a diet of fruit and milk, through the day. A few observe a stricter fast on the first and last days only. None of these is the real one. They are all real.

What about coffee? Many families allow tea or coffee with milk, since milk is a fasting food; others give it up as part of the discipline. If you are unsure, follow your household's custom, or simply try the nine days without it and see what happens.

## Why these rules?

Look at the list again with your body rather than your rulebook. Grains are heavy. Onion, garlic and meat are stimulating. Fruit, milk and root vegetables are light and warm. The tradition calls this sattvic food, food that leaves the mind clear rather than dull or restless. Navratri arrives at the change of season, when the monsoon is ending and digestion is often sluggish. A lighter plate at that hinge makes sense whether or not you think of it as religious.

But the deeper function is reminder. Every time you reach for bread and stop, you remember what these nine nights are for. The fast becomes a small bell ringing through the day. It is not a feat of endurance. Nobody gets a prize for being the hungriest person in the family.

Many people find that by the fourth day something shifts. The first days are mostly about food: planning it, missing it, thinking about dinner at eleven in the morning. Then the noise drops. You have a little more space in the head for the evening lamp, for the chanting, for the stories of [why Navratri lasts nine nights](/blog/why-navratri-lasts-nine-nights).

## What if you cannot fast fully?

Then do not. The tradition has always made room for the body. If you are pregnant, diabetic, on regular medication, recovering from illness, elderly, or working long physical shifts, talk to your doctor before changing how you eat, and choose something gentle.

Gentle options that still count:

- One sattvic meal a day, with normal food at other times.
- Giving up one thing for nine days: sugar, coffee, social media after dinner.
- Keeping the fast only on the eighth or ninth day.
- Fasting from grains but eating well otherwise.

The same principle runs through other fasts in the calendar; [fasting and focus](/blog/fasting-and-focus) looks at what many people notice when they try.

## Try this for the nine days

Before the first night, decide what your fast will be and write it on a piece of paper. Make it something you can keep on the busiest day of the nine, not the easiest. Stick it on the fridge.

Then pay attention. Notice when the craving comes, what time of day, what it is really asking for. Notice your sleep. Notice your temper. By day four, note one thing that has changed.

If you are setting up a [Golu in a small apartment](/blog/golu-in-a-small-apartment) or reading the [Durga Saptashati for the first time](/blog/durga-saptashati-for-beginners), let the fast support that rather than compete with it. And on the tenth day, [Dussehra and the story of Ravana](/blog/dussehra-ravana-story-meaning) is a good moment to break it slowly: something warm, something simple, eaten without hurry.
