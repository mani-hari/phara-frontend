My aunt in New Jersey booked her first online puja on a lunch break, and she cried when the priest said her grandson's name in Kanchipuram. That is the simplest way to describe an online puja from the USA: you choose a ritual, share a few details, and a priest at a temple in India performs it in your family's name. You receive confirmation, photos or video, and later a packet of prasadam, the blessed offering, in the post. What takes more thought is the part no website can do for you, which is making it feel like your own prayer from eight thousand miles away.

## How online puja from the USA works

The steps are fewer than people expect.

First, you choose the ritual. It might be a simple archana, the chanting of a deity's names with your name added, or a larger homam, a fire ritual with offerings into a sacred fire. If the difference is unclear, there is a plain explanation of [what a homam is and how it differs from a puja](/blog/what-is-a-homam).

Second, you share the details for the sankalpam, the spoken statement of intention that opens every puja. Usually this means the names of the people it is for, their gotra or family lineage, and their nakshatra or birth star. If you do not know some of these, it does not block anything; I would read [what sankalpam means, with name, gotra and star explained](/blog/what-is-sankalpam) before you worry.

Third, you pick a date. Temples follow the Hindu calendar, so certain days suit certain rituals, and the service will often suggest one.

Then the priest does what priests have always done for families who could not be present: he stands before the deity, speaks your details aloud, and performs the ritual in your name. Families in India have sponsored pujas for sons in the army and daughters married into distant towns for generations. The internet has only shortened the distance of the message.

## What will I see and receive?

After booking, you should receive a confirmation with the date and the time in Indian Standard Time. Temple rituals usually happen in the early morning, which in the US is often late evening or night before, depending on your coast and the season. Some services stream it live; most share photos or a video afterwards.

Later comes the prasadam: sacred ash, kumkum, a thread, sometimes a sealed sweet. It travels by international post, so it takes a while. What it contains and how it clears customs is covered in [prasadam delivery to the USA](/blog/prasadam-delivery-to-the-usa). When my aunt's arrived, she kept the envelope on her altar for a month.

## Making it feel real from far away

People sometimes ask whether an online puja counts. The Bhagavad Gita says that a person is made of their faith: as one's faith is, so one is. The ritual is complete when the priest performs it with your name. Whether it feels like yours depends on what you do at your end.

Here is what has helped the families I know:

- **Light a lamp at home at the same hour.** If the puja is at six in the morning in India, that may be half past eight the previous evening in New York. Sit before your own lamp while it happens, even if you are not watching.
- **Involve the children.** Let them say the names of the people the puja is for. They remember more than we think.
- **Watch with attention, or not at all.** A live stream on a phone while cooking dinner is not the same as ten quiet minutes. There are practical suggestions for [joining a live puja across time zones](/blog/joining-a-puja-across-time-zones).

If you are booking for someone else, especially parents in India, the details and choices are slightly different; I have written about [booking a puja for your parents from abroad](/blog/booking-a-puja-for-parents).

## One small thing to carry into the week

Before you book, write down on a piece of paper why you are doing it. One or two sentences. For my aunt, it was simply: "So that he grows up knowing where he comes from."

Fold the paper and put it by your altar. When the prasadam arrives weeks later, open the envelope and read your note again before you open the packet. It closes the circle. The puja began with an intention in your kitchen, travelled to a temple and came back to you. Let it end where it started.
