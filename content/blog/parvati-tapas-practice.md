Parvati did not win Shiva by charm. The charm was tried, and it failed: Kama, the god of desire, was burned to ash by a single glance. What worked was tapas, a steady discipline kept long past the point of comfort. That is the Parvati tapas story in one line, and the useful thing about it is that tapas is not reserved for goddesses on mountains. It is something you can try this week, in a kitchen, with an alarm clock.

## The Parvati tapas story

The Shiva Purana and Kalidasa's Kumarasambhavam tell it with different flavours but the same shape. Shiva, grieving the loss of Sati, had withdrawn into meditation. Parvati, the daughter of the mountain king, wanted him. The gods wanted him too, for their own reasons, and sent Kama to stir desire in him while Parvati served nearby. Shiva opened his third eye, and Kama was gone.

So Parvati did something else. She left the palace and went up into the mountains to practise austerity. Kalidasa describes her standing in summer between four fires with the sun overhead as the fifth, and in winter standing in icy water through the night. She fasted. First she lived on fruit, then on fallen leaves, and finally on nothing at all. That last stage gave her a name, Aparna, the one without even a leaf. The aparna meaning is simply that: she let go of the last small thing she was leaning on.

The telling is extreme on purpose. Myths exaggerate so the lesson cannot be missed. You are not being asked to stand between fires.

## What is tapas, really?

If you ask what tapas is in Hinduism, the word itself answers first. Tapas means heat. It is the warmth that builds when you keep one discipline steadily, the way friction warms your palms.

It is easy to misread this as punishment, as though the body were an enemy to be beaten into line. The story does not support that reading. Parvati is not hurting herself for being bad. She is gathering herself. Every distraction she drops, comfort, food, attention to what others think, makes her more concentrated, more entirely one thing.

A simple working definition: tapas is attention held a little longer than is comfortable. The moment you want to check your phone during a meal, and don't, is tapas. The moment you want to sleep in, and get up at the time you chose, is tapas. Nothing dramatic. The heat comes from repetition, not intensity.

Try it and you will notice something. The first three days feel like effort. Around day four or five, the discipline starts to hold you up instead of the other way round. The energy you were spending on negotiating with yourself comes back to you. That returning energy is what [Shakti in daily practice](/blog/shakti-in-daily-practice) is about.

## What did the young ascetic's test teach her?

Near the end of her tapas, a young ascetic arrives at her hermitage. He is charming and curious, and then he starts to mock. Why would a princess waste herself on Shiva, he asks, a man who smears himself with ash, wears snakes, lives in cremation grounds, and owns nothing but a bull?

Parvati does not argue with him. She tells him to stop, and turns to leave rather than listen. At that moment the ascetic shows his real form. It was Shiva, testing her.

Notice what was being tested. Not her strength; she had already shown that. It was whether her discipline needed an audience. Would praise or mockery move her? It did not. Her tapas was not a performance for anyone, including the person she was doing it for. The fuller story of her choice is told in [Swayamvara Parvathi](/blog/swayamvara-parvathi-story), and the union that followed is the subject of [the Ardhanarishvara form](/blog/ardhanarishvara-meaning).

This is the part most of us skip. We start a discipline and immediately announce it. The announcement gives a small hit of satisfaction, and oddly, that often drains the will to continue.

## Try one small tapas for seven days

Choose one. A fixed wake time. One meal a day without the phone. Ten minutes of silence before the house wakes up. Keep it small enough that you can do it on your worst day.

Then keep it for seven days without telling anyone. No posts, no updates, no "I'm trying this thing." Notice the pull to mention it, and let the pull itself be part of the practice.

At the end of the week, sit for a minute and ask where the heat went. Many people find the discipline has spilled into other parts of the day. Some find it has shown them how much of their energy goes into being seen. Either way, you will have tasted what Parvati knew on the mountain, and why so many devotees keep the night of [Maha Shivaratri](/blog/maha-shivaratri-meaning) as a small tapas of their own.
