Ugadi pachadi is the only festival food designed to be a little unpleasant. One spoon holds sweet, sour, salt, bitter, hot and tangy together, and you are asked to eat it without making a face. This ugadi pachadi recipe needs no stove and takes ten minutes: tamarind, jaggery, raw mango, salt, chilli and neem flowers, stirred together in one bowl and offered at the new year morning pooja before breakfast.

## Ugadi pachadi recipe: six ingredients, one bowl

**Ingredients (a small bowl, enough for a family)**

- A small lime-sized piece of tamarind (about 15 g), soaked in 120 ml warm water (½ cup)
- 3 tablespoons grated jaggery
- 3 tablespoons finely chopped raw mango
- A pinch of salt
- 1 small green chilli, finely chopped, or a pinch of crushed black pepper
- 1 teaspoon neem flowers, fresh, or dried and lightly toasted

**Method**

1. Squeeze the tamarind and strain the water. Discard the pulp.
2. Stir the jaggery into the tamarind water until it dissolves.
3. Add the raw mango, salt and chilli or pepper.
4. Add the neem flowers last. If they are dried, toast them in a dry pan for a few seconds first; it takes the edge off the bitterness without removing it.
5. Stir and let it sit for a few minutes.

Some families add a few slices of banana, a spoon of grated coconut, or a little roasted chana dal. The six tastes stay the point.

Neem flowers can be hard to find abroad. Many Indian stores stock them dried in the weeks before the new year; if not, a few drops of bitter gourd juice or a very small piece of neem leaf are sometimes used, though families differ on this.

## What do the six tastes stand for?

Tradition reads the six as the year ahead, all present from the first day:

- **Jaggery** for joy.
- **Neem** for sorrow.
- **Tamarind** for the unpleasant, the things you would rather avoid.
- **Salt** for interest and flavour in life.
- **Chilli or pepper** for anger, or surprise.
- **Raw mango** for the new, the unfamiliar.

Now try eating it slowly, one spoon, and pay attention. You will notice the tongue does not taste all six at once. Sweet arrives first, then sour, and the bitterness of the neem comes last, lingering after the rest has gone. It is a small, honest model of how a year actually unfolds.

The practice is not to enjoy every taste. It is to receive all of them without spitting any out. That is harder than it sounds, and it is exactly what the ritual asks.

## When is it made?

Ugadi, the Telugu and Kannada new year, falls on the first day of the waxing moon in the month of Chaitra, usually March or April. For why the year turns here, read [the significance of Ugadi](/blog/ugadi-significance).

Tamil homes keep a related custom on Puthandu, the Tamil solar new year in mid-April, with maanga pachadi: raw mango cooked with jaggery and a little neem flower, sweet and bitter together. If you are keeping the day far from home, [Puthandu in the USA](/blog/puthandu-tamil-new-year-usa) has practical ideas.

Either way, the pachadi is offered at the morning pooja, after the bath and before breakfast, and everyone in the house takes a spoon.

## Notice the taste you resist

Other festivals have their own signature dishes, from [ada pradhaman for Onam](/blog/ada-pradhaman-for-onam) to [Thiruvathirai kali](/blog/thiruvathirai-kali-recipe) in the cold of Margazhi. None of them asks quite what this one does.

On the next new year morning, take one spoon slowly. Notice which taste you resist, the one your face wants to react to. Then let that be the one you are ready for in the year ahead.
