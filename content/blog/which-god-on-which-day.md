Ask a grandmother which god to worship on which day and she will answer before you finish the question. Monday is Shiva's. Tuesday is Murugan's, or Durga's, or Hanuman's, depending on where she grew up. Friday is Lakshmi's. Ask her why, and the answer usually trails off into "that is how it is done". But the real reason is written into the names of the days themselves. Every weekday in the Indian calendar is named after a graha, a planet of Jyotish, and each god is traditionally matched to the qualities of that planet. The story leads back to the sky.

## Which god to worship on which day?

Here is the common pattern, with the understanding that families and regions vary.

- **Sunday** is for Surya, the Sun. Water offered at sunrise; there is a fuller account in [Surya graha worship](/blog/surya-graha-worship).
- **Monday** is for Shiva, who wears the crescent moon in his hair. Many people keep a light fast; see [the somvar vrat](/blog/somvar-vrat-monday-fast).
- **Tuesday** is for Murugan in Tamil homes, Durga for courage, Hanuman in much of the north. The customs are described in [Tuesday worship of Murugan and Durga](/blog/tuesday-worship-murugan-durga).
- **Wednesday** is for Vishnu, Krishna or Ganesha, depending on the family.
- **Thursday** is for the Guru: Dakshinamurthy, Sai Baba, Guru Raghavendra, or Brihaspati himself.
- **Friday** is for Lakshmi and the Devi in her many forms; the [Friday Lakshmi pooja](/blog/friday-lakshmi-pooja) is one of the most widely kept weekly practices.
- **Saturday** is for Shani, for Hanuman, and in Tamil and Telugu homes for Venkateswara, Perumal of the Seven Hills.

## Why are the days tied to planets?

The Sanskrit names of the week are a list of the grahas: Ravivara, the Sun's day; Somavara, the Moon's; Mangalavara, Mars's; Budhavara, Mercury's; Guruvara, Jupiter's; Shukravara, Venus's; Shanivara, Saturn's. Tamil keeps the same pattern: Nyayiru, Thingal, Sevvai, Budhan, Viyazhan, Velli, Sani.

Once you see that, the gods fall into place.

Monday belongs to the Moon, and the Moon rests on Shiva's head, so Shiva gets Monday. Tuesday belongs to Mars, the red warrior, so it goes to the warrior gods: Murugan with his spear, Durga on her lion, Hanuman with his mace. Thursday belongs to Jupiter, the guru of the gods, so it goes to the teachers. Friday belongs to Venus, the planet of beauty and comfort, so it goes to Lakshmi, who is beauty and plenty. Saturday belongs to Shani, the planet of discipline and delay, and Hanuman is honoured on Saturdays because a well-known story says he freed Shani from Ravana's prison, and Shani promised to go easy on Hanuman's devotees. That is why so many people recite the [Hanuman Chalisa on Saturdays](/blog/hanuman-chalisa-on-saturdays).

The pattern is not a law laid down in one scripture. It grew, the way a path through a field grows, because enough feet walked it.

## Why does it change from region to region?

Because the week was always a meeting of the sky with local devotion. Tamil homes honour Perumal on Saturdays, especially in the month of Purattasi. Northern homes turn to Hanuman on Tuesdays. Maharashtrian families often give Tuesday to Ganesha. Some Bengali homes keep Thursday for Lakshmi. A family's weekday gods usually tell you where its grandparents came from.

None of these is the correct version. The story is really about rhythm. A week of seven identical days is shapeless; the mind slides through it without noticing. Giving each day a face to turn toward is a way of giving the week a shape. Monday feels different from Friday, not because the gods keep office hours, but because you have agreed to meet them differently.

This is also why the tradition makes room for your own [ishta devata, the chosen deity](/blog/ishta-devata-meaning). You can pray to your own god every day and still let the week's rhythm add colour.

## No god is closed on the wrong day

A small reframe, because people worry about this. No god is closed on the wrong day. You can pray to Lakshmi on a Tuesday and to Shiva on a Friday, and nothing is lost. The weekday pattern is an invitation, not a timetable.

So try this: pick one day this week, find out which deity your family links with it, and light a lamp for that god in the evening. Just one day. Then see, at the end of the week, whether it felt a little less shapeless. My niece once asked why the temple smells of jasmine on Fridays. The answer, it turns out, is Venus.
