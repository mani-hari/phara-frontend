Every autumn someone in the family group chat asks why Diwali has moved again. So, when is Diwali in the USA? It falls on the new-moon night of the lunar month of Karthika, which usually lands in late October or November, and because the lunar day is reckoned for local time, the American evening can fall a day apart from India's. The short answer is that the moon decides. The longer answer is about living by two calendars at once: one on the office wall, and one in the sky.

## When is Diwali in the USA?

The Hindu calendar is lunisolar. Months follow the moon, and years are nudged back into line with the sun by adding an extra month every few years. Diwali is tied to one particular night in that lunar year: Amavasya, the new moon, when the sky is at its darkest.

Which month's new moon depends on how your family counts. Many northern calendars end each month at the full moon, so this new moon falls in the middle of Kartika and is called Kartik Amavasya. Many southern and western calendars end the month at the new moon, so the same night closes the month of Ashvin. The Tamil solar calendar places Deepavali in Aippasi. Three names, one dark night. Nobody is wrong; they are just starting the month at different points on the same circle.

Because a lunar month is about twenty-nine and a half days, twelve of them fall short of a solar year. The festival slides earlier each year until the extra month pulls it back. That is why Diwali drifts between mid-October and mid-November. If you want the whole system explained, including the solar months of the South, there is a longer piece on [how the Hindu festival calendar works in the USA](/blog/hindu-festival-calendar-usa).

## Why can the US date differ from India?

Here the moon becomes a matter of geography. A tithi, the lunar day, is not a calendar square. It is the time the moon takes to move twelve degrees away from the sun, and it begins and ends at one precise moment, the same moment everywhere on earth. What changes is what the clock says in your city when that moment arrives.

Lakshmi puja is traditionally done in the evening hours after sunset while Amavasya prevails. When Amavasya begins in the small hours of an Indian morning, it has already begun by sunset the previous evening in America, so the US Lakshmi puja can fall a calendar day earlier. When it begins later in the Indian day, both countries usually share the same date. So California, New Jersey and Chennai can honestly reach different answers. It is the same new moon, just seen from different places.

The old texts were never troubled by this. The Surya Siddhanta, the classical astronomical treatise, calculates for a given location; the idea of one date for the whole planet is a modern habit, not an ancient one.

## How to find the right evening where you live

Use a panchangam, the traditional Hindu almanac, set to your own city rather than to Delhi or Chennai. Most online panchangams let you choose a location, and they will show when Amavasya begins and ends in your local time. Look for the evening when it prevails after sunset.

Easier still, follow your nearest temple. American temples publish festival calendars calculated for their own region, and if the temple is where you will gather, its date is the one that matters. When the temple calendar and your family in India disagree, it is fine to light a lamp on both evenings. Nobody is keeping score.

## A festival that asks you to look up

It would be convenient if Diwali sat on a fixed square, like Thanksgiving. It would also be poorer. A festival that moves forces you to check the sky, or at least an almanac that checks it for you. You become, for a few weeks each autumn, someone who knows where the moon is.

That is half the point. The new moon is the darkest night of the month, and the whole festival is a decision to light lamps precisely then, when there is least light to borrow. The date moves because the darkness moves. The lamps follow it wherever it goes, including a porch in Ohio.
