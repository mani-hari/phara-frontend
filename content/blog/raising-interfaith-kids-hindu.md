A friend in Seattle, Hindu and married to a Catholic, told me their daughter calls herself Hindu on Saturdays and Christian on Sundays. He was worried about it, until he noticed that she prayed at both with the same seriousness. Raising interfaith children is less about choosing a winner than about choosing what to keep, and keeping it well. A few Hindu threads, held steadily, will stay with a child far longer than a whole tradition half-remembered. (How the marriage itself finds its feet is a different story, told in [interfaith marriage in a Hindu family](/blog/interfaith-marriage-hindu).)

## Raising interfaith children: what is the Hindu thread?

You cannot pass on everything. Nobody can, not even families where both parents grew up in the same temple. So choose a few things and do them properly.

- **The evening lamp.** A small flame at dusk, lit by the child when they are old enough. It takes a minute and it lasts a lifetime. I wrote about [the dusk lamp ritual](/blog/the-dusk-lamp-ritual) because it was the one thing my mother never let slip.
- **A festival or two.** Not all of them. Deepavali, perhaps, and Pongal or Ganesh Chaturthi, done fully, with the food and the stories and the new clothes.
- **Stories.** Ganesha and the broken tusk, Krishna lifting the mountain, Hanuman's leap. Children remember stories long after they forget rules. [Hindu bedtime stories for kids](/blog/hindu-bedtime-stories-for-kids) is a good collection to start with.
- **The star birthday.** In Tamil homes, the janma nakshatra, the birth star, is the real birthday. A child born in America has one too; see [finding the birth star for a US-born baby](/blog/birth-star-for-us-born-baby). A small puja or a temple visit on that day tells them they have a place in a very old calendar.

That is enough. A child who has the lamp, two festivals, a dozen stories and a star has a Hindu thread they can pull on for the rest of their life.

## How do two traditions share one calendar?

With planning, mostly. The collisions are real. Deepavali and Christmas both claim the dark end of the year. Pongal falls in the middle of January, when everyone is back at work. Easter and Tamil New Year sometimes land within days of each other in spring.

The families I know who manage it well agree ahead of time, not in the moment. Sit down with your partner at the start of the year, with both calendars, and decide which festivals each of you most wants to mark and how. Nobody should discover on the morning of Deepavali that the other has booked a ski trip.

Invite both sets of grandparents. My friend in Seattle has his parents join Christmas dinner by video from Coimbatore, and his wife's parents come for the Deepavali lamps. The grandparents have become oddly competitive about who is more included, which he considers a success. There is more on keeping both festivals intact in [Christmas and Diwali in one home](/blog/christmas-and-diwali-at-home).

## When the child asks which one they are

Sooner or later, they will. Usually in the car. Usually when you are late.

Try not to force a choice. You might say: "You are both. You belong to Amma's family and Dad's family, and to both of their ways of praying." Many children find that entirely satisfying. Some will lean one way for a while, then the other, especially as teenagers.

The Hindu tradition has a line for this. The Rig Veda says ekam sat vipra bahudha vadanti: truth is one, the wise call it by many names. A child who hears that early learns that their two traditions need not be at war inside them. They are two names for a love that was there before them.

What the child is really asking is whether they belong. The answer should always be yes.

## One small thing to carry into the week

Sit down with your partner this week, with two sheets of paper. Each of you writes the three things from your own side you most want your child to remember at forty. Not beliefs, but things: a song, a dish, a festival, a prayer before meals. Swap the sheets. Then quietly make sure those six things happen in your house every year.
