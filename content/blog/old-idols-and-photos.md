When we moved apartments, I found a shoebox of framed god pictures, faded, a few with cracked glass, some from Thatha's shop in Kumbakonam. I did not know what to do with them, and I did not want to get it wrong. If you are wondering what to do with old god photos, the gentle answer is this: thank the deity, remove the glass and frame, and return the picture to the earth, by burying it, burning it respectfully, or giving it to a temple that accepts them. Nothing goes in the trash.

## What to do with old god photos: the gentle options

Start with a short prayer. It need not be formal. Hold the picture, thank the deity for the years in your home, and ask permission to let it go. My mother does this in one sentence, in Tamil, and then gets on with it.

Then separate the materials. Take the picture out of its frame. The glass and the frame are just glass and wood, and can be recycled or reused. The paper or cloth image is the part that was worshipped.

For the image itself, families choose one of a few paths:

- Bury it in a garden or a large planter, with a flower on top.
- Burn it respectfully, in a small fire, perhaps with a little camphor, and put the ash in the soil.
- Ask your local temple whether they accept old pictures. Many US temples do, and have a place for them, especially after big festivals. Call first.

Any of these is fine. The one thing tradition asks is that the image not be thrown out with the garbage.

## What about broken idols?

This is where people worry most. Tradition generally discourages worshipping a broken murti, a consecrated image of a deity, at home. A cracked image is seen as no longer fit to hold the attention of worship, much as you would not serve food on a broken plate to a guest. So can you keep a broken idol at home? Many families do keep one wrapped and set aside, out of love, but they stop worshipping it.

For broken idols, the method depends on the material. Clay, paper-mache and other natural idols can be immersed; in an American home that often means a bucket of water, where the clay dissolves over a day or two, and then the water is poured into the garden. The same approach is used for [eco-friendly Ganesh visarjan](/blog/eco-friendly-ganesh-visarjan) after Ganesh Chaturthi. Metal and stone idols do not dissolve; they can go to a temple, or be wrapped in clean cloth and kept.

For old puja items more generally: follow local rules, and never leave them by a road, under a tree in a public park, or in a trash bag. Brass lamps that are still sound can be cleaned and reused, with tamarind and patience, or by the method in [cleaning brass puja items](/blog/cleaning-brass-puja-items).

## Why is it hard to let go?

That shoebox sat in our closet for months. I kept moving it from shelf to shelf.

The pictures were not only gods. They were the people who gave them: a Murugan from my aunt, a Venkateswara a cousin brought back from Tirupati, the Lakshmi and Ganesha picture that had stood on the shelf above the cash box in Thatha's shop for thirty years. Anyone who has read [honest trade in Thatha's shop](/blog/honest-trade-thathas-shop) will know that counter. Letting go of the picture felt like letting go of the room it had hung in.

On a video call, my mother put it simply. The image is a doorway, she said. When a doorway is worn, you can close it with thanks. The deity does not leave with the paper. [Why Hindus worship idols](/blog/why-hindus-worship-idols) says the same thing at more length: the image is a support for attention, not a prison for God.

## One small thing to carry

Keep one or two pictures that still carry the family's attention; the ones you actually look at, the ones someone lights a lamp for. A small altar needs very little, as the guide to [a small puja corner in an apartment](/blog/small-puja-corner-apartment) shows.

Give the rest back with a single sentence of gratitude. That is the whole ritual. It took me months to do it. It took ten minutes once I began.
