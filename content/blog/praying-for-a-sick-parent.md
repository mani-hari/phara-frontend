The phone buzzes at three in the morning in Edison, and it is my brother calling from Chennai. Amma, my mother, has been admitted. The doctors are running tests. There is no flight I can take tonight that will change anything by morning. If you have lived abroad long enough, you know this hour. A prayer for sick parents, from thousands of miles away, often begins right there: in the dark, phone in hand, with nothing to do and an enormous need to do something. What follows is what helped me, and what I have seen help friends, when the helplessness had to go somewhere.

## Why distance makes it harder

The distance is not only miles. It is nine and a half or ten and a half hours of time difference, depending on the season, which means the doctor's rounds happen while you sleep and the good news arrives while you are in a meeting. It is the sibling who stayed, who sleeps on the hospital bench and handles the forms, and whom you are grateful to and a little afraid of disappointing. It is guilt, the old question of whether you should have moved at all.

None of these feelings is wrong. I found it helped to name them plainly, once, to someone. My wife listened while I said, "I feel useless, and I feel guilty for being far, and I am angry that I cannot fix it." Saying it did not fix it. It did make room for the next thing.

## Prayer for a sick parent: what can you do from far away?

Here is what I did, and what many families abroad do.

**A fixed time, every day.** I chose the minute my mother usually lit her evening lamp in India, which fell in my morning. At that minute I lit one too, even at my office desk with a battery tea-light. The regularity mattered more than the words.

**One mantra, chanted simply.** The Mahamrityunjaya mantra to Shiva is the mantra for a sick family member that most families reach for. If you want to know what you are actually saying, this [word-by-word Mahamrityunjaya mantra meaning](/blog/mahamrityunjaya-mantra-meaning) is a good place to start. Eleven times is enough on a busy day.

**A homam in their name.** Families often arrange a Mrityunjaya homam, a fire ritual for health and longevity, or a Dhanvantari homam at a temple in India, with a sankalpam, the spoken statement of intention, in the parent's name and birth star. Some read about [the Dhanvantari homam and healing](/blog/dhanvantari-and-healing) first to choose between them. If you are unsure how to set one up, [booking a puja for your parents in India](/blog/booking-a-puja-for-parents) explains the practical side, and [joining a puja across time zones](/blog/joining-a-puja-across-time-zones) helps if you want to watch live.

**The practical prayer.** Take one task off your sibling's plate. I became the person who spoke to the doctor on a video call every other day and kept the notes. It was the most useful thing I did, and it felt like prayer too.

## A verse to hold

Tirumular, the Tamil saint, wrote in the Tirumantiram that those who neglect the body let the life within it wither, and that he learned to care for the body and so cared for the spirit. I read that line on the hospital's visitor app, of all places, while waiting for a discharge summary.

It reminded me that the body in that hospital bed was being cared for by people who knew what they were doing. My prayer was not a substitute for their work. It stood beside it, the way I wished I could stand beside my mother. Medical decisions belong with the doctors and the family on the ground; prayer is how the rest of us keep vigil.

## One small thing for this week

Set an alarm for the same minute every day. When it rings, light a lamp, or a tea-light if that is what you have. Chant one prayer, or simply say your parent's name. Then send them one short voice note, not about the illness, just your voice saying something ordinary: what you ate, what the weather is doing. Parents in hospital beds play those voice notes more than once.
