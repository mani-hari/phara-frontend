The envelope from Palani sat on my New Jersey doorstep in the snow, and inside was a smell of camphor that took me straight back to the hill. That is what prasadam delivery to the USA really is: a small, sealed piece of the temple arriving at your door, weeks after the puja was performed in your name. Usually it holds shelf-stable things, vibhuti or sacred ash, kumkum, a thread, sometimes a sealed sweet or a jar of panchamritham. It takes longer than a domestic parcel, and food items need to be sealed and declared, but most of it travels well.

## What does prasadam delivery to the USA include?

Prasadam is anything offered to the deity during worship and then returned to devotees as a blessing. At a temple in India, that often means warm pongal or a laddu handed to you in a leaf. Those do not survive a trip across the ocean. What travels is the dry, sturdy part of the offering:

- **Vibhuti**, the sacred ash from Shiva and Murugan temples, folded in paper.
- **Kumkum and turmeric**, the red and yellow powders offered to the Goddess.
- **Sacred threads**, often yellow or red, tied on the wrist.
- **Dry sweets** in sealed packets, and in some cases **panchamritham**, the thick fruit jam of Palani made from banana, jaggery, ghee, honey and cardamom.
- A small picture of the deity, sometimes.

Fresh flowers, cooked food and fruit do not ship. If the idea of prasadam itself is new to you, there is a lovely story behind it in [why prasad is shared](/blog/why-prasadam-is-shared). And if your parcel is from Palani, you might enjoy reading about [the six abodes of Murugan](/blog/six-abodes-of-murugan), of which Palani is the most visited.

## Shipping times and customs

International post from India to the US usually takes one to three weeks after the puja, sometimes more around festivals when everyone is sending something. Customs can add a few days. The first time, I checked the tracking every morning. By the third time, I had learned to let it arrive when it arrives.

On customs: dry items such as ash, kumkum and threads generally pass without trouble. Food items are different. US customs asks that all food be declared, and commercially sealed, clearly labelled packets are what move smoothly. What causes problems is usually undeclared or loosely packed food, not the prasadam itself. A careful sender will label every item and pack it to survive being opened and resealed.

It helps to change how you think about the wait. The puja itself was complete on the day the priest performed it. The prasadam is the tail end of the ritual, not a parcel on a deadline. If you want to understand what happens on the day, [how online puja from the USA works](/blog/online-puja-from-usa-explained) walks through it.

## Storing it with care

Once it arrives, keep it dry, clean and close to your altar. I use a small wooden box that my son decorated; the vibhuti packets go in one side and the threads in the other. Glass jars with lids work well for ash and kumkum, especially in humid summers. Panchamritham should be eaten within a sensible time; check the packet and keep it cool once opened.

Share it out. That is the whole point of prasadam. A pinch of vibhuti to a friend who is anxious about a scan, a spoonful of panchamritham for the neighbour's children, a thread for your brother before his interview. There are practical suggestions for [what to do with prasadam when it arrives](/blog/what-to-do-with-prasadam), including how to return old prasadam to nature rather than the bin.

## One small thing to carry into the week

Tirumular wrote in the Tirumantiram that only the unknowing say love and Shiva are two. I think of that line whenever a parcel arrives, because the prasadam is not really camphor and ash. It is someone's care, a priest's, a parent's, your own, made small enough to post.

So when it arrives, do not open it at the kitchen counter between the bills and the takeout menus. Carry it to your altar, or to any clean quiet place. Wash your hands. Light a lamp if you can. Then open it slowly, and let the smell of the temple fill the room for a moment before you do anything else.
